HOW WOULD YOU DECIDE? 


By GODFREY M. LEBHAR 


jitor-in-Chief 


@ [If you had the responsibility of 
deciding whether a tax-cut would 
be in the public interest, and, if 
so, how much, how soon, in what 
form and on what conditions, what 
would you say? 

Before you reached a final con- 
clusion on all these points, you 
would undoubtedly have to evalu- 
ate all the various factors involved. 
Your problem would be aggravated 
by the fact that even the most 
ardent advocates of a tax-cut dis- 
agree as to the objectives to be 
attained and how to attain them. 


Conflicting views 


To illustrate your difficulty, here 
is a summary of some of the con- 
flicting views currently being ex- 
pressed by top men in government, 
finance, business, labor, the press 
and other areas: 

(1) Asubstantial tax-cut is needed 
now to avert a recession. 

(2) No recession is in sight, so no 
tax-cut is needed. 

(3) Even if a recession were 
otherwise inevitable, the tax-cut 
proposed for this year by President 
Kennedy would be too little and 
too late. 

(4) Even if a recession could be 
ruled out positively, a tax-cut is 
essential because the present tax 


burden has long been a “drag on 
the economy.” 

(5) A minimum tax-cut of $10 
billion is needed to create jobs for 
some of our 4,300,000 unem- 
ployed. By slashing personal in- 
come tax rates, particularly in the 
lower brackets, most of the tax 
savings would go into the immedi- 
ate purchase of consumer goods 
and give our economy the thrust 
it needs to provide more jobs now 
and in the future. 

(6) Cutting personal taxes, par- 
ticularly in the top and middle 
brackets, and reducing corporate 
taxes, would increase investments 
in new projects and the expansion 
of existing business. That is the 
only way to provide and maintain 
employment for our 
growing labor force. 

(7) No tax-cuts should be con- 
sidered while our national budget 
remains unbalanced. 

(8) An unbalanced budget is 
nothing to be complacent about, 
particularly when bigger deficits 
are impending. But the imbalance 
is nothing to get panicky about 
either. In any event, “by increasing 
incentives and reducing the tax 
burden, the prospects for attaining 
sustained prosperity — and thus 
budget surpluses — will be greater 
than by continuing with the cur- 
rent tax structure.” 
(9) No tax-cuts 


maximum 


should be en- 


acted unless accompanied and 
partly offset by tax reforms. 

(10) The greatest defect in our 
present tax system ‘ies in the ex- 
cessive burden it imposes on the 
economy. Therefore, the most 
important tax reform this Congress 
can put through—and the simplest 
—is a substantial reduction of tax 
rates across the board. 

(11) Raising tax rates does not 
necessarily mean more revenue. 
Conversely, reducing tax rates does 
not necessarily mean less revenue. 
Just as lower prices can mean more 
sales and profits, so Jower tax rates 
can mean more government rev- 
enue, 

(12) Our national budget can be 
kept in balance only by a dynamic 
economy. That is impossible as 
long as the tax-bite remains at its 
present destructive level. If re- 
ducing tax rates means a greater 
deficit temporarily, it is neverthe- 
less the only practicable way to 
balance the budget eventually. 


Help Congress decide 


Which of the foregoing points 
make sense to you? Why not make 
them the basis for a letter to Hon. 
Wilbur D. Mills, Chairman, Ways 
and Means Committee, with a 


copy to your own Congressman? 
The decision Congress makes will 
depend largely upon how it inter- 
prets informed public opinion.

CHAINS WIDER DESIGN RANGE 


Manufacturers of pre-engineered steel] build- 
ings don’t use the word “prefab” any more for 
the best of all reasons: they don’t make prefabs 
any more. Prefabs used to be offered in cne or 
(rarely) two basic building types. They offered 
economy and the buildings showed it. 

Today, manufacturers specializing in pre-engi- 
neered systems offer standard, factory-precise pre- 
engineered parts and pieces that can be combined 
in thousands of ways to create a range of build- 
ing shapes geared to any “image” a chain has 
established for itself, or would like to establish. 

With such a pre-engineered system, a chain 
can select a rigid-frame basic structure from 
twelve clear-span widths running from 20 to 120 
feet. The chain can choose from several wall 
heights and load-bearing methods. Any attempt 
to combine “traditional” facing materials with the 
corrugated sidewalls of the old “prefab” was a 
bastard job which only emphasized the build- 
ing’s basic awkwardness. But now, a manufac- 
turer such as Armco offers chains sculptured 
panels which provide a true curtain wall. Facings 
of the building can be finished with masonry, 
glass, or other facade materials. 

A pre-engineered building is not only made 
up of extremely adaptable components, but the 
finished building itself is a component in a flexi- 
ble building system. Almost any imaginable com- 
bination of the basic rigid-frame structures can 
be worked out. This means that a chain need not 
invest in any more space than it needs at a par- 
ticular moment. Later, at its own convenience, 
the chain can add new buildings without having 
to redesign the whole structure. Chains can 
choose from several basic framing types such 
as these rigid-frame structures offered by Armco 
Steel Corp. (above).

NEW TAX CREDIT BENEFITS CHAINS 


™@ The 1962 tax law is designed to 
inject enthusiasm into the nation’s 
businessmen, but there’s been little 
cheering in the chain store in- 
dustry. Most chain store executives 
have been watching and waiting to 
see what equipment will be eligi- 
ble for the investment credit. 
Washington’s hope is that equip- 
ment-buying will soar. 

The more types of equipment 
eligible for credit, of course, the 
happier the taxpayer—but no one 
is making expansion plans on the 
basis of the investment credit 
alone. A 7% investment credit 
seems to be the law’s promise to 
chain store equipment purchasers. 
The fact is that chain stores do 
benefit but the 7% figure is not all 
that it seems. 


Buying based on needs 


At any rate, the decision-makers 
base their buying on_ business 
needs, not on a minor tax credit. 
True, no one has changed his leas- 
ing or buying policy because of the 
credit available, but many have 
agreed that future expansion plans 
may be affected by the investment 
‘credit provisions. 

According to H. G. Althouse, 
treasurer of Peoples Drug Stores, 
Inc., Washington, D. C., the 7% 
credit may affect expansion plans 
for 1964, but not much before that. 

“The credit won't affect our ex- 
pansion timetable for at least an- 
other year,” he said, “but we're 
certainly happy about the law’s ef- 
fect on our liquid cash position.” 

The comptroller of a large cloth- 
ing chain put it this way: “The law 
right now just helps our liquid cash 
position. When your business is 
cash-and-carry this is important. 
Basically we’re merchandisers and 
we believe in putting our money 
into the merchandise we sell.” 

Other national chain executives 
feel that the usefulness of the 
credit provision depends on the 
extent to which state laws follow 
Washington’s lead. (The invest- 
ment credits, of course, are applic- 
able for Federal tax purposes re- 
gardless of the legal classification

Still, some chain store executives

and they're not about to examine 
its teeth. These men are happy 
with whatever percent the benefit

What is investment credit? 


Specifically, the law allows a 
percentage of your investment in 
new and used depreciable property 
to be subtracted from your tax lia- 
bility if the property has a useful

credit provision.) 

Property with a useful life of 
eight or more years is eligible for 
the full 7% investment credit; if 
the useful life is six or seven years, 
the credit allowed is 2/3 of 7%; and 
if the useful life is four or five 
years, the credit is 1/3 of 7%. One 
nice feature of the new law is that 
you are allowed the full tax credit 
against current tax liabilities in 
the year in which you buy equip- 
ment.

Guidelines for investment credit leasing 


The investment credit is available to those who lease equipment in the same 
manner and to the same extent as if the lessee had purchased the equipment, 
except for the limitations and exceptions below: 


> The equipment must be new. 


> The useful life on which the investment credit is based is that set by the 


lessor. 


> The lessee has the option of refusing the investment credit. 

> The fact that the investment credit has been taken must be reflected in 
reduced rent deductions declared by the lessee. 

>» Once the lessee takes the investment credit, he is responsible for all ac- 
counting procedures involved, AND for repaying any part of the credit 
taken but not earned by reason of disposition of the equipment prior to the 
number of years of useful life claimed. 
The basis on which the investment credit is taken depends on the nature of 
the lessor. If the lessor is the manufacturer of the equipment, the basis is 
the fair market price. If the lessor is a leasing company or anyone but the 
manufacturer, the basis is the price that the lessor paid for the equipment.

Push-button stores? 


™ The latest phase of’ the retailing re- 
volution is “robot retailing”, a develop- 
ment that is being heralded in some 
quarters as the final solution to the prob- 
lems of checkout bottlenecks and shopper 
fatigue brought on by the advent of self- 
service. Vending is being touted also as 
a way to cut the cost of store operation. 

The increasing vogue of vending is 
certainly not a development that can be 
ignored. Both foods and non-foods are 
being spewed forth from automatic ven- 
dors at an ever-increasing rate. 

And, as rapidly as it becomes eco- 
nomically feasible, there is little doubt 
that electronics blended with conveyors 
will be able to produce a push-button 
store. The shopper will pick a card or 
activate a recorder, her selections will be 
assembled automatically, and her pur- 
chases will be totalled automatically. She 
might even pay a machine. 

But before we get carried away by a 
vision of a store that is untouched by 
human hands, let’s remember that ma- 
chines don’t fill themselves. Somebody 
has to stand behind or in front of or on 
top of a machine in order to put into it 
the things that automation takes out of it. 
And it’s doubtful that the machines will 
be made so foolproof that they'll never 
need fixing. Chances are that any break- 
downs that do occur will coincide with 
peak sales periods. The most important 
man in the push-button store could turn 
out to be the Store Mechanic. 


™@ The case for the push-button store is 
a persuasive one. It is possible to cite a 
large number. of cases in which robot 
retailing is being attempted—in many 
instances with great success. It’s sup- 
posed to eliminate shopper _ pilferage, 
speed up the buying process, make bet- 
ter use of square footage, eliminate dam- 
age to merchandise and cut down the 
rate of walk-outs during peak hours. 
Maybe so, but here are a few considera- 
tions that may have been overlooked by 
the “vend trend” proponents: 


> Perhaps the customer who pushes a 
shopping cart through a self-service store 
does experience shopper fatigue, but 
before she gets tuckered ‘out she’s been 
exposed to a large amount and variety 
of merchandise. Much as we sympathize 
with the shopper, the fact is that the 
longer she shops the store the more she 
is likely to buy. 

> Multiple-item sales might be some- 
what difficult to achieve via automation. 
It’s easy for a shopper to scoop three of ~ 
something into her cart, but she also 
has the option of selecting only one of 
the item. The machines would have to be 
set something like the machine that gives 
you coftee-black or coffee-black-sugar 
or coffee-sugar-cream or coffee-cream. 
& A large sea of merchandise consists 
of things that have to be tried on for 
size, for fit, for appearance. I wouldn't 
like to have to solve the problem of 


vending shoes, dresses, coats, suits, hats 


or women’s swim suits, much less mink 
coats or even engagement rings. 


® Undoubtedly, technological advances 


will produce palatable vended dinners, 
but I’ve tried them recently and, from 
where I sit, technology has quite a bit 
to go. 

& And then, of course, there are many 
things you just have to touch or squeeze 
or rub before you decide to buy them. 
And how can you kick a tire through a 
vending machine’s glass face? 

It is probable that vending will in- 
crease to the point where push-button 
stores will not be uncommon. But, just as 
self-service and discounting were impor- 
tant trends that nevertheless failed to 
engulf all of retailing, so I believe it 
will be with robot retailing. It will find 
its place in the scheme of things but that 
place will be defined by its own built- 


in limitations.
