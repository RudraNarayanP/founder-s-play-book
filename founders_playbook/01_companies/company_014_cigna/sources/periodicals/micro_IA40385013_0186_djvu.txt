There now exists a division between 
the circuits over the standard of review 
of ERISA' trials. Specifically, when a 
plan administrator denies a claim, is his 
factual determination subject to de novo 
review or the deferential “arbitrary and 
capricious" standard? In 1989, Firestone 

a =” , 489 U.S. 
101, 109 S.Ct. 948, 103 L.Ed. 2d 80 
(1989), (hereinafter Bruch) articulated 
the Supreme Court’s rationale in requiring 
de _ novo review of plan interpretations 
where specific authority is lacking. 
Since then, at least four circuits (The 
Fourth, Sixth, Seventh, and Eighth) have 


applied de novo review to factual 


determinations.

Security Act of 1974, 29 U.S.C. Sects. 
1001-1461.

The Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals 
reasons otherwise and has held, in the 
instant case, that the arbitrary and 
capricious standard of review is 
appropriate, even without specific 
discretionary authority in the plan. 

There is no dispute that petitioner 


prevails in a de novo review. The trial 


court judge reversed his previous judgment 
when he changed the standard of review. 
But the Supreme Court need not 


address the question of de novo review if 


it holds, as Petitioner urges, that the 
Federal Rules of Evidence are applicable 
to ERISA trials or, alternatively, that 
hearsay cannot be admitted to such trials 
without substantial guarantees of 
trustworthiness.

resolving this dispute, the plan

declarant. On review, however, we can

murder suspect’s hearsay statement that, 
"I shot Pierre when he tried to strike

reasonable and impartial decision to 
deny the claim. In the light of the 
entire circumstances surrounding the

the plan administrator’s factual

discretion.

121

judicial review of benefit

Capricious standard. Dennard v. 
Richards Group, Inc., 681 F.2d 306, 313

States, Southeast and Southwest Pension

determination with respect to a claim 
for benefits under an employee welfare 
plan must be sustained as a matter of 
law unless the plaintiff can prove that 
the decision was arbitrary and 
Capricious. In other words, in a case 
involving accidental death benefits, 
plaintiff bears the burden of proving 
that the defendant claim administrator 
acted arbitrarily and capriciously in

122 
The arbitrary and capricious 
standard is the least demanding form of 
judicial review and is the same standard 
which the Administrative Procedure Act 


prescribes for the actions of 


administrative agencies. Pokratz v. 


Jones Dairy Farm, 771 F.2d 206, 209 (7th 
Cir. 1985); see also, 5 U.S.C. Sect. 
706. As the Fifth Circuit explained in 
Dennard, 681 F.2d 306, 313, the 
arbitrary and capricious standard 
derives from trust law and, consistent 
with the trust law underpinnings of 
ERISA, prevents excessive judicial 
intervention in the fiduciary activity 
of ERISA claim administrators. Thus, 
under ERISA the court is to focus its 
inquiry upon whether the denial of the 
Claim was arbitrary or whether it was 
based on “substantial evidence" known to 


the plan fiduciary at the time of 


123 
denying the claim. Berry v. CIBA-Geigy, 
761 F.2d 1003, 1007 (4th Cir. 1985). 
Substantial evidence is "such relevant 
evidence as a reasonable mind might 
accept as adequate to support a 


conclusion." Consolo v. Federal 


Maritime Commission, 383 U.S. 607, 620 


(1966). Before condemning a decision as 
arbitrary and capricious a court "must 
be very confident" that the 
administrator overlooked something 
important or "seriously erred" in 


assessing the evidence. Pokratz, 771 


F.2d 206, 209.

denial of benefits to be based on 
substantial evidence and not arbitrary 


and capricious, we affirm. 


Background 


New Orleans police found the 
body of James Pierre in his car on 
October 26, 1986. He had been shot 


twice. Antoinette Collins, a girlfriend 


139 
of Mr. Pierre, turned herself in to 
police the next day. She confessed to 
shooting Mr. Pierre when he refused to 
stop beating her with his own 
handgun. The police, accepting Miss 
Collins’ story, declined to prosecute 
her. 
Celestine Pierre filed claims to recover 
benefits under an accident insurance 
policy and an accidental injury benefit 
provision of a separate life insurance 
policy. Recovery under both policies is 
precluded if Mr. Pierre was shot and 
killed in self-defense. The claims were 
investigated by Robert Weidele, an 
assistant claims manager for Connecticut 
General. The circumstances of the 
shooting were gleaned from a discussion 
with the detective on the case, the 
death certificate and autopsy report, 


and the written report of a private 


140 
investigator who reviewed police records 
and interviewed Miss Collins. On the 
basis of this information, Connecticut 
General concluded that Mr. Pierre’s 
death was not an accident and denied 
Mrs. Pierre’s claims for recovery of 
benefits. 

Mrs. Pierre did not request a 
reconsideration hearing and initially 
filed her claim in state court. The 
employee welfare benefits plan, through 
which Connecticut General provides 
insurance, is governed by the Employee 
Retirement Income Security Act (ERISA). 
Connecticut General removed the action 
to federal district court. Mrs. 
Pierre’s motion to remand the claim to 
state court was denied. On the basis of 
written trial memoranda and 
stipulations, the district court issued 


an opinion and order on the merits. The 


141 
district court held that hearsay 
statements were properly considered in 
assessing the claim for benefits and 
that denial of the claim was not 


arbitrary and capricious. 


Discussion 


The insurance policies in this 
action are herself in to the police the 
next day. She governed by ERISA, 29 
U.S.C. Sects.1001-1461. Because it 
exercises authority or control in the 
distribution of assets of the benefits 
plan, Connecticut General is a fiduciary 
for purposes of ERISA. 29 U.S.C. Sect. 
1002(21) (A). ERISA imposes duties of 
care, fair dealing, and impartiality on 
a fiduciary. See 29 U.S.C. Sect. 


1104(a) (1). 


142 


Standard of Review 


Denial of benefits by a 
fiduciary may not be disturbed under 
ERISA unless the decision was made 
arbitrarily and capriciously or in bad 
faith, as the decision would be if it 
were unsupported by substantial evidence 
or based on an erroneous conclusion on a 
question of law. Dennard v. Richards 


Group, Inc., 681 F.2d 306, 314 (5th 
Cir.1982) (citing Wardle v. Central 


States, Southeast & Southwest Areas, 


Pension Fund, 627 F.2d 820, 824 (7th 
Cir.1980), cert. denied, 449 U.S. 1112, 


101 S.Ct. 922, 66 L.Ed.2d 841 (1981)). 
The standard of review has elasticity to 
allow reviewing courts to account for 
possibilities for self-interest and 
other biases. This flexibility is 


evident in the different incantations of 


143 
the standard that look for bad faith and 


substantial evidence. See Van Boxel v. 


’ i st, 


836 P.2d 1048, 1049-50 (7th Cir. 1987). 


Mrs. Pierre contends that the 
arbitrary and capricious standard is 
inappropriate in this case because of 
the self-interest Connecticut General 
has in denying benefits. While Mrs. 
Pierre’s concern is not without merit, 
this circuit’s rule follows the weight 
of authority applying the arbitrary and 
capricious standard in ERISA cases. See 
Denton v. First Nat’] Bank of Waco, 765 
F.2d 1295, 1303-04 (5th Cir.1985); Ganze 
v. Dart Industries, Inc., 741 F.2d 790, 
792 (Sth Cir.1984); Vv al 

a s wes eas 


Pension Fund, 602 F.2d 97, 99 (5th Cir. 
1979). The standard applies equally when 


144 
the insurance carrier is the 
decision-making fiduciary. See Reilly 
v. Blue Cross & Blue Shield United of 
Wisconsin, 846 F.2d 416, 419 (7th Cir.), 
cert. denied, -- U.S.--, 109 S.Ct. 145, 
102 L.Ed.2d 117 (1988). The district 
court correctly applied an arbitrary and 


capricious standard of review. 


Admissibility of Hearsay 


Mrs. Pierre next contends 
that even if the arbitrary and 
capricious standard of review applies in 
this case the use of hearsay by 
Connecticut General in its 
determination requires reversal. It is 
difficult to see how a plan 
administrator or fiduciary could base a 
decision on anything but extrajudicial 


statements. Even in formal 


145 

administrative proceedings controlled by 
the Administrative Procedure Act (APA) 
hearsay evidence is permitted. To 
determine whether hearsay evidence may 
constitute substantial evidence in 
support of a decision, however, the 
reviewing court must look to factors 
establishing underlying reliability and 
probative weight. , oward 
County v. Dept. of Health, Education & 
Welfare, 525 F.2d 900, 906 (5th 
Cir.1976) (citing Richardson v. 
Perales, 402 U.S. 389, 402, 91 S.Ct. 
1420, 1428, 28 L.Ed.2d 842 (1971)). 

Although ceterminations under 
ERISA are not controlled by the APA and 
the use of "hearsay" is not explicitly 
addressed in ERISA, the same policies of 
informality and practicality appear apt. 
The intent behind administrative 


procedures is to make them manageable 


146 

and comprehensible by non-lawyers while 
maintaining fairness. Richardson, 402 
U.S. at 400-401, 91 S.Ct. at 1426-1427. 
The same concerns are applicable to 
ERISA procedures under School Bd. and 
Richardson, therefore, second step 
declarant evidence may or may not be 
sufficient to uphold a determination 
under an ERISA plan, but its 
consideration is not improper. See 

Vv. u oss & e Shield 
United of Wisconsin, 846 F.2d at 423-24. 
The district court did not err in 
finding Connecticut General properly 
considered the available evidence. 

The question then becomes 
whether the decision made by Connecticut 
General was arbitrary and capricious in 
light of all the evidence, second hand 
or otherwise, upon which it was based. 


Again, the district court properly 


147 
upheld the decision to deny benefits. 
Connecticut General considered all the 
available evidence. Mrs. Pierre does 
not contend that any additional evidence 
exists. Although the story of 
self-defense given by Antoinette Collins 
is self-serving, it is not incredible. 
Connecticut General based its decision 
to deny benefits under the accident 
policies on its conclusions that Miss 
Collins’ account of the shooting was 
credible, that Miss Collins killed Mr. 
Pierre in self-defense (a version of the 
occurrence apparently accepted by the 
law enforcement authorities), and that 
because Mr. Pierre provoked Miss 
Collins’ retaliation his death was not 
an accident. We agree with the district 
court that the denial of Mrs. Pierre’s 
Claim was not arbitrary and capricious 


or in bad faith or unsupported by 


148 


substantial evidence. 


Reconsideration Hearing 


Mrs. Pierre asks this 
court to remand this case to the 
administrator for consideration if the 
admission of hearsay evidence is upheld. 
Remand is appropriate in those cases 
where proper evidence should have been 
considered by the administrator but was 


not. See Berry v. Ciba-Geigy Corp., 761 


F.2d 1003, 1007 (4th Cir. 1985). Mrs. 


Pierre does not assert that proper 
evidence has not been heard; rather, 

she claims that she has not been allowed 
to argue her claim because she failed to 
take advantage of a reconsideration 
hearing when it was available. 

Moreover, remand is inappropriate if 


reconsideration would be futile. Wolfe 


149 


v. J.C. Penney Co., 710 F.2d 388, 394 


(7th Cir.1983). Reconsideration in this 


case would be futile. In addition, Mrs. 


Pierre’s request for a reconsideration 
hearing before the plan administrator 

appears to be raised for the first tine 
on appeal; she asked the district court 


only to remand the case to state court. 


AFFIRMED.

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


Insurance policies were issued by respondents, Con- 
necticut General Life Insurance Company (‘““CGLIC’’) 
and Life Insurance Company of North America 


(““LINA’’) (collectively, ““CG/LINA’’), to Martin Mar- 
ietta Corporation, employer of petitioner’s husband, 
to fund an employee welfare benefit plan governed 
by ERISA (the “‘Plan’’). Martin Marietta, the Plan 
Administrator, authorized CG/LINA to administer 
claims under the Plan pursuant to its terms. Pet. App. 
at 61, 932 F.2d at 1553. 


In November of 1986, petitioner filed claims as- 
serting that she was entitled to death benefits under 
the ‘‘accidental death’’ provisions of the Plan because 
of the death of her husband. After an investigation, 
the CG/LINA claim administrator concluded that, un- 
der the terms of the policies, claimant was not en- 
titled to death benefits because her husband had been 
the aggressor and by his own acts precipitated his 
death by foreseeable means. Pet. App. at 65, 932 F.2d 
at 1554. Her claim was accordingly denied. 


Respondents’ investigation included consideration of 
information supplied by investigators with the New 
Orleans Police Department, examination of the au- 
topsy report, and a separate private investigation. 
The conclusion that decedent had been shot by his 
mistress during his physical assault on her was sup- 
ported by the police department’s decision not to 
prosecute the assailant, the police reports, police pho- 
tographs of the bruises and lacerations sustained by 
the assailant, and interviews with her. Pet. App. at 
62-64, 932 F.2d at 1554. 


The CG/LINA claim administrator informed the 
petitioner of his decision to deny her claims for ben- 
efits and the basis for his denial. He provided 
petitioner with the opportunity to submit any addi- 
tional or contrary information to support her claim. 


She failed to do so. Pet. App. at 64-65, 932 F.2d at 
1554. 


Petitioner brought this action in the Civil District 
Court for the Parish of Orleans, State of Louisiana, 
alleging that respondents wrongfully denied her ben- 
efits under the Plan. Respondents removed the action 
to the United States District Court for the Eastern 
District of Louisiana on the ground that the District 
Court had jurisdiction under 29 U.S.C. § 1132(e\1). 
At the initial hearing, the matter was submitted to 
the court on stipulated facts. The District Court sus- 
tained the denial of benefits as a matter of law be- 
cause respondents’ determination that Pierre’s death 
was not accidental under the terms of the Plan was 
not arbitrary and capricious. Pet. App. at 135. 


Petitioner appealed the District Court’s decision, 
and the Fifth Circuit affirmed. Pet. App. at 149, 866 
F.2d at 144. On petition for rehearing, the Fifth Cir- 
cuit vacated its opinion and remanded the matter to 
the District Court for reconsideration in light of Fire- 
stone Tire & Rubber Co. v. Bruch, 489 U.S. 101 (1989). 
See Pierre v. Connecticut Gen. Life Ins. Co., 877 F.2d 
345, 346 (5th Cir. 1989). 


On remand, the District Court determined that 
Bruch required a de novo review of the factual find- 
ings that led to the decision to deny benefits because 
the Plan did not contain specific language granting 
discretionary authority with respect to the claim ad- 
ministrator’s conclusions. Pet. App. at 156. The Dis- 
trict Court ruled that the assailant’s statements 
corroborating the police conclusion of self-defense 
were inadmissible as ‘“‘hearsay,’’ and that the re- 
maining non-hearsay evidence was insufficient, stand- 
ing alone, to support the finding that the death was 


not accidental. The District Court, therefore, reversed 
its earlier order and entered judgment for the 
petitioner. Pet. App. at 157-158. 


On appeal, the Fifth Circuit reversed, concluding 
that Bruch did not require a de novo review of factual 
determinations; rather, it held that the appropriate 
standard for review of determinations of fact is abuse 


. of discretion. Based on that standard, the court below 


reviewed the evidence before the claim administrator 
and upheld the denial of benefits. Pet. App. at 60, 
932 F.2d at 1553. 


SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


In Bruch, this Court held that ERISA “actions chal- 
lenging denials of benefits based on plan interpreta- 
tions,’ 489 U.S. at 108, are ‘“‘to be reviewed under 
a de novo standard.” Jd. at 115. The court below 
correctly read Bruch as requiring such an exacting 
standard of review only with respect to “plan term 
interpretations,’ Pet. App. at 83-84, 932 F.2d at 1557, 
and not with respect to determinations of “facts un- 
derlying the claim for benefits.’’ Pet. App. at 83, 932 
F.2d at 1557. In so holding, the court below rejected 
the contention that the cases relied upon by petitioner 
required a contrary result, reasoning that the lan- 
guage from those cases was not on point, was dicta, 
or represented a ‘“‘reflexive’’ application of Bruch 
without a considered analysis of the distinction be- 
tween issues of fact and interpretations of the terms 
of an ERISA plan. Pet. App. at 94-108, 932 F.2d at 
1559-1562. 


Subsequent to the filing of the certiorari petition 
in this case, the United States Court of Appeals for 
the Third Circuit issued its decision in Luby v. Teams- 


ters Health, Welfare & Pension Trust Fund, Nos. 90- 
1900 & 90-1912 (3d Cir. Sept. 20, 1991). That decision 
held, contrary to the decision of the court below in 
Pierre, that the Court in Bruch intended the de novo 
standard to apply to factual determinations. However, 
although a conflict in the circuits does now exist, 
principally between Pierre and Luby, that conflict is 
limited to benefit determinations made under plans 
which do not give claim administrators discretionary 
authority to determine eligibility or construe terms of 
the plans. Once plans have responded to this Court’s 
holding in Bruch, the distinction between interpre- 
tations of plan terms and factual determinations will 
have little significance. In the words of this Court, 
‘“‘a denial of benefits challenged under § 1132(a)1)\B) 
is to be reviewed under a de novo standard unless 
the benefit plan gives the administrator or fiduciary 
discretionary authority to determine eligibility for 
benefits or to construe the terms of the plan.”’ 489 
U.S. at 115 (emphasis added). Thus, Bruch provides 
a roadmap for resolving the issue of the standard of 
review: where the plan expressly grants discretion to 
determine eligibility for benefits or to construe the 
terms of the plan, the de novo standard of review 
will not apply. Concern over the ambit of de novo 
review, therefore, will be confined to that relatively 
small and diminishing category of cases consisting of 
claims that arise pursuant to plans that did not con- 
tain discretionary language prior to Bruch, and have 
not been amended pursuant to the guidance provided 
by this Court in Bruch. 


Moreover, even if the conflict urged by petitioner 
were of sufficient importance to merit this Court’s 
review, the issue involved has been squarely and thor- 


oughly addressed by only two courts of appeal. See 
infra notes 2-3 and accompanying text. Under these 
circumstances, a grant of certiorari now would be 
premature. The lower courts should have a greater 
opportunity to resolve this issue of receding impor- 
tance, before this Court need find it necessary to 
exercise its discretion and expend its resources on 
such a narrow question. Accordingly, the Court should 
deny certiorari as it did at the outset of this Term 
in a similar case. Siska v. The Travelers, 60 U.S.L.W. 
3262 (Oct. 7, 1991), denying cert. to 467 N.W.2d 174 
(Wis. App. 1991). 


The second issue presented for review, whether the 
Federal Rules of Evidence apply to a claim admin- 
istrator’s benefit determinations, is not worthy of 
certiorari under any standard, and the circuits are 
not in conflict. The relevant cases are clear and uni- 
form in rejecting the applicability of formal rules of 
evidence to a claim administrator’s disposition of ben- 
efit claims. Daniel v. Eaton Corp., 839 F.2d 263 (6th 
Cir.), cert. denied, 488 U.S. 826 (1988), cited by the 
petitioner as reflecting a conflict with the decision 
below, stands only for the proposition that the Fed- 
eral Rules of Evidence apply at trial to govern the 
admissibility of documents considered by a claim ad- 
ministrator. Thus, Daniel does not address the sub- 
stantive content of what a claim administrator may 
consider in making benefit determinations. 


The cases cited by petitioner as requiring a sub- 
stantial guarantee of trustworthiness for considera- 
tion by a claim administrator of hearsay evidence are 
not inconsistent with the decision below. The Fifth 
Circuit considered the existence of corroborating evi- 
dence and other indicia of reliability in affirming the 


claim administrator’s consideration of the hearsay evi- 
dence in this case. Accordingly, the second issue pre- 
sented by the petitioner, like the first, does not 
provide a basis for a grant of certiorari in this case.

A. The Issue that Divides Pierre and Luby—Whether

As a result of Luby v. Teamsters Health, Welfare 
& Pension Trust Funds, Nos. 90-1900 & 90-1912 (3d 
Cir. Sept. 20, 1991), there is a division between the 
Third Circuit, which holds that the de novo review 
requirement of Bruch extends to factual determina- 
tions, and the Fifth Circuit, which holds that it does 
not. Pet. App. at 60, 932 F.2d at 1553. However, the 
significance of this conflict is confined to benefit de- 
terminations made under plans that did not contain 
discretionary language before Bruch, or made there- 
after under plan language that has not been amended 
in reponse to Bruch. Regardless of how far the Bruch 
standard of review was meant to extend, it is only 
applicable if the benefit plan fails to give the ‘“‘ad- 
ministrator or fiduciary discretionary authority to de- 
termine eligibility for benefits or to construe the terms 
of the plan.’’ Bruch, 489 U.S. at 115. Bruch makes 


it clear that the standard for review of all benefit 
determinations, whether factual, legal, or a combi- 
nation of the two, may be controlled by amendment 
of the plan language granting the claim administrator 
discretionary authority over benefit claim determi- 
nations. If such discretion is granted, courts must 
apply a deferential, rather than a de novo, standard 
in reviewing all factual and plan term interpretations 
underlying claim determinations. Thus, the issue that 
divides the court below from the Third Circuit in Luby 
applies only to a decreasing body of benefit claims 
governed by plans that antedate or that have not been 
amended in response to Bruch. 


As this Court has observed, it must limit its dis- 
cretionary review to those important legal issues that 
are “beyond the academic or the episodic.” Rice v. 
Sioux City Memorial Park Cemetery, 349 U.S. 70, 74 
(1955). This admonition applies with special force here. 
The primary issue in this case is both episodic—es- 
sentially limited in time to those cases arising from 
determinations made in the absence of plan provisions 
granting discretionary authority—and academic—hav- 
ing no impact on the current ability of plan admin- 
istrators and fiduciaries to ensure the application of 
discretionary review through simple plan amendment. 


In sum, the Court in Bruch has made it clear how 
ERISA plans may be structured to avoid de novo 
review. Once such amendments are implemented, the 
distinction between Luby and Pierre will become in- 
creasingly irrelevant. This is not a situation that war- 
rants the intervention of this Court.!

SUPREME COURT OF THE UNITED STATES 


CELESTINE PIERRE v. CONNECTICUT GENERAL 
LIFE INSURANCE COMPANY ET AL. 


ON PETITION FOR WRIT OF CERTIORARI TO THE UNITED 
STATES COURT OF APPEALS FOR THE FIFTH CIRCUIT 


No. 91-485. Decided November 18, 1991 


The petition for a writ of certiorari is denied. 


JUSTICE WHITE, with whom JUSTICE BLACKMUN joins, 
dissenting. 

This lawsuit stems from a denial of accident insurance 
benefits by the administrator of an Employee Retirement 
Income Security Act (ERISA) plan. See 29 U.S. C. §1132 
(aX1XB). In Firestone Tire & Rubber Co. v. Bruch, 489 
U.S. 101, 115 (1989), we reasoned that “the validity of a 
claim to benefits under an ERISA plan is likely to turn on 
the interpretation of terms in the plan” and therefore held 
that decisions of a plan administrator concerning eligibility 
for benefits ordinarily should be subject to de novo review. 

Since our decision in Bruch, a disagreement has devel- 
oped in the Courts of Appeals concerning the standard of 
review to be applied when a benefits decision turns on the 
facts of the case, rather than the interpretation of the terms 
in the ERISA plan. The Third and Fourth Circuits have 
held that a decision of a plan administrator should be 
reviewed de novo. Luby v. Teamsters Health, Welfare & 
Pension Trust Funds, 944 F. 2d 1176 (CA3 1991); Reinking 
v. Philadelphia American Life Insurance Co., 910 F. 2d 1210 
(CA4 1990). See also Petrilli v. Drechsel, 910 F. 2d 1441 
(CA7 1990) (dicta). However, in the present case, the Fifth 
Circuit ruled that an abuse of discretion standard should be 
applied. Pierre v. Connecticut General Life Insurance Co., 
932 F. 2d 1552 (CA5 1991). 

I would grant certiorari to resolve the conflict in the 
Courts of Appeals on this important issue.
