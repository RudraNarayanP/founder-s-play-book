under 18 U.S.C. Secs. 1962(b), (c) and (d) require that the 
“enterprise’’ be one continuing in illicit and/or illegal ac- 
tivity or is it sufficient if the ‘‘enterprise’’ is a continuing

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


This case comes to this Court on petition for a writ of 
certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Se- 
cond Circuit, which court affirmed a judgement of the 
United States District Court for the Eastern District of 
New York granting defendants’ motion to dismiss plain- 
tiffs’ complaint. Federal subject matter jurisdiction over 
the lawsuit arose under the Racketeer Influenced And 
Corrupt Organizations Act (‘‘RICO’’), 18 U.S.C. secs. 
1961-68, and ancillary claims. 


Plaintiffs, Creative Bath Products, Inc.’ (‘‘Creative 
Bath’’), Mathias Meinzinger (‘‘Meinzinger’’), and Gun- 
ther Bartsch (‘‘Bartsch’’), brought this action against 
defendants, Connecticut General Life Insurance Company 
(‘‘Connecticut General’’) and Robert Ferina (‘‘Ferina’’), 
in response to the defendants’ fraud in the sale of life in- 
Surance policies. Meinzinger and Bartsch are principals in 
Creative Bath, a company involved: with the design, 
manufacture and distribution of bathroom accessories. 
On or about September 10, 1984, Ferina, a licensed in- 
Surance agent employed by and receiving commissions 
from Connecticut General, presented to Meinzinger and 
Bartsch an insurance plan that recommended, among 
other things, that they purchase insurance from Connec- 
ticut General as replacement for their existing insurance. 
Meinzinger and Bartsch then signed applications for 2.2 
million dollars coverage of Connecticut General life in- 
surance which was to replace their existant coverage. 


1. Mathias Meinzinger and Gunther Bartsch are also principals in 
M&M Molding, Inc. located at 141 Rodeo drive, Brentwood, N.Y. 
M&M Molding manufactures injection parts for Creative Bath. While 
M&M is not a party to this action, Petitioners felt it appropriate to 
alert the Court to the above relationship.

The basis for plaintiffs’ allegations of fraud are 
threefold. First, that on numerous occasions Ferina, ac- 
ting on his own behalf and on behalf of Connecticut 
General, falsely stated that the policies purchased could be 
returned at any time without penalty. Second, Ferina, ac- 
ting on his own behalf and in a representative capacity, 
placed a statement (required by State law as a remedial 
rule) in the application for the policies of insurance that no 
policies were being replaced when he knew that the policies 
were in fact being replaced. This deceitful act was in clear 
violation of regulations, customs, practice, and fair and 
honest dealings and of the New York remedial require- 
ment of disclosure of replacement. Finally, Ferina, on his 
own behalf and on behalf of Connecticut General, stated 
that at the end of the first year each policy would have a 
substantial cash surrender value when in fact, because of a 
sizeable surrender charge which Ferina failed to disclose, 
there was no real cash surrender value. 


The purpose of these misrepresentations and deceitful 
acts was to have the plaintiffs bound to their coverage 
and/or to terminate their previous policies and instead ob- 
tain and maintain the policies of Connecticut General so 
as to engender profits and income for Ferina and Connec- 
ticut General. In furtherance of their purpose, defendants 
caused to be placed in the mails of the United States in in- 
terstate commerce letters and documents containing the 
misrepresentations and/or engendered markings through 
their misrepresentation and on numerous occasions used 
and/or caused to be used the wires in interstate commerce 
with the same purposes. The mails and wires were also us- 
ed in regard to policy-type documents and notices to the 
plaintiffs and amongst the defendants and the use of 
telephones and/or telexes and/or telefax in like manner. 
All these uses of the mails and wires were within no more 
than a ten-year period from each other and after the adop- 
tion of the RICO statute.

ARGUMENT IN SUPPORT 
OF THE ISSUANCE OF A WRIT 


The Second Circuit’s recent approach to the ‘‘enter- 
prise’’ requirement of Chapter 96 (‘‘RICO’’) is a novel 
and restrictive one which is in conflict with several circuits’ 
interpretations of that term and contrary to Congress’ in- 
tent that RICO be broadly interpreted as stated in Sedima, 
S.P.R.L. v. Imrex Co., 473 U.S. 479 (1985). Plaintiffs 
seek review of this court so as to correct the Second Cir- 
cuit’s mistaken and restrictive requirement that the 
‘enterprise’ must be continuing in illicit and/or illegal ac- 
tivity directly contrary to this Court’s decisions in U.S. v. 
Turkette, 452 U.S. 576 (1981) and Sedima. 


Establishing the existence of an ‘‘enterprise’’ should 
not be predicated upon a showing that it engages in illicit 
activities or unlawful goals of a continuing nature. The 
definitions section of Rico is void of any such requirement 
and this court and the various circuits have spoken to the 
contrary. Thus the statute states: 


[A]s used in this chapter—‘‘enterprise’’ includes any in- 
dividual, partnership, corporation, association, or other 
legal entity, and any union or group of individuals 
associated in fact although not a legal entity. 

18 U.S.C. sec. 1961(4) 


Congress expressly stated that Rico is to ‘‘be liberally con- 
strued to effectuate its remedial purposes.’’ Pub L. 
91-452, sec. 904(a) 84 Stat. 947. By adopting a stringent 
view of the term ‘‘enterprise’’ the Second Circuit has 
frustrated Congress’ intent to give wide application to 
RICO and has engaged in judicial legislation by the fiat of 
its Opinion. 


This restrictive approach grew out of the Second Cir- 


1] 


cuit’s holding in U.S. v. Weisman, 624 F.2d 1118 (2d Cir. 
1980). In an attempt to define the ‘‘pattern’’ requirement 
of an action brought under section 1962(c) the court grap- 
pled with the defendant’s argument that the word ‘‘pat- 
tern’’ should be construed to require that the predicate 
acts of racketeering be ‘‘connected with each other by 
some common scheme, plan or motive’’ in order to pre- 
vent the application of the statute to isolated and sporadic 
criminal acts. 624 F 2d at 1122. In explaining the pattern 
requirement the court stated: 


[T]he predicate acts constituting a ‘‘pattern of 
racketeering activity’’ must all be done in the conduct of 
the affairs of an ‘‘enterprise.’’ 18 U.S.C. §1962(c). Thus, 
the enterprise itself supplies a significant unifying link bet- 
ween the various predicate acts specified in section 1961(1) 
that may may constitute a ‘‘pattern of racketeering activi- 
ty.”’ 624 F.2d at 1122 referring to United States v. Elliott, 
571 F.2d 880, 844 n. 23 (Sth Cir. 1978). 


This merging of the concepts of ‘‘enterprise’’ and 
“‘pattern’’ gave rise to the Second Circuit’s requirement 
that the ‘‘enterprise’’ has to have been conducted through 
a ‘pattern of racketeering activity’’ distinct from the 
‘“‘enterprise’’ [cf. U.S. v. Janniello, 808 F.2d 184 (2d Cir. 
1986) cert. den. 107 S.Ct. 3229 and Beck v. Manufacturers 
Hanover Trust Co., 820 F.2d 46 (2d Cir. 1987)*]. Such a 
requirement is at odds with this Court’s holding in U.S. v. 
Turkette, 452 U.S. 576, 101 S.Ct. 2524, 69 L.Ed. 2d 246 
)1981). In Turkette this Court held that the term ‘‘enter- 
prise’’ as used in RICO encompasses both legitimate and 
illegitimate enterprises. In so holding this court went to 
great lengths in distinguishing and explaining the ‘‘enter- 
prise’ and ‘‘pattern’’ elements of RICO. 


*Cited in Creative Bath, 837 F.2d 561, $64 (2d Cir. 1988) 


[I]n order to secure a conviction under Rico, the Govern- 
ment must prove both the existence of an ‘‘enterprise’’ and 
the connected ‘‘pattern of racketeering activity.’’ The 
enterprise is an entity . . . The pattern of racketeering ac- 
tivity is, on the other hand, a series of crimina! acts as 
defined by the statute. The former is proved by evidence of 
an ongoing organization, formal or informal, and by 
evidence that the various associates function as a continu- 
ing unit. the latter is proved by evidence of the requisite 
number of acts of racketeering committed by the par- 
ticipants in the enterprise . . . The enterprise is not the pat- 
tern of racketeering activity; if is en entity separate and 
apart from the pattern of activity in which it engaged (em- 
phasis added) 452 U.S. at 583. 


It should be noted that the Court’s discussion of proving 
the existence of an enterprise imposed no requirement that 
plaintiffs or prosecutors establish the existence of an 
‘“‘enterprise’’ whose illicit activities or unlawful goals are 
continuing ones. [contra to Creative Bath Products, Inc. 
v. Connecticut General Life Ins. Co., 837 F.2d 561 (2d 
Cir. 1988); Albany Insurance Co. v. Esses, 831 F.2d 41 (2d 
Cir. 1987);? Beck v. Manufacturers Hanover Trust Co., 
820 F.2d 46 (2d Cir. 1987), cert. den., 98 L Ed 2d 650, 108 
S Ct 698 56 USLW 3459.] Rather, the Court in 7Turkette 
painstakingly sought to keep the statute’s ‘‘enterprise’’ 
and ‘‘pattern’’ requirements mutually exclusive and in- 
dependent. 7urkette, 452 U.S. 576, 583. 


The Court went on to state that, ‘‘as a measure to

BACKGROUND 


Plaintiffs commenced the present action in 1985, 
alleging that they had purchased four life insurance 
policies from Connecticut General through Ferina, a 
licensed insurance agent. As finally amended, the com- 
plaint alleged that, in selling the policies to plaintiffs, 
Ferina made three false representations: (1) he falsely 
assured the plaintiffs that the policies could be returend at 
any time without penalty; (2) he inserted in the application 
for the insurance policies a statement that the new policies 
were not replacing older policies, knowing that statement 
to be false, and allowed the application to be processed 
through the mails and wires; and 3) he told the plaintiffs in 
writing that their policies would have a substantial cash 
surrender value at the end of the first year, when in fact 
the policies would have no real cash surrender value. 
Plaintiffs alleged conclusorily that defendants had also us- 
ed the mails and wires in making fraudulent misrepresen- 
tations to other insureds. Discovery revealed, inter alia, * 
that Meinzinger and Bartsch were partners in Creative

DISCUSSION 


In Sedima, S.P.R.L. v. Imrex Co., 473 U.S. 479, 
495-96 (1985), the Supreme Court explored the contours 
of RICO’s requirement of a pattern of racketeering activi- 
ty. The court stated, inter alia, that the legislative history 
of RICO supported the view that 


two isolated acts of racketeering activity do not constitute 
a pattern. As the Senate Report explained: ‘‘The target of 
[RICO] is thus not sporadic activity. The infiltration of 
legitimate business normally requires more than one 
‘racketeering activity’ and the threat of continuing activity 
to be effective. It is this factor of continuity plus relation- 
ship which combines to produce a pattern.’’ 


473 U.S. at 496n.14 (quoting S. Rep. No. 617, at 158, 91st 
Cong., 2d Sess. (1969)) (emphasis in Sedia). In United 
States v. Ianniello, 808 F.2d 184, 192 (2d Cir. 1986), cert. 
denied, 107 S. Ct. 3230 (1987), this Court viewed as dic- 
tum the Sedima discussion of the number of acts needed to 
constitute a pattern, and we ruled that where there was a 
continuing RICO ‘“‘enterprise,’’ see 18 U.S.C. §1961(4), 
two related predicate acts would suffice to establish a pat- 
tern within the meaning of RICO. 


ee 


8a 


Our cases since Janniello have interpreted the ‘‘con- 
tinuity plus relationship’’ factor emphasized in Sedima, 
and adverted to in Janniello, as requiring that the plaintiff 
establish the existence of an enterprise whose illicit ac- 
tivities or unlawful goals are continuing ones. See, e.g., 
Albany Insurance Co. v. Esses, 831 F.2d 41 (2d Cir. 1987); 
Beck v. Manufacturers Hanover Trust Co., 820 F.2d 46 
(2d Cir. 1987), petition for cert. filed, 56 U.S.L.W. 3322 
(U.S. Oct. 13, 1987) (No. 87-616); see also United States v. 
Benevento, Nos. 87-1173, et al., slip op. 597, at 620 (2d 
Cir. Dec. 18, 1987). Thus, in Beck the court ruled that the 
complaint against Manufacturers Hanover Trust Co. and 
others for allegedly unlawful dealing with certain col- 
laterai securing plaintiffs’ bonds failed adequately to 
allege a RICO enterprise because ‘‘the enterprise alleged 
by plaintiffs had but one straightforward, short-lived 
goal—the sale of the U.S. collateral at a reduced price.’’ 
820 F.2d at 51. Similarly, in A/bany, we affirmed the 
dismissal of a RICO complaint where an insurer claimed 
that the insured defendants had committed mail fraud and 
arson; we found the enterprise requirement unsatistied 
because ‘‘the purpose of the enterprise alleged in Albany’s 
amended complaint had an ‘obvious terminating goal or 
date’—inducing the insurer of its goods to pay a false in- 
surance claim.’’ 831 F.2d at 44 (quoting Jannieilo, 808 
F.2d at 192). 


Because J/annielio establishes that two _ related 
predicate acts suffice to constitute a RICO ‘‘pattern,”’ it 
was probably error in the present case for the district court 
to rule that the pattern requirement could not be satisfied 
by a showing that there were three fraudulent actions dur- 
ing the course of a single scheme. See, e.g., Beck, 820 F.2d 
at 51. Nonetheless, our post-Janniel/lo cases establish that 
the amended complaint was properly dismissable for 


9a 


plaintiffs’ failure to establish ‘‘continuity’’ as that factor 
relates to the RICO enterprise. Because the court properly 
rejected plaintiffs’ contention that their evidence showed 
defendants to have engaged in other fraudulent schemes, 
plaintiffs’ case consisted of the proposition that defen- 
dants had made three fraudulent representations in pursuit 
of a single short-lived goal, j.e., the sale to Creative Bath 
and its affiliated company of the four insurance policies 
on the lives of the two partners. That RICO claim is not 
distinguishable in a meaningful way from those claims 
whose dismissals were upheld in Beck and Albany. Accor- 
dingly, we affirm the judgment of dismissal.

Fed. R. Civ. P. 9(b) mandates that ‘‘[i]n all averments 
of fraud or mistake, the circumstances constituting fraud 
or mistake shall be stated with particularity.’’ Mere con- 
clusory allegations of fraudulent or deceptive behavior are 
insufficient to satisfy Rule 9(b). Decker v. Massey- 
Ferguson, Ltd., 681 F.2d 111, 114-15 (2d Cir. 1982). As 
the Second Circuit noted in Segal v. Gordon, 467 F.2d 602 
(2d Cir. 1972), it is a serious matter to charge a defendant 
with fraud, and a court should not-allow allegations of 
fraud to stand unless a plaintiff is both in a position to and 
willing to put himself on record as to the specific nature of 
the fraud. 467 F.2d at 607 (quoting 1A W. Barron & A. 
Holtzoff, Federel Practices and Procedures §302, at 
215-16 (Wright rev. 1960)). Allegations of fraud sufficient 
to comply with Rule 9(b) must set forth matters such as the 
time, place, and contents of purportedly false representa- 
tions, as well as the identity of persons making such 
representations. Luce v. Edelstein, 802 F.2d 49 (2d Cir. 
1986): The Limited, Inc. v. McCrory Corp., 645 F.Supp. 
1038 (S.D.N.Y. 1986); Robertson v. National Basketball 
Association, 67 F.R.D. 691, 697 (S.D.N.Y. 1975). Addi- 
tionally, when a suit names multiple defendants, the plain- 
tiff has the obligation of detailing precisely what par- 
ticular fraudulent actions each individual defendant 
allegedly performed. Claims sounding in fraud must assert 
the performance (or non-performance) of specific acts by 
specific parties. Tannenbaum v. Walco National Corp., 


18a 


(1983-84) Fed. Sec. L. Rep. (CCH) par. 99,652 (S.D.N.Y. 
1984); Natowitz v. Mehlman, 542 F.Supp. 674, 676 
(S.D.N.Y. 1982). Furthermore, allegations of fraud 
resting on nothing more than ‘‘information and belief’’ 
are generally inadequate under Rule 9(b) and, even when 
such allegations pertain to matters peculiarly within the 
adverse parties’ knowledge, Rule 9(b) requires that the 
allegations be accompanied by a statement of facts upon 
which the asserted ‘‘information and belief’’ is founded. 
Luce, 802 F.2d at 54 n. 1; Segal, 467 F.2d at 608; Tlie 
Limiied, 645 F.Supp. at 1042. 


Plaintiffs’ complaint falls far short of these re- 
quirements of Rule 9(b). The complaint completely fails to 
set forth the time or place of defendan:s’ allegedly 
fraudulent actions and does not in any meaningful manner

as to the source of plaintiffs’ information or the bases of

Failure to comply with Rule 9(b) ordinarily results, 
however, not in Outright dismissal, but in an order that a 
plaintiff amend his complaint so as to conform with the

In fact, dismissal of a complaint for failure to satisfy Rule 
9(b) without granting leave to amend may, under certain 
circumstances, be deemed a reversible abuse of discretion. 
Luce, 802 F.2d at 56-57. The Court will therefore give 
plaintiffs twenty days from the entry of this Order to serve 
and file an amended complaint that attempts more fully to 
comply with Rule 9(b).

REASONS FOR DENYING THE WRIT 
I. This Case Does Not Warrant Supreme Court Review. 


Petitioners’ RICO claim against respondents is prem- 
ised upon alleged acts of mail and wire fraud committed 
during the course of the sale of a single life insurance 
plan. Petitioners argue that the Second Circuit’s defini- 
tion of “pattern of racketeering activity,” as interpreted 
by “petitioners, prevents RICO from being used against 
“legitimate enterprises which are tainted with a short 
lived but broad ‘pattern of racketeering activity’.” Peti- 
tion For Writ of Certiorari at p. 13. The facts proved 
below, however, do not support the broad argument peti- 
tioners foist upon this Court. The District Court spe- 
cifically found that, after extensive discovery, petitioners 
offered no evidence that the respondents were engaged in 


- 6 


acts of fraud in relation to other applicants for insur- 
ance coverage. In addition, the District Court indicated 
that the terms of the insurance policies in question belied 
the allegations that respondents had committed acts of 
fraud in relation to the petitioners. Petitioners’ action 
against respondents was dismissed accordingly. 


It is clear that no matter what asserted test is ap- 
plied, petitioners have not stated a claim under RICO. 
The fraudulent acts alleged by petitioners are not the 
type of fraud which RICO was designed to prevent. Not 
only have petitioners alleged mere “garden-variety” 
fraud, but also fraud that allegedly occurred in an in- 
dustry that is traditionally and heavily regulated by 
state law, here New York, as in virtually every other 
state. 


In addition, petitioners have misconstrued the Second 
Circuit’s decision in this case. In appealing the dismissal 
of their amended complaint to the Second Circuit, peti- 
tioners phrased the question presented for review, and 
premised their argument upon‘, whether the RICO stat- 
ute requires multiple schemes to constitute a pattern of 
racketeering activity. 


The dismissal of the amended complaint was affirmed 
by the Second Circuit on the grounds that petitioners 
failed to establish ‘“‘continuity’ as that factor relates to 
RICO enterprise. The question presented for review in 
the petition for writ for certiorari is phrased in terms of 
whether a RICO claimant must allege an illegal enter- 
prise. The Second Circuit has never required, nor does 
their decision herein hold, that a RICO enterprise be an 
unlawful operation. See United States v. Angelilli, 660

II. The Issues Presented In The Petition Are Already 
Before This Court. 


In essence, the petitioners seek a determination of 
whether alleged fraudulent acts committed by respondents 
during the course of the sale of one insurance plan, with- 
out more, satisfies the “pattern of racketeering activity” 
required to establish a cause of action under RICO. On 
March 21, 1988, this Court granted certiorari to review 
the definition of “pattern of racketeering activity” in an 
Kighth Circuit case, H. J. Inc. v. Northwestern Bell Tele- 
phone Co., 829 F.2d 648 (8th Cir. 1988), (en bane), 
cert. granted, 56 U.S.L.W. 3647 (March 21, 1988). Pe- 
titioner’s reply brief was scheduled to be filed on J uly 7, 
1988, with argument anticipated for the November term. 
In H. J. Inc., supra, the plaintiffs alleged that the defend- 
ant undertook to illegally influence members of the Minn- 
esota Public Utilities Commission (MPUC), the state 
regulatory agency responsible for determining the de-

nor has there ever been, a requirement that a RICO ‘enterprise’ be 
an unlawful operation.”

fendant’s rates. The Eighth Circuit affirmed the lower 
court’s dismissal of the complaint on the grounds 
that it failed to satisfy the continuity require- 
ment of “pattern of racketeering activity,” since 
it alleged no more than fraudulent acts committed 
in furtherance of a single scheme to influence MPUC 
commissioners ....” Jd. at 650. Although H.J. Inc., 
supra, defined “pattern of racketeering activity” in terms 
of “continuity plus relationship,” and the Second Cir- 
cuit, in this case, defined “pattern of racketeering activ- 
ity” in terms of continuity in relation to the RICO en- 
terprise, the issues are so entwined that separate deci- 
sions from this Court are not likely to clarify what con- 
stitutes a RICO “pattern of racketeering activity.” In 
addition, this case, and the cases relied upon in the Sec- 
ond Cireuit’s decision, have been interpreted to hold that 
a single criminal episode or scheme does not charge a 
claim under RICO. See Beauford, supra at p. 110. 
Therefore, since the question of what constitutes a RICO 
“pattern of racketeering activity” is already before this 
Court, petitioners request for a writ of certiorari should 
be denied. 


Ill. The Beauford En Banc Decision Will Provide A Better 
Vehicle For Review. 

Much of petitioners’ argument in support of the issu- 
ance of a writ of certiorari is devoted to the Second Cir- 
cuit’s most recent decision defining RICO’s “pattern of 
racketeering activity” requirement, Beauford, supra. The 
plaintiffs in Beauford alleged that the defendants com- 
mitted various acts of fraud in offering condominiums 
in an apartment building complex for sale pursuant to 
an offering plan. After an extensive discussion of dis- 
trict courts’, courts of appeals’ and the Second Circuit’s 
interpretation of “pattern of racketeering activity,” 
Judge Oakes affirmed the District Court’s dismissal of 
plaintiffs’ complaint on the grounds that a “discreet, 
even if widespread, and a continuing even if finite, 


9 


scheme” to defraud plaintiffs is not sufficient to satisfy 
RICO’s “pattern of racketeering activity” requirement. 
Id. at p. 110. 


Judge Oakes’ lengthy opinion, unlike the Second Cir- 
cuit opinion in this case, interprets the decisions, includ- 
ing this case, regarding the RICO “pattern of racketeer- 
ing activity” requirement that have been rendered by 
the second Circuit since this Court’s decision in Sedima, 
S.P.R.L. v. Imex Co. Ine., 473 U.S. 479 (1985). In af- 
firming the dismissal of plaintiffs’ complaint, Judge Oakes 
requested that the case be “reheard en banc to clarify 
Second Circuit law.” Jd. at p. 110. On June 138, 1988, 
the Second Circuit reheard Beauford, en banc, and United 
States v. Indelicato, No. 87-1085 (2d Cir. 1988), rear- 
gued (June 13, 1988), a criminal RICO ease. As of this 
date, decisions have not been rendered. 


The decision to be rendered by the Second Circuit en 
banc in Beauford will result in a more definitive inter- 
pretation of “pattern of racketeering activity.” This 
Court should not grant a writ of certiorari in this civil 
case, wherein one panel of the Second Circuit merely re- 
lied upon existing precedent, when the entire Second Cir- 
cuit is about to render a decision that is intended to 
clarify and set forth its concept of “pattern of racketeer- 
ing activity” in both civil and criminal cases.* To grant 
a writ of certiorari in a case decided prior to the deci- 
sion in Beauford, will only lead to confusion rather than 
clarification of what constitutes a “pattern of racketeer- 
ing activity.”

petitioners’ request for a writ of certiorari, this case

REASONS FOR GRANTING THE WRIT 


Respondents claim that the writ herein should be 
denied as this case, according to them, does not warrant 
review by this Court and the issues presented in the peti- 
tion are already before this Court. Respondents state that 
we have misconstrued the Second Circuit’s decision in this 
case. They go on to say that our appeal to the Second Cir- 
cuit was based upon the question of whether the RICO 
statute requires multiple schemes to constitute a pattern of 
racketeering activity. In fact, that was a basis of the appeal 
to the Second Circuit. It is a further fact that the Second 
Circuit found that the District Court erred in requiring 
multiple schemes as opposed to the single scheme upon 
which the plaintiffs relied. However, for the first time in 
this action, the Second Circuit adopted the novel theory of 
law as to criminal continuity of the enterprise upon which 
this appeal and this request for certiorari is based. 


Ironically, respondents apparently agree with peti- 
tioner that an enterprise need not be criminal in its con- 
tinual nature. They state: ‘‘The Second Circuit has never 
required nor does their decision herein hold, that a RICO 
enterprise be an unlawful operation.’’ (Cases cited omit- 
ted.) (Respondents’ brief at 6-7) However, a reading of the 
Second Circuit’s opinion in this case clearly shows as 
follows: 


“Our cases since Janiello have interpreted the ‘continuity 
plus relationship’ factor emphasized in Sedima and 
adverted to in Janiello, as requiring that the plaintiff 
establish the existence of an enterprise whose illicit ac- 
tivities or unlawful goals are continuing ones.”’ (Decision 
of the Second Circuit below.) 


Clearly, the Second Circuit is requiring that the enter- 
prise have continuing illicit and unlawful goals. In plain 


5 


English, that means that the enterprise is a continuing 
crirninal enterprise, thus effectively taking RICO out of 
the purview of all those cases where legitimate enterprises 
have been subverted for racketeering activity. Exemplary 
thereof are the cases involving city agencies and other 
governmental agencies which were subverted for 
racketeering purposes. When the persons conducting the 
racketeering were caught, there was no question that the 
racketeering stopped. If the Second Circuit’s opinion were 
to be the law of the land, then one would be hard pressed 
to say that after the racketeers were caught the municipal 
and state agencies involved of necessity had continuing il- 
licit activities and unlawful goals. 


The respondents state that the issues in the petition 
are already before this Court and that a better case to be 
determined by this Court is the Beauford v. Helmsley case 
now sub judice en banc by the Second Circuit and 
previously reported at 843 F.2d 103 (2d Cir. 1988), and 
H.J. Inc. v. Northwestern Bell Telephone Co., 829 F2d 
648 (8th Cir. 1988), cert. granted 56 U.S.L.W. 3647 
(March 21, 1988). While H.J. Inc., supra, apparently has 
as the question therein the number of schemes required to 
constitute a pattern, it does not address the new theory ad- 
vanced by the Second Circuit in this case of a requirement 
that the enterprise continue to be criminal. The Second 
Circuit has denied our motion to be heard jointly with 
Beauford, supra, and this case is, in fact, ready for review 
at this time. This case has, in fact, been adopted as one of 
the pillars of RICO in the Second Circuit and continually 
cited for this new theory advanced by the Second Circuit 
of a requirement of the enterprise required to continue be- 
ing a criminal enterprise. Thus, as recently as June ae 
1988, District Judge Lasker in Cullen v. Paine Webber 
Group, Inc. et al, Docket #87CIV.2048, appearing at 1988 
U.S. Dist. Lexis 5957 __ F.Sup. __ states: ‘‘as regards this 


6 


action that Janiello and its progeny require that the plain- 
tiff establish the existence of an enterprise whose illicit ac- 
tivities or unlawful goals are continuing ones.”’ 


In that case the Court points out that upon the ac- 
complishment of the goal there was no further suggestion 
of criminal activity continuing. If that be the case then all 
the RICO actions cited in our main brief involving state 
and city agencies would have no RICO foundation as there 
is nO suggestion that those agencies had continuing 
criminal activity. 


On June 29, 1988, District Judge Conner of the 
Southern District of New York, in deciding Banque 
Worms S.A. v. Messrs. Worms et Cie, et al, __ F. Supp _ 
(SDNY 1988), addressed the plaintiff in that case to the 
decision of the Circuit Court in this case to determine his 
future pleading in that action. In Bruce v. Thomas A. 
Martin et al., Docket #87CIV7737, appearing at 1988 U.S. 
Dist. Lexis 327 and Federal Securities Law Reported CCH 
P93, 927, District Judge Sweet of the Southern District of 
New York dismissed the RICO claim therein because ‘‘the 
RICO claim cannot stand, however, because the com- 
plaint fails to allege ‘the existence of an enterprise whose 
illicit activities or unlawful goals are continuing ones.’ 
Creative Bath Products Inc. v. Connecticut General Life 
Insurance Co., 837 F.2d 561, 564 (2d Cir. 1988).’’ 


In DeLyon v. Fravola et al, Docket #87CIV0187, ap- 
pearing at 1988 U.S. Dist. Lexis 3579__F.Sup__decided on 
April 28, i988, District Judge Keenan cites this case as the 
leading Circuit Court decision in this area for the proposi- 
tion that ‘‘this Circuit interprets this factor to require ‘the 
existence of an enterprise whose illicit activities or 
unlawful goals are continuing ones.’ Creative Bath Pro- 
ducts Inc. v. Connecticut General Life Insurance Co., 837 
F.2d 561, 564 (2d Cir. 1988).’’ 


The Court then goes on to state therein ‘‘in Creative 
Bath, Albany Ins. and Beck, the Second Circuit affirmed 
dismissals of [the] RICO count where the alleged scheme 
had a single, short-lived goal. Whether one seeks to 
characterize a pleading as containing multiple schemes, or 
a single scheme, a plaintiff must present the situation that 
manifests a threat of continuing criminal activity.’’ 


This cause has become a ‘‘cause celebre’’ as the 
leading RICO case setting forth the law of the Second Cir- 
cuit—at the least that for a RICO case to be sufficient the 
enterprise msut continue as a criminal one. As stated in 
our main brief, such a ruling would obliterate prosecution 
of racketeers for infiltration and operation enterprises by 
racketeering activity. Anytime the racketeering activity is 
arrested, the continuing criminal nature of the enterprise 
ceases and the racketeering activity could no longer be 
prosecuted. 


We submit that this cause requires review by this 
Court as otherwise continuing reliance upon the decision 
below by the Courts throughout the country will obliterate 
the intent of Congress in the enactment of the RICO 
Statute. 


We have no objection to respondents’ suggestion that 
the appeal herein be heard together with the appeal in H. J. 
Inc., supra. As the Circuit has refused re-consideration of 
this case together with its en banc hearing in Beauford, 
supra, we submit that determination of this Court need 
not await that cause. In fact, a determination of the pro- 
per RICO laws to enterprise in this case would be either a 
guiding light to the Second Circuit in its future decision in 
Beauford, supra, or in case of conflict with its future deci- 
sion in Beauford, supra, would result in judicial economy 
by the non-reliance of Courts throughout the tand on such 
decision.
