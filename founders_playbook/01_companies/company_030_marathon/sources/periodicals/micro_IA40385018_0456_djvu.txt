QUESTIONS PRESENTED FOR REVIEW 


1. Whether the Crude Oil Windfall Profit Tax is a tax 
on income or profits or an exeise or severance tax on the 
production of oil. 


2. Whether the United States Court of Appeals for the 
Ninth Cireuit erred in determining the Crude Oil Wind- 
fall Profit Tax to be a tax on ineome or profits* by 
considering only the tax’s descriptive language while 
ignoring the relevant legislative history, failing to ex- 
amine the tax’s intended and actual operation, and hold- 
ing itself bound by a state appellate court’s erroneous 
interpretation of the tax. 


3. Whether the burden of the Crude Oil Windfall 
Profit Tax falls, under the tax-shifting clauses of the oil 
and gas leases in question, upon the operator-lessee 
rather than the owner-lessor as a tax upon or referable to 
any operations or acts of the lessee upon the premises.

When these leases and numerous others containing 
identical language were executed, no federal or state 
taxes on “production, extraction, severance or removal”’ 
existed. On Mareh 1, 1980, however, the Crude Oil Wind- 
fall Profit Tax Act (“Act”), 26 U.S.C. $§ 4986 et seq., 
imposed a tax (“Tax”) on the initial producer of erude oil 
at the time the oil is removed from the premises: “An 
excise tax is hereby imposed on the windfall profit from 
taxable crude oil removed from the premises during each 
taxable period.” Id. § 4986(a). Significantly, imposition 
of the Tax does not depend upon the sale of the oil or the 
realization of any income or profit from its disposition. 
Rather, liability for the Tax attaches upon removal of the 
oil from the premises whether the oil is sold, refined, lost 
or destroyed. Id. §§ 4986, 4988(c). In short, the Tax is not 
in any way based upon, applied to, or measured by income 
or profits. 


The Tax’s ambiguous “windfall profit” label is easily 
clarified by reference to the legislative history, which 
establishes that the tax is not a tax on income or profits, 
Lut a specific type of excise tax, namely a severance tax on

Even in a diversity ease, a federal court is under no

state law based upon a misinterpretation of federal law.

99 (7th Cir.), cert. denied, 445 U.S. 907, 100 S.Ct. 1087, 63 
L.Ed. 2d 235 (1979). In such a ease, the diversity court 
remains free to reexamine the federal law foundation of 
the state court decision. Tipton, 298 U.S. at 151-52, 56 
S.Ct. at 719. Accordingly, Crocker’s miseconstruction of

ISSUE 


The issue in this ease is whether, as between the parties 
only, defendant must bear the entire WPT resulting from 
production from the leased properties or whether it must 
be apportioned between the parties as provided in the 
WPT Act. In urging the former proposition, plaintiff 
contends that the parties have, by agreement, effectively 
shifted incidence of the tax to defendant. Plaintiff relies 
for this purpose on the net royaity clause of each lease 
and on the “tax on operations” clause of the three leases 
which contain it. 


ALLOCATION OF TAXES 


It is axiomatie that parties to a contract may agree as 
to which of them will bear the burden of taxes imposed by 
reason of actions contemplated by the contract. Safe 
Harbor Water Power Corp. v. United States, 303 F.2d 928, 
157 Ct.Cl. 912 (Ct.Cl. 1962). This principle is fully appli- 
cable to oil and gas leases. 47 Cal.Jur.3d § 47. 


It is likewise clear that a contractual tax allocation 
clause wii! be effective as to taxes enacted after formation 
of the agreement if the language of that provision can be 
construed as applying to the particular tax in issue. J. W. 
Perry Company v. City of Norfolk, 220 U.S. 472, 31 S.Ct. 
465, 55 L.Ed. 548 (1911); Terminal Inv. Co. v. Pope Estate 
Co., 122 Cal.App. 281, 10 P.2d 139 (1932). 


A eareful review of the WPT Act itself and the availa- 
ble legislative history behind the statute fails to disclose 
evidence of Congressional intent that the above rules 


A5 


should not govern incidence of the WPT in appropriate 
cases. 


ROYALTY CLAUSE 


Tenneco contends that the net royalty clause of each 
lease compels Marathon to bear the entire WPT. That 
position is untenable. Certainly this clause creates a 
royalty interest which is free of production expenses and 
deductions, but it cannot be construed as embracing an 
excise tax imposed by its terms directly on the royalty 
owner. Although the working interest operator withholds 
and transmits the WPT attributable to the royalty inter- 
est this is not a “deduction” or “expense” in any sense 
intended by the parties executing the lease. The net 
royalty language simply cannot reasonably be interpreted 
to shift incidence of the WPT to the defendant. It is 
significant that each lease has extensive and specific 
treatment of some taxes, but the word “‘tax”’ is not used at 
all in the royalty provisions. 


TAX CLAUSE 


The heart of this case is the question of whether the 
above-quoted tax on operations provision contained in 
three of the subject leases operates to shift all WPT to 
Marathon. Resolution of this question requires close anal- 
ysis of the Act in order to determine its precise nature. 


The Conference Committee Report, which attended 
enactment of the WPT, described the tax as “a temporary 
excise or severance tax.” 838 CCH Stan.Fed.Tax Rep. 
€ 4972.-15. The Act itself describes the tax: 


A6 


An excise tax is hereby imposed on the windfall 
profit from taxable crude oil removed from the prem- 
ises during each taxable period. 


26 U.S.C. § 4986(a). 


The key to operation of the tax is removal, in that 
ealeulation of the tax starts with a “removal price,” which 
is the price for which each barrel of oil is sold or deemed 
sold. The tax structure creatéd by the Act is very com- 
plex, but essentially the tax is equal to each barrel’s 
removal price, less its adjusted base price and any state 
severance tax adjustment, multiplied by the applicable oil 
tier rate. 


The Act imposes the WPT upon the sale and removal of 
oil from the producing premises, recognizing that sales of 
erude are normally made at the wellhead. If oil is removed 
from the producing premises prior to sale, the statute 
provides for a constructive sale upon such removal. The 
same treatment is accorded to refining upon the premises 
as of the day that process commences. 26 U.S.C. 
§ 4988 (c) (3)-(4). The exceptions to this constructive sale 
and removal rule are few and narrowly drawn, and they 
generally involve use of the oil in tertiary recovery or in a 
fraccing process on the premises. Reg. § 51.4996-1(d). 


Marathon’s principal argument on this issue is that the 
tax is not upon actions or operations on the property but 
rather upon an economic interest in oil. This position, 
which is imaginative and most ably presented, is nonethe- 
less at clear odds with the plain language of the tax on 
operations clause and provisions of the WPT Act. As may 
be seen, the WPT levy is a severance tax’ on production, 
imposed at the wellhead as each barrel is removed and 


'The Act’s credit for state severance taxes buttresses this 
characterization. 


A7 


sold, actually or constructively. As such, the tax matches 
perfectly the description in the subject leases of taxes 
intended by the parties to be shifted. The tax clause 
specifically applies to taxes referable to any “operations 
... upon the demised premises,” to “production,” to “sev- 
erance,”’ to “removal” and to “sale.” The conclusion is 
inescapable that the effect of the tax clause is to shift the 
incidence of WPT entirely to the operator. . 


DECISION 


Lease I-MA-27 contains the net royalty clause but not 
the tax on operations language. The only tax provisions of 
that lease refer to property taxes and are clearly inappli- 
cable to the WPT. Accordingly, the court will declare 
that, with respect to production from that lease, Tenneco 
must bear its ratable portion or WPT. With respect to the 
other subject leases the tax clause effectively shifts the 
entire WPT to Marathon.” 


"The court is aware of the contrary decision by the California Court 
of Appeal in Crocker National Bank v. McFarland Energy, Inr., 140 
Cal.App.3d 6, 189 Cal.Rptr. 302 (1983).

In 1965 Joseph M. Thomas conveyed certain property 
to a trust he had created two years before for the benefit 
of his children. The instrument was entitled, “Conveyance 
Of Perpetual, Expense And Deduction Free Royalty.” 
The property in question was a one-sixth interest in oil, 
gas and other hydrocarbon substances produced from 
described real estate in Kern County. 


The conveyance included paragraph 7 requiring grantor 
to pay certain taxes. That paragraph contained the follow- 
ing provision: 


“Grantor shall pay and discharge before delin- 
quency all taxes, assessments and other governmen- 
tal charges upon or referable to any operations or

acts of Grantor or on Grantor’s behalf on Said

therefrom, and which are created by or shall arise

ordinance, regulation or order whatever, including 
(without limiting the generality of the foregoing) 
any charge, assessment, claim or obligation created 
by or arising under the California Public Resources 
Code or any act amendatory thereof or supplementai 
or additional thereto.” 


The conveyance also provided in another paragraph, “It 
is the purpose and intent of this conveyance of royalty 
that it shall (exeept as specifically set forth herein) be

Appellant McFarland Energy, Ine. (herein appellant) 
sueceeded to the interest of joseph M. Thomas in 1974.

In 1980 the U.S. Congress enacted a “Windfall Profit

ISSUE 


Is the Windfall Profit Tax a tax, assessment or charge 
upon or referable to any operations or acts of appellant on 
the land, within the meaning of paragraph 7 of the 
conveyance? 


D4 


DISCUSSION 


In the language of the statute, Windfall Profit Tax is a 
tax “imposed on the windfall profit from taxable crude oil 
removed from the premises.” Thus, as oil is removed and 
sold, a tax is imposed on the revenue realized. The tax is 
to “be paid by the producer of the erude oil.” 26 U.S.C.A. 
§ 4986(b). 


Producer is defined as follows, ‘Except as provided in 
subparagraph (B),"! the term ‘producer’ means the 
holder of the economic interest with respect to erude oil.” 
(26 U.S.C.A. § 4996(a) (1) (A).) 


The tax is ealeulated by referring to the amount for 
which the oil is sold. (26 U.S.C.A. § 4988 (ce) (1).) 


Under the conveyance in question, who is the holder of 
the economic interest with respect to the crude oil? It 
seems to us that respondents hold that interest as to one- 
sixth of the oil and therefore are responsible under the 
- eonveyance for one-sixth of the tax. The conveyance pro- 
vides that what is being conveyed is 16% percent of a!! oil, 
gas and other hydrocarbon substances. Under the convey- 
ance respondents “have the option to receive royalty oil 
either in kind or in money.” The product thus belongs to 
respondents as it is produced by appellant. It would be 
respondents’ free of windfall profit tax only if the tax was 
of the kind required under the conveyance to be paid by 
appellant. (Transport Oil Co. v. Bush (1931) 114 Cal.App. 
152, 160, 1 P.2d 1060.) 


The conveyance under scrutiny imposes certain condi- 
tions which must be met before a tax is required to be 
borne by appellant. The tax must be referable to acts or

operations of appellant. Those acts must occur on the

The Windfall Profit Tax does not meet these require-

Respondents argue that the Windfall Profit Tax is 
really a severance tax. Their theory is that the tax fS 
imposed on the removal of the oil. But that is not what the 
statute says. The reference in 26 U.S.C.A., section 
4986(a) to “oil removed from the premises during each 
taxable period” simply is inserted for purposes of know- 
ing when the tax is imposed. If the oil is removed in this 
taxable year, regardless of the taxable year in which the 
sale is made. 

The statute imposes an excise tax on profit and fur- 
nishes the mechanics by which the profit and the tax are 
to be caleulated. Had Congress desired to impose a tax on 
removal, other language would have been used. Then, no 
reference to profit, windfall or otherwise, would have been 
required. Such a tax would have been imposed based on 
the number of barrels produced. 

There is on dispute that the parties to an oil lease may 
provide any basis they wish for division of tax expenses. 
(47 Cal.Jur.3d, Oil and Gas, § 47.) Here the basis is that 
the tax must be referable to acts or operations of appel- 
lant on the property. The Windfall Profit Tax, as we read 
it, is referable to the revenue generated from sales. As such 
it is not included within paragraph 7 of the conveyance.

The judgment is reversed.

For purposes of this Memorandum, this somewhat sim-

In 1980, Congress enacted the “Windfall Profit Tax on

The plaintiff companies, in computing the net profit to be

Long Beach has moved for dismissal of the five actions

Shell contends that in challenging or threatening to sue

Inasmuch as the controversy involved in this litigation 
presents no substantial federal question arising either 
under the Constitution or a statute, section 1331 does not

III. Principle of “Wise Judicial Administration”. 


The issue of the proper computation of net profits 
under the Contractors’ Agreement, in light of the Wind- 
fall Profit Tax, was first raised by Long Beach. Long 
Beach sought to resolve the matter by negotiation with 
the oil companies. When that failed, the city expressed its 
intention to litigate the issue in state court and promptly 
filed the action that the defendants removed here (Case 
No. 83-7303-WPG). All of the five cil companies, as well 
as Shell California Production Ine. and Pauley Petroleum 
Ine., are named in that action and, as is noted above, it 
seeks damages for alleged underpayment to Long Beach 
of net profits. 


For reasons best known to themselves, the five defen- 
dant oil companies and Shell California Production Ine. 
preferred to be in federal court. They knew that the action 
that Long Beach was about to file could not be removed 
on the ground of diversity. Presumably, they also were 
doubtful of establishing “federal question” jurisdiction 
under section 1331. So they hurriedly prepared and filed 
the individual actions that we now confront, thus raising 
in six separate proceedings, which were assigned to six 
separate judges, the very issue that is embodied in the 
single Long Beach action filed in state court. Subse- 
quently, pursuant to interior policies of this district court, 
five of the judges took the time to give consideration to 


E9 


their respective matters and, as a matter of individual 
discretion, caused them to become my responsibility. A 
sixth action, filed by Shell California Production Ine., has 
been dismissed by the plaintiff. 


I already have expressed the conclusion that this court 
does have diversity jurisdiction over the four diverse oil 
company cases. I also am thoroughly mindful of the 
admonition expressed by Justice Brennan in Colorado 
River Water Conservation District, et al., v. United States, 
424 U.S. 800, 813, 96 S.Ct. 1236, 1244, 47 L.Ed.2d 483 
(1976): 


Abstention from the exercise of federal jurisdiec- 
_tion is the exception, not the rule. “The doctine of 
abstention, under which a District Court may decline 
to exereise or postpone the exercise of its jurisdic- 
tion, is an extraordinary and narrow exception to the 
duty of a District Court to adjudicate a controversy 
_ properly before it. Abdication of the obligation to 
decide cases ean be justified under this doctrine only 
in the exceptional circumstances where the order to 
the parties to repair to the State court would clearly 
serve an important coutervailing interest.” County of 
Allegheny v. Frank Mashuda Co., 360 U.S. 185, 188- 
189, 3 L.Ed.2d 1163, 79 S.Ct. 1060 [1062-1063] 
(1959). 


However, Justice Brennan also stated that there are “... 
circumstances permitting the dismissal of a federal suit 
due to the presence of a concurrent state proceeding for 
reasons of wise judicial administration ....” 424 U.S. at 
818, 96 S.Ct. at 1246. I believe that such circumstances 
are present in these cases, for the following reasons: 


1. As is stated earlier in this Memorandum, the State 
action filed by Long Beach could not properly have been. 
removed to this court, and the oil companies sought to 


E10 


solve that problem by bringing declaratory relief actions 
through which they proposed to litigate the same issue as 
that raised in the State action. Much the same thing 
happened in the remarkably similar case of Shell Oil 
Company v. Frusetta, 290 F.2d 689 (9th Cir.1961). Our 
Court of Appeals affirmed the district judge in having 
dismissed the declaratory judgment action beeause he 
‘“... felt this action was nothing more than an attempt to 
use the Declaratory Judgment Act, 28 U.S.C.A. § 2201 et 
seq., aS a device to remove a nonremovable dispute from 
the state courts, and that granting such declaratory 
judgments ‘would in many respects evade the purpose and 
intent of the Removal Act [28 U.S.C.A. § 1441 et seq.].’ ” 
290 F.2d at 691. The opinion of the Court of Appeals went 
on to state: 


It is not the purpose of the Declaratory Judgment 
Act to allow disputes arising in the course of state 
court litigation to be argued in the federal courts 
instead. “The object of the statute is to afford a new 
form of relief where needed, not to furnish a new 
choice of tribunals or to draw into the federal courts 
the adjudication of causes properly cognizable by 
courts of the states.” 


290 F.2d at 692. 


2. The “race to the courthouse” that was involved here 
is troublesome. Colorado River Water Conservation Dis- 
trict v. United States, 424 U.S. 800, 818, 96 S.Ct. 1236, 
1246, 47 L.Ed.2d 483 (1976), teaches that “In assessing 
the appropriateness of dismissal in the event of an exer- 
cise of concurrent jurisdiction, a federal court may also 
consider such factors as ... the order in which jurisdic- 
tion was obtained by the concurrent forums, ....” Here, 
the oil companies filed their actions in this court a few 
days before Long Beach instituted its state court case. 


Ell 


But the oil companies knew that it was coming and 
hurried to get theirs on file first. I quote once again from 
Shell Oil Company v. Frusetta, 290 F.2d 689, 692 (9th 
Cir.1961): 


The mere fact that Shell succeeded in filing its 

complaint in the federal court three days before 

appellees filed their motions in the state court dges 

not automatically entitle Shell to a federal declara- 

tory judgment hearing. Kerotest Mfg. Co. v C-O-Two 

Co., 1951, 342 U.S. 180, 72 S.Ct. 219, 96 L.Ed. 200. It 

is not the purpose of the Declaratory Judgment Act 

to encourage a race to the courthouse for the purpose 

of transferring litigation to the federal courts from 

- the state courts. The purpose of the Declaratory 

Judgment Act is to afford an added remedy to one 

who is uncertain of hi. rights and who desires an 

early adjudication thereof without having to wait 

until his adversary should decide to bring suit, and 

to act at his peril in the interim. Aetna Casualty & 

Surety Co. v. Quarles, 4 Cir., 1937, 92 F.2d 321; 

i Berlitz School of Languages v. Donnelly & Suess, 

D.C.E.D.Pa.1949, 84 F.Supp. 75. Shell was in no 

such situation. Its adversary had announced in open 

court that it was going to bring these same issues 

before the state court, which it promptly proceeded 
to do. 


The above comments are equally applicable here. The oil 

companies have had and still have ample and timely 
: opportunity to litigate the subject issue in the State 
g action. For purposes of balancing the factors for and 
: against abstention by this court, I am considering the 
Y matter as though the State action had been filed first. 


E12 


3. The comments in the preceding paragraph bring 
this ease reasonably close to a Younger v. Harris situa- 
tion, which the opinion in Colorado River stated was one 
of the cireumstaneces that would justify federal absten- 
tion. 424 U.S. at 816, 96 S.Ct. at 1245. Of course, in 
Younger the state action involved a criminal prosecution. 
However, Juidice v. Vail, 430 U.S. 327, 97 S.Ct. 1211, 51 
L.Ed.2d 376 (1977), held that the principles of Younger 
are applicable to appropriate civil actions. In that case, 
Vail was a judgment debtor in New York. He brought a 
civil rights action in federal court under‘42 U.S.C. § 1983, 
in which he sought damages growing out of his having 
been held in contempt and jailed for having ignored 
subpoenas to appear at supplemental proceedings against 
judgment debtors pursuant to a state statute that he 
urged the federal court to declare unconstitutional. In 
rendering the opinion of the Court, Justice Rehnquist 
observed that Vail had had an opportunity to present his 
federal claims in the state proceedings and that “No more 
is required to invoke Younger abstention.” 430 U.S. at 
337, 97 S.Ct. at 1218. 


Likewise, in the instant matters, the oil companies had 
ample opportunity to present their contentions in the 
state proceedings. Instead, they filed new actions here 
that not oniy sought declaratory relief, but three of them 
(Texaco, Mobil and Union) asxed for injunctions that 
would restrain Long Beach from proceeding in state 
eourt. Although Juidice is distinguishable from this litiga- 
tion in several ways, I think that the quotation from 
Younger, upon which Justice Rehnquist relied, has some 
pertinence here:

As we emphasized in Huffman, the “‘more vital 
consideration’ ” behind the Younger doctine of non- 
intervention lay not in the fact that the state criminal 
process was involved but rather in 


ee 6 


the notion of “comity,” that is, a proper respect 
for state functions, a recognition of the fact that 
the entire country is made up of a Union of sepa- 
rate state governments, and a continuance of the 
belief that the National Government will fare best 
if the States and their institutions are left free to 
perform their separate functions in their separate 
ways.” Huffman [v. Pursue, Lid.], 420 U.S. [592] 
at 601, 43 L.Ed.2d 482, 95 S.Ct. 1200 [at 1206], 
quoting Younger, 401 U.S. at 44, 27 L.Ed.2d 669, 
91 S.Ct. 746 [at 750]. 


430 U.S. at 334, 97 S.Ct. at 1216. 


4. Finally, the opinion in Shell Oil Company v. 
Frusetta, 290 F.2d 689, 691 (9th Cir.1961) stated that “It 
is well established that even though jurisdiction may exist 
under the Declaratory Judgment Act the granting of 
declaratory judgments is at the discretion of the district 
court [citing eases]. Said discretion is to be exercised in 
accordance with sound judicial principles and the pur- 
poses of the Declaratory Judgment Act.” After giving all 
of the consideration to these principles and purposes of 
which I am capable, I exercise such discretion and elect to 
dismiss the action brought by the oil companies. 


For the reasons stated in this Memorandum, and on the 
court’s own motion, the State action will be remanded to

Section 702 of the Administrative Procedure Act con-

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


Contrary to petitioner’s assertions, this is not a case 
which has far reaching implications, is not a case where the 
Ninth Circuit erroneously construed the nature of the 
windfall profit tax, is not a case involving an important 
question of federal law and is not a case involving an 
intercircuit conflict. The holding in the Ninth Circuit rests 
on two very narrow points of law. The first point is one of 
contract interpretation already established as controlling 
state law by the courts of California in Crocker National 
Bank v. McFarland Energy, Inc., 140 Cal.App.3d 6, 189 
Cal.Rptr. 302 (1983), petition for hearing denied, April 27, 


1983 (Appendix D to Petition). The contract (an oil and gas 
lease) in the case at bar requires the lessee to pay all taxes 
“upon or referable” to lessee’s acts or operations on the 
leased premises. The question is whether that language 
requires the lessee to pay only taxes which are on or 
referable to the physical act of severing oil from the ground 
or is it very broad in scope requiring the lessee to pay all 
taxes which would not have come into existence if 
producing activities had not been carried out and the oil had 
been allowed to remain in place in the ground? California 
has adopted the former interpretation and under Erie 
Railroad Company v. Tompkins, 304 U.S. 64 (1938), federal 
courts are bound by that interpretation. 


The second point of law upon which the decision below 
rests is that the windfall profit tax, whatever else it may be, 
is clearly not a tax upon or referable to the doing of any 
physical act. It is, as the statute says and as the Court has 
declared in United States v. Ptasynski, 462 U.S. 74 (1983), 
an excise tax on “‘windfall profit” as that term is defined in 
the Act. 


Taken together, these two narrow points of law compel 
an obvious conclusion. This is a matter of California 
contract law. There is no substantial federal question. The 
contract clause in question does not require respondent 
Marathon to pay petitioner Tenneco’s royalty share of the 
windfall profit tax. 


ARGUMENT 


A Writ of Certiorari Should Not Be Issued

Profit Tax Is Not Such A Tax. The Decision

The decision of the court of appeals was correct and no 
writ of certiorari should issue. The lease in question only 
requires lessee to pay taxes which are upon or are referable 
to the lessee’s physical acts of severing oil and gas. 
Therefore, the lease does not require respondent Marathon, 
the lessee, to pay petitioner Tenneco’s royalty share of the 
windfall profit tax because whatever dispute there may be 
as to a proper description of the windfall profit tax, one 
thing is clear: The windfall profit tax is mot a tax on or 
referable to the doing of any physical act. We will discuss 
this in two steps. First, we will demonstrate that the 
windfall profit tax is not a tax on the doing of any physical 
act. Second, we will demonstrate that under controlling 
California case law, the lease language requiring the lessee 
to pay all taxes “upon or referable to” its acts or operations 
on the lease only requires it to pay taxes on or referable to 
lessee’s physical acts associated with producing oil and does 
not require lessee to pay lessor’s windfall profit tax. 


The Windfall Profit Tax Act at 26 U.S.C. § 4986 quite 
clearly describes the tax. It provides as follows: 


(a) Imposition of tax. —An excise tax is hereby 
imposed on the windfall profit from taxable crude 


oil removed from the premises during each 
taxable period.

crude oil. 


(Emphasis supplied.)

See HR Doc No. 96-107, p 2 (1979). He

demonstrate that the windfall profit tax is imposed on an 
economic interest owner’s “‘windfall profit.” It is certainly

persons who hold economic interests, specifically including 
royalty owners as to their royalty share of the windfall

acts at all. Common sense dictates that if Congress had 
intended the tax to be one levied upon the doing of a 
physical act, Congress would not have required persons 
who do not perform physical acts (such as royalty owners) 
to pay their share of the windfall profii tax.

tax on the physical act of severance, we now turn to the

only requires the lessee to pay taxes upon or referable to its

only requires it to pay taxes on or referable to its physical 
acts of severance. Contrary to Tenneco’s apparent 
argument, the lease does not require the lessee to pay all 
taxes which would not have become due but for the lessee’s 
having conducted operations. As the Ninth Circuit pointed 
out, that would be absurd. Carried to its logical conclusion, 
it would mean that a lessee would pay all of the lessor’s

Webster’s Third New’ International Dictionary 
(Unabridged) defines “referable” as “capable of being 
considered in relation to something else.” ‘‘Referable” 
derives from “refer” which, according to Webster, means 
“to think of, regard, or classify under a subsuming principle 
or with a general group.” Thus, if things are to be 
considered under a general principle, i.e., are “referable” to 
one another, they must share common characteristics. 
Because the windfall profit tax is “upon” windfall profit and 
not “upon” the doing of any physical act, the tax can only 
be “referable” to something which shares common 
characteristics with “windfall profit.” Windfall profit is 
associated with price or money. It shares no common 
characteristics with a physical act.2 Therefore, just as the 
windfall profit tax is not a tax on the doing of an physical 
act, so neither is it a tax which is “referable to” the doing of 
any physical act. 


At this point in time, any argument over what the

California, the court of appeal has ruled on what is meant

Crocker National Bank v. McFarland Energy, Inc. 140 
Cal.App.3d 6, 189 Cal.Rptr. 302 (1983), petition for hearing 
denied, April 27, 1983 (Appendix D to Petition). Because

A Writ Of Certiorari Should Not Be Issued 
Because The Decision Of The Ninth Circuit 
Court Of Appeals Does Not Involve An 
Important Question Of Federal Law Which Has 
Not Been But Should Be Settled By This Court 
And Is Not A Decision In Conflict With The 
Decision Of Another Federal Court Of Appeals 
On The Same Matter. 


Petitioner seeks to present the Ninth Circuit decision in 
the case at bar as involving an important question of federal 
law which has not been but should be settled by this Court 
and as one involving a potential conflict between circuits. 
Neither of these two characterizations is at all accurate and 
the case is simply not the sort of case that should be taken 
by this Court. 


First, as already pointed out in the preceding section of 
this brief, the Supreme Court has spoken on the character 
of the Crude Oil Windfall Profit Tax Act in United States 
v. Ptasynski, 462 U.S. 74 (1983). The decision of the Ninth 
Circuit in the case at bar is perfectly consistent with this 
Court’s characterization of the Crude Oil Windfall Profit 
Tax Act in Ptasynski. There is therefore no important 
unanswered question of law here involved. 


_—_. oe 


Second, there is no conflict between circuits. All but one 
of the cases to which petitioner points as creating a 
potential conflict are cases arising in the United States 
District Court for the Central District of California 
(Petition at 10-12). As petitioner notes, these cases are on 
appeal to the Ninth Circuit. It seems unlikely to respondent 
Marathon that any of those cases will be affected by the 
case at bar because none of those cases involve the same 
lease language as is involved here and because none seem to 
depend in any way on whether the windfall profit tax is a 
tax on the doing of any physical act. Even if these cases 
were to depend in some way on the outcome of the case at 
bar, they certainly present no potential for intercircuit 
conflict because they will be decided by the same court that 
decided this case and presumably will be decided in a way 
that does not create conflict. At most, there could be an 
intracircuit conflict. In that event, there is still no 
compelling reason for the Supreme Court to issue a writ of 
certiorari. See, Davis v. United States, 417 U.S. 333, 340 
(1974). (Solicitor General argued that certiorari should not 
be granted where the conflict was intracircuit. The petition 
for writ of certiorari was denied.) The Ninth Circuit can 
resolve an intracircuit conflict itself in an in banc 
proceeding. 28 U.S.C.§ 46(c); Fed. R. App. P. 35. 


The other case cited by petitioner in support of its claim 
of a “potential” conflict is New Mexico v. Regan, 745 F.2d 
1318 (10th Cir. 1984) (transferred to Ct. Cl.) (A copy of 
this case is reproduced as Appendix G to the Petition.) 
(Petition at 10, 12-15.) Petitioner admits that that case 
involves different issues than those present in this case. 
(Petition at 10.) Indeed, the main thrust of New Mexico v. 
Regan concerns the district court’s lack of jurisdiction over 
money claims in excess of $10,000 against the United 
States. Id. at 1320-22. (Petition Appendix at G6-G10.)

Although the dispute in that case concerns the windfall 
profit tax, the case at bar would not seem to have any effect 
on the outcome because that case does not seem to depend 
in any way on either the lease language in the case at bar or 
a determination that the windfall profit tax is not a tax on 
the physical act of severing. Further, the case at bar does 
not even touch upon the Mineral Lands Leasing Act of 
1920 which is of prime importance to the New Mexico case. 
Even if the New Mexico case were ultimately found to turn 
on some point involved with the case at bar, that still would 
not demonstrate any likelihood of, or even a potential for, a 
conflict between circuits. There is nothing to indicate that 
the New Mexico case is likely to be decided in a way which 
will be inconsistent with the case at bar. 


As petitioner recognizes, this case is the only opinion of a 
federal court of appeals on the issues in question here. 
(Petition at 14.) Now federal and state courts will have a 
decision to which to look for guidance.° Therefore, rather 
than create inevitable conflict, the presence of this case 
actually promotes uniformity. 


‘Petitioner also states (Petition at 14) that Humble Exploration Co. v. 
Amcap Petroleum Associates — 1977, 658 S.W.2d 860, 861 (Tex. Civ. 
App. 1983) is in conflict with the Ninth Circuit decision in the case at 
bar. This is not so. There, the Texas court specifically declined to decide 
whether the windfall profit tax could properly be denominated as a

context of the contract there involved, this decision was not necessary. 
Even if ome were to assume, for sake of argument, that the case at bar, 
which applies California law, and the Humble case were in conflict, it 
would be irrelevant. A conflict over a question controlled by state law is 
not a ground for granting certiorari because such a conflict may be 
corollary to a permissible difference of opinion between state courts. 
Ruhlin v. New York Life Insurance Co., 304 U.S. 202, 206 (1938).

CONCLUSION 


The decision of the court of appeals in the case at bar 
which followed controlling California law in Crocker 
National Bank v. McFarland Energy, Inc. 140 Cal.App.3d 
6, 189 Cal.Rptr. 302 (1983), petition for hearing denied, 
April 27, 1983, is correct. Furthermore, contrary to 
petitioner’s exhortations, the decision in the Ninth Circuit 
Court of Appeals does not involve an unanswered question 
of federal law which should be settled by this Court and is 
not a decision in conflict with the decision of any other 
federal court of appeals. A writ of certiorari should 
therefore not be issued and the petition for writ of certiorari 
should be denied.
