## A. EXECUTIVE STATE SUMMARY

At 31 December 1995 the company was a Washington corporation with about five and a half months of trading behind it. Audited: net sales $511,000 (ship-basis, including outbound shipping and handling), cost of sales $409,000, gross profit $102,000, operating expenses $406,000 (marketing $200,000; product development $171,000; G&A $35,000; advertising $30,000), operating loss $(304,000), cash $996,000, assets $1,084,000, equity $977,000, inventory ~$17,000, gross equipment $81,000, no long-term obligations, 11 employees, international sales $198,000 (S-1, T1, FACT [E-04–E-10, E-32, E-36, E-37, E-87]). All sales moved through one Seattle warehouse. The store advertised "more than one million different titles" at 10–40 percent off list, shipped via UPS or Airborne Express, ran a free email notification service, Eyes & Editors, and solicited reader reviews (press release 1995-10-03, T1 [D-06, E-71–E-74]).

The founder held little a 1995 observer would call strength. He carried the President and Chairman titles — **CEO only from May 1996** [H-25] — 10,200,000 shares bought for $10,000, and a position still carrying a company repurchase right over 612,000 of those shares [E-50]. Card acceptance at a two-then-eleven-person firm existed on his personal guarantee, not the company's standing [H-07–H-09].

Still broken: an availability tail the company conceded — "some titles may not be available at all" [E-41]; no vendor contract guaranteeing merchandise, terms or credit, with one distributor at 59% of purchases on a **1996** basis and the 1995 share undisclosed [E-16, E-17]; transaction systems "not integrated" with accounting, needing "a significant amount of manual effort" [E-22]; interruptions with no redundancy or disaster-recovery plan [E-52]; and a cost structure in which, on the audited ratios, no order size was profitable (DERIVED).

Evidence that existed: the audited filing family; one dated company release; two dated outside press observations (Tallahassee Democrat 1995-10-22; Knight Ridder Nov 1995), both aggregator-sourced, so the chain is incomplete [D-18, D-19; RD-011]. No archived amazon.com page from inside the window exists; the earliest capture is a 1998-12-12 redirect [A-84, D-21].

Unknown: order counts, average order value, the effect of every acquisition channel, 1994–95 headcount, his Stage-1 salary, the identity and terms of non-family investors, the launch day, the first customer, and whether any 1994 planning document existed.

Failure remained entirely plausible. The measurable audience was low thousands of visits a day against 24.1% of U.S. households owning a PC in 1994 (T1 [I-16, I-21]); card-security fear was the registrant's own named barrier [I-30]; a rival direct-book operation reported "we haven't sold many books" (CSM 1996-09-18, T1 [D-52]); a 1995 printed skeptic's case held there was no trustworthy way to move money online (Newsweek 1995-02-26, T2 [I-50]); period press called the firm "either the next light bulb or another Edsel" and noted it "had lost $9 million real dollars to generate sales of $32 million" (LA Times 1997-07-20, T2 [G-31]).

### D.2 Assessment

The test showed a narrow, real thing: a firm of two, then eleven, people could put a searchable licensed book catalog before a few thousand daily visitors and take payment from strangers in all fifty states and 45-plus countries, on a buy-per-order chain, with almost no inventory and almost no advertising money, closing the year with ~$1M cash and no debt. It did not show a business. The audited $511,000 covers ~5.5 months and is a total, not a trend; quarterly disclosure begins at Q1-1996, so every 1995 weekly or monthly curve in the literature is non-primary by construction [E-93]. The demand signals are self-measured — visits, countries and repeat share all originate with the seller — and the only independent 1995 evidence shows a journalist testing a search, not a delivered order. Unit economics were conceded as weak in the company's own filing: marketing at 39% of sales against 20% gross margin means 1995 growth cost more than it earned. Success was unsettled on evidence available to anyone then alive — buyers were a minority of households, payment trust a named industry barrier, a rival direct-book operation reported selling almost nothing, a general-purpose Web mall had already been shut down, and the largest physical chains had announced online programs. The most consequential fact is that this experiment's best-known datum — the identity, date and price of the first sale — cannot be substantiated from any document made while it was happening.
