## U. CONFLICTING EVIDENCE

One record per contradiction, both sides kept, with a BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION and a RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY. Nothing here is reconciled silently.

U.1 Claim: LAUNCH WINDOW. CLAIM A: the filings say Amazon "commenced offering products for sale on its Web site in July 1995" with "no sales" "through July 1995", while its 4 October 1995 release counted only "its first four weeks of operation". CLAIM B: the canon dates the first sale to 16 July 1995, and one buyer claims 3 April 1995. WHY THEY DIFFER: the day-level date is anniversary memory, SEC language is month-level and accounting-driven, and the release may define "operation" as unrestricted public ordering rather than the first transaction — or "four weeks" may be loose PR arithmetic, since a 16 July opening would make it eleven weeks. EVIDENCE WEIGHT: the S-1 and the 1995 release are Tier 1 and mutually compatible with a July-September opening; the July-16 canon is Tier 2/3 and ten years downstream. BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: Amazon could transact in July 1995; the only dated company statement about when outside demand appeared is "first four weeks of operation" as of 4 October 1995; report the launch as "July 1995 per SEC filings, publicly announced 3-4 October 1995" and refuse a day. RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: the day is unrecoverable from Tier-1 sources, and an April-1995 invite-only order or a ~May-1995 soft launch would mean the first transaction predates every official dating. CONFIDENCE: High in the interpretation, High in the residual. [src: D-05; D-01; E-02; A-60; A-62]

CLAIM B: the S-1 records only three named related-party common purchases totalling ≈$295,568, an FY1995 financing inflow of $1.2 million "primarily from the issuance of Common Stock and the exercise of stock options" — audited at exactly $1,272,000 — and NO round total, participant count, valuation or percentage sold. WHY THEY DIFFER: A is memoir and press compressed into a single event, while B is a filing reporting cash received and named insiders with no reason to bundle the rest; the "$250,000" specifically is a 2018 newspaper inference, an inflation template around $245,573 built from LATER share holdings, and "$150,000" is an unreconciled competing figure from a 2013 book relayed by a 2025 essay. EVIDENCE WEIGHT: B for anything numerical; A survives only as an order-of-magnitude corroboration, and the element that survives across independent lineages is the COUNT, 20 to 22, not the AMOUNT. BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: approximately $1.2 million of common-equity cash reached Amazon during calendar 1995, of which about $296,000 traces to three named insiders; parental investment of roughly $245.5K across the two 1995 share purchases, ≈$100,020 plus ≈$145,553, is the best-supported reconstruction of the family figure; and the 22-person, $50,000-each, $5 million-valuation packaging is NOT primary-evidenced and should be tagged memoir. Note also that the Kleiner Perkins $8.0 million arrived in June 1996, a year later and a different investor class, so collapsing the two into "the first $1 million raise" is the most common error in this space. RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: the identity and count of the residual ≈$0.9 million of purchasers, option-exercise proceeds being commingled with issuance proceeds and inseparable. CONFIDENCE: High on totals, Low on structure. [src: E-84; E-85; E-26; E-27; E-76; B-40; B-47; B-110; B-122; G-22; G-24]

U.9 Claim: STAGE-1 SALES MAGNITUDE AND BASIS. CLAIM A: weekly first-run sales of $12,000 and $14,000, with variants "$20,000 per week within two months" and "about $1 million in the first 30 days". CLAIM B: audited FY1995 net sales of $511,000. WHY THEY DIFFER: three ways at once — basis, since "returns" is an order-taken measure while the S-1 recognises revenue on SHIPMENT, leaving late-December orders in 1996; period, the weeks being undated launch weeks rather than a run-rate; and metric, net-of-returns versus gross-of-discount. EVIDENCE WEIGHT: B is audited by Ernst & Young while A is a tertiary local-history page with no cited primary; measured against B, 1995 averaged about $21,000 per week across the whole launch year, so the "$20,000 a week" anecdote describes an ordinary ramp rather than a detonation, and the "$1 million in 30 days" variant is flatly inconsistent with the audited annual figure and is classified folklore. BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: use $511,000 for calendar 1995 and treat the weekly figures as unfalsifiable colour, noting that $12K plus $14K over two weeks is not arithmetically hostile to $511K over some twenty-six weeks — the mismatch is about basis and dating, not magnitude. RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: high, since without an order count or a monthly series no reconciliation is possible, and because quarterly disclosure begins only at Q1 1996 any Stage-1 monthly or weekly curve is by construction non-primary. CONFIDENCE: High on B, Low on A. [src: E-04; E-35; E-77; E-93; A-63; D-12; G-17]

U.10 Claim: PHYSICAL AND HUMAN SCALE AT STAGE END. CLAIM A: "The Company carries minimal inventory", with Bezos in 1999 describing "a basement" and then "a real office with a 400-square-foot warehouse", HistoryLink describing a "200-square-foot warehouse" in SODO, and memoir reporting "four employees". CLAIM B: FY1995 cash flow was reduced by an inventory increase of exactly $17,000, $0 in the 1994 stub, with a further $554,000 build in FY1996; Note 1 sets a lower-of-average-cost-or-market policy; gross equipment was $81,000 with ZERO leasehold improvements; employees numbered 11 at 31 December 1995; and the S-1 discloses a Seattle warehouse through which all sales passed. WHY THEY DIFFER: A is a relative marketing claim, minimal measured against a bookstore's stock, plus two unfenced recollections of a space nobody measured and that was immaterial enough to appear in no filing, while B is absolute accounting. EVIDENCE WEIGHT: A and B draw on the same filing for the inventory point, where B is the harder evidence, and for premises and headcount B is under securities law where A is memory. BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: Amazon was a buy-on-order business that nonetheless carried inventory from its first trading year, but so little that the whole FY1995 position was ≈$17,000, about 3.3 percent of net sales, so the "no inventory" myth is unsupported and so is any suggestion that Stage-1 Amazon was inventory-intensive; stage-1 premises square footage is UNKNOWN, what IS evidenced being $0 of leasehold improvements at 31 December 1995, an independent check that the operation had still not built out occupied space; headcount of 4 to 11 is the right order of magnitude for 1995 and "four" is a rounded recollection. RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: the ≈$17,000 balance is DERIVED from cumulative period movements rather than lifted from a balance-sheet line and Note 1 discloses that "certain prior-year balances have been reclassified"; high uncertainty on the number, low on the conclusion. CONFIDENCE: High on the audited lines, UNKNOWN on square footage. [src: E-87; E-32; E-15; E-44; E-61; E-77; E-92; G-07; G-09]

U.11 Claim: PRIORITY AND THE ADVERTISED POSITION. CLAIM A: Amazon was the pioneer — "Earth's Biggest Bookstore", the motto "If it's in print, it's in stock", and "first mover advantages and momentum" asserted in its own filing, the LA Times calling it "the world's first electronic bookstore". CLAIM B: the company's own lawyers named incumbents — Book Stacks Unlimited, a CUC International subsidiary whose start is given as 1991 by Wired and 1992 by the Seattle Times, other small entrants, AOL and Microsoft as channel aggregators, Simon & Schuster going direct — the NCSA Mosaic "What's New" listing of 2 August 1995 already shows multiple commercial web book sites, contemporaneous press stated Amazon "isn't the first or necessarily the cheapest online book merchant", and B&N sued on 12 May 1997 to block "Earth's biggest bookstore" as false advertising, arguing Amazon is "a 'book broker' and not a 'bookstore'" whose warehouse stocks a few hundred titles. WHY THEY DIFFER: "first" is usually asserted on chronology while Amazon's claim is about breadth of listed selection and brand, and competitors and reporters counted catalogues differently. EVIDENCE WEIGHT: Tier 1 on both sides, including Amazon's own competition disclosure, and the S-1 itself claims only "first mover", not priority. BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: Amazon was not the first web bookstore; its Stage-1 distinctiveness must be argued from operational filings — directory discoverability, tiered discounting, e-mail notification and centralized sourcing over the 350,000-to-400,000 fillable pool — not from priority, and its slogan was under legal attack while the origin narrative was being built. RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: Book Stacks' own start year conflicts between two 1996-97 sources, so even the runner-up position is uncertain. CONFIDENCE: High. [src: E-79; E-80; D-69; D-67; G-12; G-14; G-36]

U.12 Claim: COMPANY-SUPPLIED RATE METRICS ARE NOT COMPARABLE. CLAIM A: 60 percent of orders came from repeat customers, Bezos to the WSJ in May 1996, and sales grew 34 to 35 percent per month sustained for 12 to 18 months, in three company-supplied variants between September 1996 and January 1997. CLAIM B: "repeat customers currently account for over 40% of orders" in the March 1997 S-1 and 44 percent in the January 1997 Seattle Times, while filed 1996 quarters run $875k, $2,230k, $4,173k and $8,468k, roughly 17 to 19 percent per month within 1996, against FY1995 net sales of $511,000 in total. WHY THEY DIFFER: on repeat purchase, shifting definitions, a genuinely falling repeat share as acquisition accelerated, or an early flattering number; on growth, the soundbite may describe ORDERS rather than revenue, or a later window, or be compounded off an undisclosed base, and eighteen months back from January 1997 reaches January 1995, six months before the company says it sold anything. EVIDENCE WEIGHT: all figures are company-supplied and only the S-1 carries filing accountability, the FY1997 10-K later RAISING the repeat figure to "over 58%" and so confirming that the metric moved with its definition, while the S-1 itself states that "Growth rates experienced to date are not sustainable." BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: repeat purchase was real and material and growth was extraordinary, but no level is comparable across 1996 and 1997; cite the filing, log the 60 percent and the "34-35% a month for N months" as founder-supplied claims with undisclosed base, window and definition, and use filed quarters for magnitude. RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: whether "sales" meant orders received or revenue booked is nowhere defined, and no 1995 figure of either kind exists. CONFIDENCE: High on the conflict, Low on any level. [src: D-30; D-25; D-26; J-14; C-66]

U.13 Claim: WHY SEATTLE — AND WHETHER THERE WAS A PLAN AT ALL. CLAIM A: Bezos in 1999, "The two primary factors… I wanted to be near a major book warehouse and near a pool of technical talent. Seattle had both." CLAIM B: Stone in 2013, the driver was sales-tax nexus, Bezos dropping Santa Cruz after learning of Quill Corp. v. North Dakota, 504 U.S. 298 (1992). CLAIM C: the 1997 LA Times, "Sales taxes dictated a small state. California and its 28 million book buyers were out", with Seattle chosen for high-tech workers and for being six hours from Roseburg, OREGON, "a major book-distribution center", and Kaphan's three-factor version independently naming sales tax, distribution proximity and talent, with the shortlist "Seattle or some unspecified location in Nevada". CLAIM D: the same 1997 article states that at the moment of the drive he "had no idea where he was going to live or how he would fund his scheme", the moving van's destination phoned in the next day. WHY THEY DIFFER: A, B and C are retrospective accounts optimising a story for different audiences; C is internally tense because its own distribution factor lands in OREGON, not Seattle; and D is a claim of no plan at all, flatly inconsistent with A's two-factor rationale. EVIDENCE WEIGHT: none is Tier 1 for 1994; the S-1 supplies the only primary corroboration that tax geography was REAL, Amazon collecting no sales or similar tax on shipments into any state other than Washington, but says nothing about why the office was where it was. BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: treat the site decision as multi-causal and unproven in its weighting, the tax point being corroborated as a CONDITION and the talent-and-warehouse point only as a CLAIM, and treat the deliberate-strategist and no-plan-at-all tellings as two founder-authored versions of the same winter rather than as a resolved question. RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: high — no 1994 document in this corpus discusses location, and Nevada appears in no secondary account. CONFIDENCE: Medium on multi-causality, High on the absence of a contemporaneous rationale. [src: E-60; E-58; B-74; B-73; A-29; A-30; G-34]
