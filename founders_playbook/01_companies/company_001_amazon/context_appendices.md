## A. Macro / capital environment

*As knowable across the 1990–1996 macro and rate series (a **market-data knowability window wider than the
stage boundary**, which is 1994 idea formation → 1995-12-31; the 1990–93 entries are baselines, not a dating of
the stage): growth resumed after the 1990–91 recession and inflation held near 3%, but money
roughly doubled in price during the founding year, households were cautious, and a two-person Seattle
internet firm was financed by named individuals, a family trust and a §4(2) placement to 23 purchasers. Two
constraints on that shape are documented — **Subchapter S confined shareholders to eligible individuals and one
class until 1995-03-31** [H-04], and no in-window institutional seed market is retrievable (§J.1) — and whether
the plumbing's absence *caused* the individual-money shape is **not established**; §G bars the counter-claim too
("no investor would touch it" is not evidence either way).*

- Output: real GDP, Q/Q annualized (BEA via FRED; T1, FACT, High) [I-08] — 1993 0.7/2.3/1.9/5.6; 1994
  3.9/5.5/2.4/4.7; 1995 **1.4/1.2**/3.5/2.7; 1996 3.0/6.8/3.6/4.2. The 1995 first-half pause was live while
  the firm was built and started selling [I-09, INFERENCE, Medium].
- Prices: CPI-U annual change (BLS via Minneapolis Fed table; T1, FACT, Medium) [I-07] — 3.0 / 2.6 / 2.8 /
  2.9, 1993–96 (1994 contested, §J.2).
- The Fed funds path (Board of Governors via FRED; T1, FACT, High) [I-01→I-04]: **~3% through 1993**
  (2.96–3.09; Jan 3.02, Dec 2.96) — cheap money in the year the idea formed; tightening opened
  **1994-02-04** (+25 bp to a 3.25 target) and 1994 ran **3.05 → 5.45**, a near-doubling inside the founding
  year; peak **6.05 (Apr 1995)**; 5.29 by Dec 1996. Greenspan's February 1994 "short term rates are
  abnormally low" and the hike sequence come from a 2015 Richmond Fed essay → `RETROSPECTIVE SOURCE` (T1,
  Medium) [I-05, I-06].
- The recession's shadow as felt: Michigan sentiment (T1, FACT, High) [I-10] — **82.8 (1993)**, 92.3, 92.2,
  95.3; lows 77.0 (Jul 1993) and **88.2 (Nov 1995)**, inside the first months of trading. Unemployment
  6.6% (Jan 1994) → 5.6% (early 1995) (Richmond Fed/BLS; `RETROSPECTIVE`) [I-11, Medium].
- Small-business credit was personal. All Stage-1 borrowings ever recorded: **$44,000 of interest-free
  founder notes** ($15,000 Jul 1994, $29,000 Nov 1994), repaid during 1995 → zero debt at 1995-12-31 (S-1/A;
  T1, FACT, High) [H-06; E]. The filing names the binding financial constraint as **supplier payment terms
  and credit limits**, not bank debt [I-49; H-13].
- Angels/VCs: in-window market data for a Seattle internet startup is a documented null — Venture
  Economics/MoneyTree never retrieved, so no "typical 1995 seed check" can be priced [I-15, UNKNOWN; §J.1].
  What is documented is the money that arrived (S-1/A, T1, FACT, High), in-window and straddling items counted
  separately: $10,000 founder subscription,
  10,200,000 shares (1994-07) [H-05]; $60,000 common-equity cash and advances in the 1994 stub [E]; first
  priced outside sale at **$0.1717/share** — 582,528 shares to Miguel A. Bezos (1995-02), 847,716 to the
  Gise Family Trust (1995-07), the founder's parents [H-10]; **$1,272,000** equity cash in CY1995, of which
  $295,568 is named [E]; and
  an §4(2) placement of 3,021,000 shares at ~$0.3333 to **23 purchasers = $1,007,000**, subscriptions
  **1995-12-06 → 1996-05-16 — so most of that program is post-boundary, the filings do not disaggregate it by
  date, and its in-window amount is UNKNOWN** (`stage_1.md` §K) — 1 director, 2 founder-related,
  **20 unaffiliated** [H-11]. Tom A. Alberg, a **Madrona
  Investment Group** principal, took 150,000 of those in December 1995 with option grants and joined the
  board in June 1996 [H-26; D-45]. Two constraints made the **shape of the financing** — not the firm's fate,
  which the record leaves open — near-inevitable: **Subchapter S** election
  until 1995-03-31 confined shareholders to eligible individuals and one class [H-04, FACT + INFERENCE]; the
  first institutional round — **$8,000,014 at $14.05/share, two Kleiner Perkins funds** (the Item 5 aggregate;
  569,396 sh × $14.05 = $8,000,013.80, S-1 (orig.) 1997-03-24) — is June 1996,
  outside Stage 1, and so forms no part of any Stage-1 capital figure [E]. Whether those constraints *caused*
  the individual-money form is **not established** (preamble; §G bars the counter-claim as well). The folk
  "$1.1M from 22 friends and family at $50,000 each, ~$5M post for 20%" has no
  filing support: magnitude corroborated by the audited $1,272,000, structure by nothing [E].

**So what.** The environment was friendly in the ways that matter least and hostile in the way that matters
most: growth resumed, inflation stayed near 3% and unemployment fell through the founding year, but
short-term money roughly doubled during 1994 and stayed expensive through 1995 while sentiment ran soft. That
suited a business with a small capital need and no credit-financed inputs — the first outside money came from
named individuals, a family trust and a §4(2) placement to 23 people, and the filing's own risk language
names supplier terms and credit limits, not bank debt, as binding. It made implausible any plan assuming cheap
borrowed working capital or a consumer rush into a channel a quarter of households could reach; since the
data that would price a 1995 Seattle seed check is a gap, this environment can be described only by its price
(expensive) and by who wrote the checks (individuals). **Mechanism (the only documented one):** Subchapter S
confined holders to eligible individuals and one class until 1995-03-31, and the round on file is a §4(2)
private placement to 23 purchasers — a form individual money fits; that establishes the *shape*, not the cause.
**Alternative the record cannot exclude:** institutional money at this size was never sought, or a round this
small needed no institution; with the Venture Economics/MoneyTree series unretrieved (§J.1) neither reading is
evidenced, and §G bars "no investor would touch it" in the same breath. **Confidence:** High that the money came
from named individuals and that short-term credit was expensive; **not established** that the absent plumbing
produced that shape; **UNKNOWN** whether any institutional 1995 option existed to be missed.

## B. Household technology reality

*As knowable in 1994–1995 (series labelled 1993–95 below are null-spanning labels, not a dating of the stage):
a minority condition — about one household in four owned a PC in 1994, household
internet access was below one in five when comparably measured, the buyer dialed up, and the population that
demonstrably bought was small, technical and skewed.*

- Adoption: U.S. households owning a PC **24.1% in 1994** (NTIA from Census CPS; T1, FACT, High) [I-16] —
  observation contemporaneous, the 1999 page restating it `RETROSPECTIVE SOURCE`. Household internet access
  **18.6% (1997)**, 26.2% (1998/99) [I-17]; **no comparable 1995 value exists**, since earlier surveys
  measured modem/computer connectivity differently and direct-access tracking began only in 1997 [I-18,
  Medium]. The circulating "≈6.8% of households in 1994" is Tier-4 attribution to NTIA's July-1995 report and
  is **not established** [I-20; §J.3]. Skew from the start: **8.1% vs 76.5%** computer ownership, **2.9% vs
  62.0%** internet access across the lowest/highest income-education brackets [I-19].
- Modems and access cost are unquantified in-window — bit rates, ISP prices and 1994–95 penetration failed
  retrieval; the only period statements are qualitative ("advances in the performance and speed of personal
  computers and modems… easier and cheaper access"; "technical difficulties, system downtime or Internet
  brownouts"; S-1; T1, CONTEMPORARY OBSERVATION, Medium) [C-70; C-12]. Online-service subscriber totals
  1993–95: documented null [§J.2].
- Audience, from the seller's own instrument: average daily visits — "visits (not 'hits')" — **~2,200
  (Dec 1995)**, ~50,000 (Dec 1996) (S-1; T1, FACT, High) [I-21; C-38].
- Who bought, per period evidence: Amazon's 1996 top ten put web-design and technical titles beside mass
  fiction, *Creating Killer Web Sites* first (release 1997-01-08; T1, FACT, High) [D-40; J-33]; Los Angeles
  Times 1997-07-20, "the same geeks who'd order 'NT Server & Workstation' would type in their credit card
  numbers for a Dean Koontz novel" (T3, CONTEMPORARY OBSERVATION, High) [F-78]; Christian Science Monitor
  1996-09-18 quoting a publisher's new-media director, "Web users are interested in information gathering.
  The books are slightly more practical and academic" (T1, High) [D-49]; the filed reason for starting with
  books, "the demographic overlap between frequent book buyers and Internet users is high" (FOUNDER CLAIM,
  Medium) [D-77]; the one named early buyer in period press a 60-year-old professor with twelve orders (WSJ
  1996-05-16, Medium) [D-20].
- The **NCSA Mosaic August-1995** archive fixes the launch month from a period document: **3,084 site
  entries** across twelve dated listings (2–30 August 1995) — the curated, recommended Web Amazon opened into
  numbered in the low thousands (T1, CONTEMPORARY OBSERVATION, High) [F-71]. Its commerce listings are
  service-bureau shaped: "Net Trust" (Convergence Inc.), "Become a credit card merchant, accept checks
  electronically… and build an online form to let customers order products" [F-72]; "Sun Microsystems
  Internet Commerce Group," which does **not** establish Amazon's hardware (OS/machine type UNKNOWN) [F-73].
  Bounding null: across all 3,084 entries, **no card-security or payments-crypto vendor at all** — no
  VeriSign, CyberCash, First Virtual, NetMarket, SET, DigiCash (INFERENCE, Medium) [F-74]. Mosaic: a free WWW
  client on three platforms, commercial technology licensed to Spyglass [C-03]. **Scope limit:** the archive is
  evidence about the environment, not about the company — the only "Amazon" in the August-1995 "What's New"
  listing is the river, so nothing in it may be read as an Amazon listing or reference [COR-08].
- Friction: dial up; arrive via directory/search; read a deliberately low-graphics page; **type card and
  shipping details into a form**, with off-line doors kept open for the buyers the filing itself calls
  "reluctant to enter their credit card numbers through the Web site" — a company-stated purpose for a channel,
  not a measured effect, and the coda below grades its causal force **Low** — "either by
  e-mail or by telephone," "a toll-free line for customers who are reluctant to enter their credit card
  numbers through the Web site," nine published mailboxes (S-1; T1, FACT, High) [F-53; D-53; F-79]; a Knight
  Ridder wire of Nov 1995 describes card online, toll-free phone or fax (primary not retrieved; chain of
  custody incomplete) [D-18, Medium]; the book was bought after the order arrived, "a week or longer" [D-16],
  at **$3 per order plus $0.95 per book**, which reviewers noted "can cancel out savings" [D-51]; searches
  took minutes and the network answered "Too many connections, try again later" (Newsweek 1995-02-26; T2,
  Medium) [I-60].

**So what.** With a quarter of households owning a PC in 1994 and household internet below one in five when
first comparably measured, the 1995 addressable market was a thin, income- and education-skewed slice — making
a low-overhead, selection-heavy, mail-fulfilled model rational, since the seller needed no foot traffic where
its customers were, while making volume bets unreasonable against a site measured at ~2,200 visits a day.
**The transaction was fragile on the evidence available:** card details by e-mail or telephone, a toll-free line
kept open *specifically* for buyers unwilling to type a number, nine published mailboxes (§E). **The
registrant's own risk language names secure transmission as the category barrier** — company-issued, and here
counted as the filing's assessment of its market, not as independent confirmation of a condition we know only
from it. **Mechanism:** off-line order doors and a deliberately low-graphics page are what a dial-up minority
could actually use, so their presence is explained by access as well as by distrust. **Alternative the record
cannot exclude:** the phone line and mailboxes were ordinary order-capture and service channels rather than
fear-management, and the low-graphics page was modem prudence rather than an answer to card anxiety — no
document says which, and no 1995 refusal or chargeback rate exists (§J.5). **Confidence:** High that the
channels and the page constraint existed; Low that consumer distrust produced them; the barrier itself remains
the company's claim. The demonstrable buyer (technical, practical, book-buying) is the only basis for the
launch assortment that a period document actually states — the filed reason for starting with books is that
"the demographic overlap between frequent book buyers and Internet users is high" [D-77, FOUNDER CLAIM,
Medium] — and a low-graphics page plus a published telephone number are **consistent with** designing for such
an audience, which is not the same as evidencing why they were chosen: **no in-window document states a design
rationale for the page or for the phone line, and whether either converted anything is not knowable inside the
boundary** (§H). That buyer profile is in any case assembled from **1996–97** items (the 1996 top-ten list, the
May-1996 WSJ professor, the Sept-1996 publisher's remark), so it dates the channel's audience and not the 1995
experiment, and is used here as environment rather than as Stage-1 validation.

## C. The mail-order and catalog precedent

*As knowable in 1994–1995: the catalog was the recognized American mechanism for buying an untouchable thing
by post from a printed listing, and Amazon's launch language claimed that identity rather than claiming to be
an internet company.*

- The company's own words, in-window: 4 Oct 1995 — a live site at `http://www.amazon.com/` "offering more
  than one million different titles," customers ordering online with books "delivered directly to their doors
  via UPS or Airborne Express," discounts "10-40 percent from the list price," motto "If it's in print, it's
  in stock" (T1, FACT, High) [J-35; J-37; J-41]. 14 Jun 1996 — "a catalogue of more than 1 million titles and
  easy online ordering," with "search and browse features, email services, Web-based credit card payment and
  direct shipping" [J-39]. 9 Jan 1997 — "offers a catalog of 1.1 million titles" [I-41] and "more than six
  times the size of the largest conventional bookstore" [I-42]: sized against the incumbent format, not
  against the internet (FOUNDER CLAIM contemporaneous, High).
- The filed economic version: shelf space "precludes even the largest physical bookstore from economically
  stocking more than a small minority of available titles," against print's "continuous printing and mailing
  costs" (S-1; T1, FACT-as-argued, High) [I-46; C-34]. Bezos's "larger than 50 New York City phone books"
  line exists only in an interview **conducted 1999, published 2000** → `RETROSPECTIVE SOURCE`, never 1994
  talk [F-54, Medium].
- Conditioning: B&N's FY1997 10-K — "the Company is the world's largest supplier of books through
  direct-mail catalogs," and "each store will special order any book from the more than 1.2 million books in
  print" (T1, FACT (self-described) / ESTIMATE, Medium) [C-33; C-21]. The behavior Amazon asked for was
  already national; only the interface was new. Period press read it that way, skeptically: "We're promised
  instant catalog shopping — just point and click for great deals" (Stoll, Newsweek 1995-02-26; T2, High)
  [I-61].
- Borrowed, evidenced: search over a licensed bibliographic file in place of a printed listing [F-18]; direct
  parcel-carrier shipment and off-list discounting [J-37]; no stock before sale [D-16; C-24];
  telephone/fax ordering as catalog firms ran [D-18; D-53]. **Not evidenced:** any in-window measure of
  catalogs mailed, catalog dollar sales or the return norm. The only trade profile retrieved — Gale SIC 5961,
  2000–2003 — claims "$211 billion by 1990," ">9.9 percent per year" to 1996, ">3 percent of retail sales":
  basis unstated, level implausible, T3, `RETROSPECTIVE SOURCE`, Low [I-43; §J.5]. DMA and *Catalog Age*
  yearbooks paywalled/non-text; a Dec 1994 NYT mailing-list article would not fetch [I-44; §J.8].

**So what.** The mail-order analogy is sourced from the firm's own mouth rather than folklore: in 1995–97
Amazon publicly described itself as a catalog — "1 million," then "1.1 million" titles, "six times the size of
the largest conventional bookstore" — with direct shipping, arguing against bookstores on shelf-space
economics, not technology. The launch language adopted the catalogue form ("a catalogue of more than 1 million
titles"), which is **consistent with** an audience already accustomed to buying sight-unseen by post — B&N
self-described as the world's largest direct-mail book supplier. **Mechanism:** that pre-existing buying habit,
and it is the whole of what this section can name. **Whether it made the pitch legible, cheap to explain, or cheap to buy into is not
evidenced:** no in-window measure of catalogs mailed, catalog dollar sales or the return norm was retrieved, and
1995 acquisition cost is UNKNOWN (`stage_1.md` §D.1, §M.9). **Alternatives the record does not exclude:** the
pitch was legible because it was a discount catalogue in ordinary retail language, or because the 1995 press
cycle needed a novelty regardless of the analogy used. **Confidence:** High that the catalogue wording was
chosen and by whom it was aimed was describable; **not evidenced** that the analogy lowered explanation or
acquisition cost. What no in-window source permits is quantifying the precedent — catalogs mailed, dollar sales and
return norms all failed retrieval and the one figure found is basis-less — so it supports a claim about
customer habits and self-presentation, not that catalog economics transferred to a two-person web bookstore.

## D. The US book trade, 1994–1995

*As knowable in 1994–1995: a fragmented trade whose size was disputed at the source, consolidating into
superstores that could nonetheless stock only a small minority of titles in print, running on generous returns
and off-list discounting, and funnelled through two distributors whose electronic ordering made order-by-order
fulfilment possible.*

- **Market size, contested at the source.** Amazon's filing cites Euromonitor for **US book sales ~$26
  billion in 1996** (worldwide ~$82bn) — T1 as a disclosure, third-party ESTIMATE, `RETROSPECTIVE SOURCE` for
  1994–95, Medium as cited / Low as fact [I-24; C-18]. A "~$11 billion" narrower basis appears in the
  project's cache **but was never retrieved**, so this is one cited figure plus an untraced counter-claim,
  not a two-sided dispute [§J.1]. Both filers worked in shares, not dollars: "the two largest U.S. retailers…
  less than 25% of total U.S. book sales" [C-32, ESTIMATE, Medium]; B&N's superstore share "approximately 9%"
  for the 53 weeks ended 1997-02-01 [C-31, FACT (company estimate), Medium].
- **Retail structure** (figures at §I): B&N's own 10-K gives **431 superstores**, 577 mall stores, 24,000
  employees at 1997-02-01, **60,000–175,000 titles** per B&N store, mall chains 15,000–25,000, and
  "special order any book from the more than 1.2 million books in print" (T1, FACT, High) [C-30; C-27; C-29;
  C-21]; Amazon's S-1 estimates an average superstore at **~130,000 titles**, i.e. **under 10%** of the
  "estimated 1.5 million English-language books believed to be in print," with **~50,000 publishers** per
  Bowker *Books In Print* [C-26; C-28; C-20; C-19]. **1994–95 superstore counts are UNKNOWN** (B&N's earliest
  EDGAR 10-K is FY1997), so the above overstates 1994 incumbency [I-27; §J.4]. Independents are characterized,
  not counted: "thousands of independent bookstores… typically carry a more limited selection of titles in a
  small selling space and have recently come under intense competitive pressure from the superstore format"
  [C-43, CONTEMPORARY OBSERVATION, Medium; count UNKNOWN C-42]. The 10-K also records channel conflict inside
  the incumbent: mall-store sales declining, "attributable primarily to superstore competition" [C-37,
  Medium].
- **Discounting and returns.** "Publishers typically offer generous rights of return… effectively bear the
  risk of their customers' demand forecasting which encourages overordering. As a result, returns in the book
  industry are high" — described, never quantified; the ~25–30% AAP figure was not retrieved and is not
  asserted (S-1; T1, FACT (qualitative), High) [C-35; §J.6]. Amazon's counter-claim: it "believes that its
  returns of books to publishers and wholesalers are significantly below industry norms" [C-36, INFERENCE,
  Medium]. Online price practice by Nov 1997 ran "30 to 40 percent off list for hardcovers and 20 to 30 for
  paperbacks" — the band superstores already ran, so price alone did not distinguish an entrant (*Library
  Systems Newsletter* 17(11); T2, Medium-High) [C-47]. **Sequence correction:** Amazon's 40%-off-featured
  titles policy is dated **March 1997**, after Stage 1 [I-47, FACT, High]; the launch posture was an
  advertised 10–40% band plus selection and convenience [J-37; I-58; D-79].
- **The distributors the offer ran on — non-exclusively.** "The Company purchases a substantial majority of its products
  from two major vendors, Ingram Book Group… and Baker & Taylor," "Ingram the single largest supplier… 59% of
  the Company's inventory purchases in 1996," with **no long-term contract guaranteeing availability, payment
  terms or credit limits** [C-25; I-26; H-13, FACT, High]. Distributors "carry up to 350,000 of the
  best-selling titles," and "electronically ordered books often are shipped by the distributor within hours
  of receipt of an order from Amazon.com" [C-23; C-24, High/Medium]. The filed qualifier: of "more than
  2.5 million titles offered," only "**up to 400,000 are currently supplied by book distributors**" [C-22,
  High] — the long tail was a *listing* asset, not a *fillable* one; a serviceable tail ~3× a superstore, not
  20× [C-67, INFERENCE, Medium-High]. Bezos's 1999 account of the wholesalers' **ten-book minimum**, enforced
  on quantity *ordered* not *received*, is `RETROSPECTIVE SOURCE` founder claim, corroborated once [F-46,
  Medium]. The alternative this heading must not hide: **the same access was on sale to everyone** — Ingram is
  separately disclosed "help[ing] others establish and operate online sites for booksellers" (`stage_1.md`
  §G.1, §M.7; → U.40) — so distributor access explains the category's emergence, not this entrant's share of
  it.

**So what.** The trade's dollar size could not be settled from any single authority — a cited ~$26bn against
an untraced ~$11bn, 1.2m against 1.5m books in print — and the 1994–95 superstore, discount and returns data
needed to describe the trade's internal debate is unretrieved. What is contemporaneously documented is the
structural argument the startup ran on: 1.2–1.5m titles in print against a superstore stocking under 10%, two
distributors whose electronic ordering shipped "within hours," generous return rights that made inventory the
incumbents' problem, and both national chains signaling online programs. **Mechanism** behind "ran on":
electronic order transmission — "electronically ordered books often are shipped by the distributor within hours
of receipt of an order from Amazon.com" — is what let a listing larger than any shelf be filled without owning
the tail. **Alternative the record cannot exclude, now stated in the heading:** the access was non-exclusive —
the same distributors are disclosed arming other entrants — so it explains why a category formed rather than why this entrant kept share;
no advantage claim follows from access alone. **Confidence:** High that the routing is as disclosed; **UNKNOWN**
that it conferred any edge; 1995 distributor share UNKNOWN (59% is FY1996). Stage-1 reasonableness must
therefore be judged on assortment, findability and inventory risk — not on discounting, dated March 1997, and
not on a market-size figure the period's own sources could not agree on.

## E. Payments, trust and fraud infrastructure

*As knowable in 1994–1995: card acceptance was obtainable but underwritten against the founder's personal
credit; no standard existed for moving a card number over a public network and its fix was not promised before
the end of 1996; and chargeback rules, fraud loss and the status of SSL cannot be established from any period
document.*

- **What a two-person Washington startup could obtain** — the strongest period fact here, and it inverts the
  later cliché. "From November 1994 to December 1996, Mr. Bezos personally guaranteed the obligations of the
  Company under a merchant account with **Seafirst Bank**. Since July 1995, Mr. Bezos has personally
  guaranteed the obligations of the Company under a bankcard merchant account with **Wells Fargo Bank**";
  "Since April 1995… personally guaranteed company credit cards. The Company intends to secure releases of
  all of Mr. Bezos' guarantees as soon as possible following the closing of this offering" (S-1/S-1/A
  "Certain Transactions"; T1, FACT, High; corroborated across dossiers C, E, F, H) [H-07→H-09; C-56; F-22].
  Best-dated refutation of "no one could take cards online then": the rails existed and were opened in the
  founding months, and on this firm's own record the binding condition was **credit and account approval**,
  taken personally, rather than the absence of technology. Guarantee amounts,
  merchant discount rates and reserves are **not disclosed** (UNKNOWN) [H-07; E].
- **The rules are unknown, by documented absence.** No in-window primary source for **card-not-present**
  rules, chargeback liability allocation, or merchant-account underwriting of micro-merchants — the networks'
