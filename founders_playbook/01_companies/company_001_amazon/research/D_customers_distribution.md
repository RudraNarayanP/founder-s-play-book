## Narrative notes

**On the "first sale".** Five versions are in circulation and they do not merge. Amazon's own earliest statements (S-1 1997; PRs of June 1996 and June 1999) say only "July 1995", never a day; its single dated 1995 document, the 3-4 October 1995 release, counts "its first four weeks of operation", which on the company's own arithmetic puts counted trading in early September 1995, and contains no first-customer anecdote at all. That absence is itself a finding: in the only 1995 text where Bezos speaks publicly, he talks about selection, not about the first order. The customer-originated version (Wainwright via MarketWatch 2015, Atlantic 2012, Yahoo Creators 2025) is dated 3 April 1995 — before any company statement of a first sale — and all three retellings descend from one interview cycle, so they are one source, not three. The 2005 anniversary version supplies the day and calls the object "a science textbook"; the 2025 state-history version supplies first-week dollar figures with no 1995-97 trace. Ranked by weight: (1) SEC filings and 1995-97 company releases (Tier 1, month only); (2) 1996-97 newspaper accounts of volume and buyer mix (Tier 1); (3) MarketWatch/Atlantic (Tier 2, identity of an early buyer); (4) Seattle Times 2005 and HistoryLink 2025 (day and dollars). Records D-01 to D-12 carry that ranking.

**On the pre-launch period.** What can be held: incorporation 5 July 1994; "commenced offering products for sale" July 1995; FY1995 net sales $511k; ~2,200 average daily visits in December 1995; and a customer's claim of an April 1995 invitation-only order. Everything else about the beta — the Bellevue house, the order bell, the friends-and-family reviews, the 200-square-foot warehouse — rests on 2025 secondary retelling. A defensible reconstruction: private invitation-only transacting capability existed by spring 1995 at latest; a restricted public presence ran from July 1995; the company's own announcement of itself to the world was 3-4 October 1995 and it spoke of that as effectively the opening. The firewall consequence: no 1995 document reports a demand event at all, so the earliest trustworthy demand texture is 1996.

**On where the first customers came from.** The Stage-1 record is small and coherent: findability inside a directory-and-search ecosystem with a few hundred thousand users; editorial nomination by Netscape and Yahoo; discussion in chat groups by people whose reading was technical enough to make them early Web users; invited friends of employees; and borrowed credibility from print media. Paid acquisition is nearly absent in 1995 — $200,000 of marketing-and-sales expense for the whole year, against $6.09m in 1996 — so the familiar "growth hack" reading is inverted by the filing: the marketing money arrives after the demand. Amazon was also not the first online bookstore: contemporaries name Book Stacks (425,000-450,000 titles) and Britain's Internet Book Shop (912,000-915,000) alongside it, and the LA Times said in December 1996 that Amazon "isn't the first or necessarily the cheapest online book merchant".

**On trust.** What existed in 1995-96 was low-tech and mostly human: a phone number in every release, a toll-free line maintained precisely for people who would not type a card number, ordering by telephone or e-mail, nine published mailboxes, minute-level e-mail confirmations, a shipping notification, and a founder whose personal signature stood behind the merchant account (invisible to customers, so not a marketing device). Borrowed credibility came from Netscape's and Yahoo's editors, then from the Wall Street Journal's front page and Time's year-end list. No money-back guarantee, security-seal campaign or "100% secure — guaranteed" claim can be evidenced before 27 August 1999. The supposed Washington Post credibility link has no support in the ownership or related-party disclosures and should be filed as an anachronism.

**On failure.** The filings admit outages, four-to-six-week or never availability, price requotes needing customer approval, security fear as a category barrier, and gatekeeper dependence. They disclose no bad-debt, chargeback or unfulfilled-order rate, and this dossier does not infer either way from that silence. The most reliable negative data points are external: a competitor's "we haven't sold many books" (September 1996), analyst revenue estimates of $5-17m a year (December 1996), Amazon's refusal to give numbers to the LA Times, and Bezos's own statement that Internet book sales were "well under 1 percent" of the US market.

## Contradictions

CLAIM A: Amazon sold its first book on 16 July 1995 (Seattle Times 2005; HistoryLink 2025; popular canon). / CLAIM B: Amazon's filings say it "commenced offering products for sale on its Web site in July 1995" with "no sales" "through July 1995", while its 4 October 1995 release counted only "its first four weeks of operation". / WHY THEY MAY DIFFER: the day-level date is anniversary memory; SEC language is month-level and accounting-driven; the release may define "operation" as unrestricted public ordering rather than the first transaction, or the "four weeks" may be loose PR arithmetic. / EVIDENCE WEIGHT: S-1 and the 1995 release are Tier 1 and mutually compatible with a July-September opening; the July-16 canon is Tier 2/3 and ten years downstream. / BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: Amazon could transact in July 1995; the only dated company statement about when outside demand appeared is "first four weeks of operation" as of 4 October 1995; report the launch as "July 1995 per SEC filings, publicly announced 3-4 October 1995" and refuse a day. / RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: The day is unrecoverable from Tier-1 sources; an April 1995 invite-only order, if accurate, means the first-ever transaction predates every official dating.

CLAIM A: The first book sold was Hofstadter's "Fluid Concepts and Creative Analogies" (Atlantic 2012; MarketWatch 2015; Wikipedia citing Amazon). / CLAIM B: The first order was "a science textbook" (Seattle Times 2005). / WHY THEY MAY DIFFER: anniversary compression — a book of AI essays is loosely "science" — and a possible conflation of first order with first shipment. / EVIDENCE WEIGHT: neither is contemporaneous; the Hofstadter version is tied to a physical packing slip and the buyer's own order history. / BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: Hofstadter's book is the best-attested first non-employee order; treat "science textbook" as a degraded variant of the same story. / RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: no Amazon document naming a first title was found; the company never identified one during Stage 1.

CLAIM A: 60% of orders were repeat customers (Bezos to WSJ, May 1996). / CLAIM B: 44% (Bezos to Seattle Times, Jan 1997) and "over 40%" (S-1, Mar 1997). / WHY THEY MAY DIFFER: shifting definitions, a genuinely falling repeat share as new acquisition accelerated, or an early flattering number. / EVIDENCE WEIGHT: all three are company-supplied; only the S-1 carries filing accountability. / BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: repeat purchase was real and material, but the level is not comparable across 1996 and 1997; cite the filing and log the 60% as an unaudited founder claim. / RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: definitions were never published, so the apparent decline cannot be interpreted either way.

CLAIM A: Growth was 34-35% per month sustained for 12-18 months (three company-supplied variants, Sept 1996 to Jan 1997). / CLAIM B: Filed 1996 quarters run $875k → $8.468m, i.e. roughly 17-19% per month within 1996, and FY1995 sales were $511k in total. / WHY THEY MAY DIFFER: the soundbite may describe orders rather than revenue, or a later window, or be compounded off an undisclosed low base; look-back periods were chosen by the speaker. / EVIDENCE WEIGHT: audited filed numbers dominate the press soundbite. / BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: treat "34-35% a month for N months" as founder-supplied rhetoric with undisclosed base and window; use filed quarterly data for magnitude. / RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: whether "sales" meant orders received or revenue booked is nowhere defined.

CLAIM A: 50 states and 45 countries in the first four weeks / first month (Amazon PR 1995-10-03; Seattle Times 2005). / CLAIM B: "the first two months", plus "$20,000 per week" in the same window (Wikipedia via Amazon's own archived pages). / WHY THEY MAY DIFFER: corporate memory is restated at each anniversary and the window drifts outward while the numbers stay. / EVIDENCE WEIGHT: the 4 October 1995 release is the only Tier-1 instance and also the earliest. / BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: "By early October 1995 the company itself reported shipments to all 50 states and more than 45 countries in its first four weeks of operation" — attach no dollar figure to that window. / RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: "shipped" may include orders later cancelled; undelivered or abandoned orders cannot be excluded.

CLAIM A: Amazon enjoyed "first mover advantages" as "Earth's Biggest Bookstore" (S-1; home page). / CLAIM B: contemporaries state Amazon "isn't the first or necessarily the cheapest online book merchant", naming Book Stacks (1991 or 1992, 425,000-450,000 titles) and Britain's Internet Book Shop (912,000-915,000 titles); B&N sued in 1997 to block the slogan, calling Amazon "a book broker". / WHY THEY MAY DIFFER: Amazon's claim is about breadth of listed selection and brand, not chronology; competitors and reporters counted catalogues differently. / EVIDENCE WEIGHT: Tier 1 on both sides, with third-party counts. / BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: Amazon was first to combine directory discoverability, tiered discounting and e-mail notification in one store; it was not the first online bookseller, and its slogan was under legal attack by May 1997. / RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: Book Stacks' start year conflicts between two 1996-97 sources, so even the runner-up position is uncertain.

CLAIM A: Word of mouth was the driver of early demand (S-1's theory; Seattle Times on chat groups; company gifts). / CLAIM B: no Stage-1 document measures word of mouth; 1995 marketing spend was $200,000, the only quantified early channel claims are editorial listings and, from July 1996, Associates referrals. / WHY THEY MAY DIFFER: "word of mouth" functions as a label for unattributed traffic; without analytics disclosure there was no way to falsify it. / EVIDENCE WEIGHT: Tier 1 supports the assertion only as an assertion. / BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: report word of mouth as claimed, contemporaneously plausible (chat groups, reviews, one invited first buyer) and never measured in Stage 1. / RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: the referral share of 1995 orders is unrecoverable.

CLAIM A: A Washington Post ownership/credibility link underwrote early trust. / CLAIM B: no media shareholder appears in the S-1's ownership, related-party or marketing disclosures; documented devices are the toll-free line, e-mail and telephone ordering, confirmations, Netscape/Yahoo editorial inclusion and WSJ/Time coverage. / WHY THEY MAY DIFFER: the founder's later purchase of the newspaper is projected backwards by retrospect-tellers. / EVIDENCE WEIGHT: Tier 1 filings plus a documented null. / BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: reject the mechanism for Stage 1. / RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: this agent found no 1995-97 Post stake; absence in one filing is strong but not absolute proof about every private arrangement.

CLAIM A: hostile 1997 testimony that ordering took 37 minutes and 12 seconds and that Amazon's own page admitted a "confusing" order form (Slate, Chait and Glass). / CLAIM B: Stephen Glass's 1995-98 journalism was found by The New Republic to be largely fabricated, and the Slate page was not retrieved here. / WHY THEY MAY DIFFER: the piece may be accurate, partly invented or reconstructed from a colleague's test. / EVIDENCE WEIGHT: Tier 3 quotation of a compromised primary; the friction theme is independently supported by the S-1 and CSM. / BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: use it only as evidence that such complaints were publishable in January 1997, never as a measurement. / RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: headline, byline and content of the Slate item are unverified at source.

CLAIM A: Amazon's Stage-1 ordering model was a novel retail invention. / CLAIM B: the mechanism — take an order, buy the book from a wholesaler, repackage and ship — was standard mail-order special-order practice and was the same thing any bookstore did when out of stock, as B&N argued in 1997. / WHY THEY MAY DIFFER: the innovation is in aggregation, search, notification and centralized sourcing economics, not in the fulfillment act. / EVIDENCE WEIGHT: Tier 1 on both descriptions. / BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: Stage-1 novelty was discoverability plus a single warehouse standing between a customer and 12 wholesalers and 20,000 publishers. / RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: how much of the customer experience differed from a good independent bookstore's special order is unmeasurable from these sources.
