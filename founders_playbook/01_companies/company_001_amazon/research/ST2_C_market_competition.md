**Company/stage:** Amazon.com, Inc., 1996 through the first hard evidence of a repeatable model. The May 1997 IPO is tested as a candidate boundary, not assumed (see S2C-01/02 and "Boundary verdict" in Narrative notes).
**Hindsight firewall:** every competitor is described only from documents dated on or before the boundary evidence used here (FY1997 annual report, filed 1998-03-30, is the last Stage-2 document admitted). Nothing is graded by outcomes after 1997. Where a post-1997 document is used, it is used **only** to date an in-window event and is labelled as such.
**Reuse policy:** Stage-1 established market facts are cited from `C_market_environment.md` (records C-nn) and `D_customers_distribution.md` (records D-nn) rather than re-derived. Documented nulls inherited: no archived amazon.com page for 1995-1996-1997 (D-21, `NULL_RESULT_wayback_1995_1996.md`); no contemporaneous founder-state interview exists — the Sheff material is Playboy, conducted 1999 / published 2000 (`K_roadmap_provenance.md` K-01, K-27, and `_EVIDENCE_CACHE.md` C-1). **Every strategy statement in this dossier is therefore sourced to a filing, a dated press release or a dated press article, and no claim here rests on founder recollection.**

## Repeatability evidence

What 1996-97 actually shows, item by item, with the document, the date, and — as required — what each item does **not** demonstrate. All company-side quantities below are **self-measurement**, filed by the company about itself with no third-party audit and no published definitions (S2C-08).

1. **Cumulative customer accounts — 180,000 (to 1996-12-31) → 340,000 (to 1997-03-31) → approximately 1.5 million (to 1997-12-31).**
   Documents: original S-1 filed 1997-03-24; S-1/A No. 5 filed 1997-05-14; Form 10-K405 filed 1998-03-30 (S2C-09, S2C-35, S2B-66, S2D-34). One registration lineage for the first two — **one source, not two**.
   *Shows:* the registered base compounded faster, in absolute terms, than revenue did across all three dates.
   *Does not demonstrate:* that any of these accounts bought anything. "Customer accounts" counts registrations, is never defined, never de-duplicated, and includes abandoned ones; accounts grew 8.3x while sales grew 9.4x, so neither ratio supports a per-customer inference (S2C-09, S2B-66).
2. **Repeat purchase — "over 40%" of orders (measured through 1996-12-31 and again through 1997-03-31) → "over 58%" (10-K405's "currently").**
   Documents: S-1 1997-03-24; S-1/A No. 5 1997-05-14; 10-K405 1998-03-30 (S2C-08, S2C-35, S2B-64).
   *Shows:* the company told the SEC, twice, that most orders came from prior buyers, and told it again a year later at a higher ratio.
   *Does not demonstrate:* retention. There is no denominator — "orders" is never quantified anywhere (S2C-07, S2B-58) — no definition of "repeat customer", and the same company said **60% in May 1996 and 44% in January 1997** outside the filings (D-30). "Either the definition changed or the metric is noise. **Do not treat 58% as evidence of a retention flywheel**" (S2C-35). The 58% measurement date is not stated beyond "currently" in a document filed 1998-03-30 (S2B-64).
3. **International orders — "over 100 countries" (1996-12-31, 1997-03-31) → "over 150 countries" (1997-12-31); international sales $198k (1995) → $5.1m (1996) → $4.5m in Q1 1997 alone; share of net sales 39% → 33% → 25% → 22% (Q4 1997).**
   Documents: S-1 lineage; 10-K405 (S2C-06, S2C-09, S2D-50, S2A-66).
   *Shows:* demand genuinely dispersed across many territories and growing in absolute dollars all four years, from one warehouse.
   *Does not demonstrate:* an international business, or reach translating into mix. There was **no foreign entity and no foreign warehouse**; the share fell in every year of the window; the countries figure is a company count with no methodology; and "no foreign country or geographical area accounted for more than 10% of revenue" means no non-US market was ever served at a scale that could be tested (S2A-66, S2A-67, S2C-06).
4. **Associates Program — "over 4,800 registered members as of December 31, 1996"; "several thousand" (1997-05); "thousands" (1998-03). Named associates: Netscape Developer's Bookstore, The Village Voice, Upside.com.**
   Documents: S-1 1997-03-24; S-1/A No. 3 and No. 5; 10-K405 (S2C-39, S2D-31, S2A-63).
   *Shows:* the strongest in-window candidate for a repeatable mechanism — third-party sites reselling Amazon's catalogue, scaling without Amazon headcount or premises, dated to a specific day (S2A-63, S2C-39).
   *Does not demonstrate:* that it worked. No revenue, order or conversion share attributable to Associates appears in any document of the window; the programme's **start date is not in the record at all** (the familiar July 1996 rests on a 1998 company assertion, D-37, and a 2007-archived timeline, S2E-44); and the disclosed metric degraded rather than grew as the disclosure aged (S2C-39).
5. **Consecutive quarters of filed net sales: 875 / 2,230 / 4,173 / 8,468 (FY1996) and 16,005 / 27,855 / 37,887 / 66,011 (FY1997), each series summing exactly to its audited year, with a repeating Q4 peak (54% of FY1996, 45% of FY1997).**
   Documents: S-1/A No. 5 quarterly table 1997-05-14; 10-K405 Note 8 1998-03-30 (S2C-01, S2B-67).
   *Shows:* demand repeated across eight consecutive quarters and seasonally, on a rising base — the best filed evidence that 1996 was not a single lucky year.
   *Does not demonstrate:* unit-level repeatability. It is revenue, not contribution; the loss **grew in absolute dollars in every one of those quarters** ($331k → $3.038m, S2C-01); the Q1-1997 figures are unaudited per the filing itself; and the same documents warn "growth rates experienced to date are not sustainable" (S2C-58).
6. **Availability improvement — from "some ... available for immediate shipment, others ... within 48 to 72 hours and the remainder ... four to six weeks" (1997-03) to "**Many** ... within 24 hours, others ... within 48 to 72 hours" plus overnight and international shipping options (1998-03).**
   Documents: S-1 and 10-K405, same heading (S2C-33).
   *Shows:* the one customer-experience metric where the filings show directional progress inside the window.
   *Does not demonstrate:* how much. "Many" is undefined; the change is "Quantified nowhere"; and no in-window document gives the share of orders actually meeting the published windows (S2C-33, S2D-25). Against the incumbent's filed plan of overnight delivery from a 400,000-title DC, the **level** is unknown even where the direction favours Amazon (S2C-20).
7. **A rule-based price programme rather than ad-hoc promotion — Amazon.com 500 at 40% off from March 1997; by FY1997, 20% and 30% on more than 400,000 titles, 40% on featured titles, up to 89% on "special value" editions.**
   Documents: S-1 1997-03-24; 10-K405 1998-03-30 (S2C-04, S2B-59).
   *Shows:* discounting became standing policy, and the discount breadth widened from 500 titles (1996) to over 400,000 (1997).
   *Does not demonstrate:* that it was affordable. The company predicted the margin damage before it happened (S2C-05) and booked it: 22.0% → 19.5%, with 3.25 points gone in the single quarter after the change (S2C-03, S2B-67). It also sits, unreconciled, against the incumbent's "up to 80% off" mail-order schedule and Book Stacks's 15%-vs-10% undercut of the same period (S2C-22, D-79).
8. **Selection compounding as a listing — 1.1m titles in public circulation (June/December 1996) → "more than 2.5 million" filed (March 1997) → over one million out-of-print titles added (March 1997).**
   Documents: PR 1996-06-13; S-1; S-1/A No. 5; 10-K405 (S2C-26, S2C-32).
   *Shows:* the catalogue claim grew 2.3x in fifteen months with an out-of-print tier bolted on.
   *Does not demonstrate:* deliverable selection. Only "up to 400,000" were distributor-sourceable at 2.5m listed — a **~2.1m-title listing/fulfilment gap described as "the load-bearing number of this section"** — and the "up to 400,000 of 2.5 million" disclosure **disappears from the FY1997 annual report**, the year the company no longer had to certify it in an offering (S2C-32, S2E-08).
9. **External capital re-marked upward three times without a change of business — Kleiner Perkins Series A at $14.05 (June 1996), director and officer rounds at $40.00 (January/February 1997, a 2.8x step-up), IPO priced at $18.00 above its own final filed range.**
   Documents: S-1 exhibit index and 10-K405 Note 5 (S2C-27); IPO pricing records in ST2_A/ST2_B.
   *Shows:* three independent sophisticated parties priced the equity upward inside five months, and the public market paid above the range.
   *Does not demonstrate:* repeatability of the model — "it proves belief that it would" (S2B Repeatability item 8). Share counts and per-share prices are basis-dependent across two splits inside the window; do not divide one round by another to get a valuation (S2C-27).
10. **Structural counter-evidence, recorded so the list above is not read as a verdict: order-level fulfilment performance was never quantified.**
    Documents: four filings, 1995-1997 (S2E-39; S2C-07).
    *Shows:* there is **no order count, no fill rate, no cancellation rate, no backorder rate and no returns figure** anywhere in the corpus, and the disclosures that come closest are warnings — inventory and fulfilment "management" and "the level of merchandise returns" appear only as quarterly variability factors, and a price-approval procedure exists precisely because estimates were wrong often enough to need one.
    *Does not demonstrate:* failure. It demonstrates that the repeatability question cannot be answered from the primary record at the unit level at all.
11. **What a repeatable mechanism required and did not have: contractual durability.** No publisher or distributor contract appears anywhere in the S-1 exhibit index; "The Company has no long-term contracts or arrangements with any of its vendors that guarantee the availability of merchandise, the continuation of particular payment terms or the extension of credit limits"; Ingram was 59% (1996) and 58% (1997) of inventory purchases — i.e. the dependence did **not** improve with scale. The company also created a "Vice President of Publisher Affairs" in February 1997, which evidences publisher relations as a management problem, not a publisher agreement (S2C-51, S2C-53).

## Narrative notes

**1. Boundary verdict.** On this dossier's own records the May 1997 IPO is a **resource discontinuity, not a repeatability certification** (S2E-38), and the two candidate boundary events the brief tests both fail on the filings: (i) *music* — the popular Stage-2 closer — shipped **June 1998**, and the FY1997 annual report filed 1998-03-30 still describes a book retailer (S2A-70, S2A-68, S2C-28); (ii) *the IPO itself*, because at 1997-05-15 the company had one quarter above $16m of revenue, a single warehouse through which all sales passed, no second category, no disclosed shipping economics and a loss that had grown in absolute dollars every quarter (S2C-01, S2D-01, S2B-58). The defensible in-window endpoint is therefore **1997-12-31 / 1998-03-30** — the FY1997 audited close plus the 23 December 1997 secured loan, which is the first document in the record that constrains the **operating model** rather than the cap table (S2C-50; S2B Repeatability item 9; S2E-38 rules 1997-05-14/15 as the *boundary* with 1997-12-31 as a "mandatory sub-endpoint that Stage 2 must narrate anyway"). On the market-and-competition evidence specifically, what repeats is narrower than "the business model": eight consecutive growing quarters, a widening rule-based discount schedule, a dispersing export base with no foreign operation, and an Associates network that was counted once in one way and never again.

**2. The registrant denied having a moat, in its own IPO document, while a needham analyst said the same thing on the wire.** "Barriers to entry are minimal, and current and new competitors can launch new sites at a relatively low cost" appears verbatim in the original S-1 and in both later amendments of the lineage (S2C-12), and independently in print the next day: "there aren't a lot of barriers to entrance" (D-82, 1997-03-25). This is the strongest anti-hindsight item the dossier holds: the company that is later described as structurally protected told paying investors it was not. Selection, the asset usually credited, is filed as *not* defensible — only up to 400,000 of 2.5m offered titles were sourceable, and the catalogue file itself was licensed from unnamed third parties (S2C-31, S2C-32, S2E-25). Technology is filed as bought rather than built, with no patent in the prospectus's IP section and collaborative filtering still in the conditional (S2D-19, S2D-30, S2E-13, S2E-16). Switching costs reduce to a stored password against a comparison-shopping technology the company expressly foresaw (S2E-25). What survived the test was commercial and contingent: an aggregated fillable long tail, purchase volume with one wholesaler, and supplier float.

**3. The incumbent picture is the reverse of the popular one, on the incumbent's own filing.** The 1997 observer looking at Barnes & Noble saw a company that had posted a **net loss of $52.976m the year before** (S2C-19), was closing eighteen of its own stores in the profitable year (S2C-18), was watching its mall base fall 781 → 577 units (S2C-18, C-37), and had *not yet launched a website* at the moment Amazon filed — only an AOL relationship (S2C-11, S2C-13). "Decisive" is not the in-window condition; "large, profitable this year, mid-ramp, and suing" is (S2C-17, S2C-54). Equally, "asleep" is not accurate either: B&N's filed online plan attacked Amazon's two weakest axes — speed and price — from a 344,000 sq ft DC stocking over 400,000 titles, with a 2.5m-title database and a direct-mail list of over a million names (S2C-20, S2C-21, S2C-22). The honest 1997 statement is that the incumbent promised to hold, in one warehouse, the entire fillable pool Amazon listed, and to ship it overnight.

**4. Two different objects were wearing the same number, which is why "selection" cannot be compared across competitors in this period.** Amazon's "2.5 million titles" was a count of items *offered for sale*, defined by the company to include "primarily books but also a small number of CDs, videotapes, audiotapes and other products" (S2C-28, S2A-72). B&N's 2.5m was a *bibliographic database* it could special-order from, and its stores' in-print universe was "more than 1.2 million books in print" (S2C-21, C-21). Amazon's own fillable number was 400,000; the average superstore carried about 130,000, under 10% of titles in print (S2D-52). A searcher wanting a moat from "selection" must first show the selection was obtainable, and the registrant itself could not (S2E-08).

**5. The company's growth story names two causes and omits its own two largest costs.** Identical sentences in three filings attribute sales growth to more customers and repeat purchase, with price as "a partial offset" (S2C-37). The same filings disclose that marketing and sales went $200k → $6.090m → $38.964m across 1995-97, that the 1997 increase is attributed to "advertising and promotional expenditures (including expenses associated with Internet aggregator promotional relationships)" and to card fees, and that fixed payments were being made to aggregators under a named risk that they may not "generate adequate revenues" (S2C-40, S2C-42). Two cautions on that line: it **cannot** be read as a customer-acquisition ratio, because "All fulfillment costs not included in cost of sales, including the cost of operating and staffing distribution centers and customer service, are included in marketing and sales" (S2C-42); and the aggregator disclosure arrives **only** in the 1998 filing — the word appears in none of the three 1997 registration documents (S2C-40). That single differential is the definitive answer to Stage 1's null: by 1997-98 directory placement was a fixed cash commitment, not a free editorial gift.

**6. The most-cited proof of reach moved the wrong way, and the press printed only the flattering half.** International sales were 39% of net sales in 1995, 33% in 1996, 25% in 1997 and 22% in Q4 1997, with the April 1997 amendment stating the decline explicitly (S2C-06). Meanwhile the headline in circulation was "customer accounts in over 150 countries" (S2C-09). Both are true; they measure different things, and only one was quoted. The territory correction goes further: there was no foreign entity and no foreign warehouse at the close of the window, and no country exceeded 10% of revenue (S2A-66, S2A-67). Stage 2 contains **no internationalisation step** — an unmanaged export by-product from one Seattle address.

**7. Disclosure quality is itself a trend line, and it runs in both directions at once.** The competitive roster was **rewritten** rather than extended between March 1997 and March 1998 — Book Stacks, CUC, AOL, Microsoft, Borders and Simon & Schuster all disappear, replaced by B&N and Bertelsmann, with no explanation given (S2C-16). The "up to 400,000 of 2.5 million" fillability disclosure vanishes with the certification requirement (S2E-08). Three risk factors present at the IPO — online commerce security, sales tax collection, and dependence on the continued growth of online commerce — are gone by the annual report, and **the tax risk was removed in the same year the company opened a Delaware distribution centre, which is precisely the event the deleted risk said would create exposure** (S2C-49). Four new factors appear, mapping what management now thought could kill it, headed by a $75m secured term loan (S2C-50). And the B&N suit, disclosed in the registration statement, is absent from the annual report's legal-proceedings position, which states the company "currently is not aware of any legal proceedings or claims that ... will have ... a material adverse effect" (S2C-54). The record shows a claim appearing and then disappearing with no disclosed resolution: **outcome UNKNOWN; neither victory nor defeat may be inferred.**

**8. The one customer-experience metric that genuinely improved, and the honest cost of it.** Availability moved from "four to six weeks" for the in-print remainder to "Many ... within 24 hours" with overnight and international options (S2C-33), and the promised out-of-print window tightened from two-to-six months to one-to-three months with the failure caveat retained (S2A-61). But the mechanism of that improvement was the abandonment of the structural advantage the same company had filed as its economic case: buying inventory, opening a 200,000 sq ft Delaware DC and expanding Seattle to 85,000 sq ft (S2C-34, S2C-36, S2B X-7). B&N had done the same thing fifteen months earlier at 344,000 sq ft (S2C-20). Stage 2's operational ending is therefore convergence with the incumbent's cost base, not escape from it.

**9. Every demand-side number in this dossier is self-measurement, and one of them is the only third-party figure available.** Accounts, repeat share, visits, countries and titles are Amazon's own instrumentation, with no audit and no published definitions (S2C-08). Against that, the single externally quantified position estimate for 1996 — PC-Meter's 1.6% of the online shopping market, with analyst revenue estimates spanning $5m to $17m a year — straddles the $15.746m Amazon later filed, i.e. the market could not see the company's size within a 3.4x band (S2C-25, D-73). The company's own refusal to give figures to newspapers the same month is what makes that band the best third-party evidence that exists (D-24). **The "average order size" commonly quoted for 1997 has no antecedent in any of the four filings and must not be used** (S2C-07).

## Contradictions

CLAIM A: Amazon's lead in 1996-97 was decisive, against a $2.448bn incumbent 16x its size.
CLAIM B: The incumbent lost $52.976m the year before, closed 18 of its own stores in its profitable year, saw its mall base fall 781 → 577, and had not launched a website at the moment Amazon filed — only an AOL relationship.
WHY THEY DIFFER: A measures a fiscal year ending 1997-02-01 and uses a size ratio as a proxy for competitive position; B reads the same filing's trend lines, which move in two directions at once.
EVIDENCE WEIGHT: Both come from **the same counterparty primary filing** (S2C-17, S2C-18, S2C-19, S2C-11, S2C-13). Neither reading is an inference — but the "1.4x" half of A's ratio is not consistent with the figures in the record that states it (Provenance note 1).
BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: Amazon led on the web channel only, and led over an incumbent that had already entered it. "Decisive incumbent" and "asleep incumbent" are both post-1997 framings the FY1997 B&N filing does not support.
RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: B&N's own online execution inside the window is unevidenced in a primary document — its 1997 online sales reach us only through a tier-3 encyclopedia history (S2E-23).
CONFIDENCE: High on both readings' facts; Medium on any comparative verdict.

CLAIM A: Repeat customers accounted for over 40% of orders (S-1, twice filed) and then over 58% (FY1997 10-K) — a rising retention curve.
CLAIM B: The same company said 60% in May 1996 and 44% in January 1997 outside the filings (D-30); no document defines "repeat customer", "order", or the measurement date of the 58%.
WHY THEY DIFFER: Undisclosed definitional change, a genuinely falling repeat share as acquisition accelerated, flattering numbers given to reporters, or all three; the 58% sits in a document filed 1998-03-30 under the word "currently".
EVIDENCE WEIGHT: Four company-supplied figures; only the filed ones carry legal accountability, and the two 40% instances are one lineage.
BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: Repeat purchase was real and material, but **its level is not comparable across 1996 and 1997**, and the 40%→58% move cannot be reconciled with a stable definition (S2C-08, S2C-35, S2B-64).
RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: Total orders are nowhere published, so neither percentage converts into anything checkable.
CONFIDENCE: High as filed; **Low as measurement. Do not treat 58% as evidence of a retention flywheel.**

CLAIM A: Amazon's reach was global — "approximately 1.5 million customer accounts in over 150 countries".
CLAIM B: International sales fell as a share of net sales every year of the window, 39% → 33% → 25% → 22% (Q4 1997); there was no foreign entity and no foreign warehouse; no country exceeded 10% of revenue.
WHY THEY DIFFER: One counts registrations reached from a single US warehouse; the other measures revenue mix as domestic sales grew faster.
EVIDENCE WEIGHT: Both are Amazon's own filings (S2C-06, S2C-09, S2A-66, S2A-67, S2D-50); the account figure is unaudited self-measurement, the shares sit in audited notes.
BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: Reach and mix are different measurements, and the press used only the former. Stage 2 contains **no internationalisation step** — dispersing export demand from one Seattle address.
RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: Cross-border freight and processing sit in undisclosed aggregates, so international contribution margin is unknown.
CONFIDENCE: High.

CLAIM A: Music and video were part of Amazon's business from 1996-97, and category expansion proves the model repeated.
CLAIM B: Every document in the window puts non-books in one of three places — a "small number" inside the definition of "titles", the **competitors'** territory, or an intention ("intends over time to expand its catalog into other information-based products, such as music"); the FY1997 annual report still calls the company "the leading online retailer of books"; and the music store's own press release is dated June 1998.
WHY THEY DIFFER: A later category history is back-projected onto earlier filings, and the "titles" definition silently absorbed a handful of discs.
EVIDENCE WEIGHT: Tier 1 on both sides, and the B-side is the registrant's own language across four documents (S2C-28, S2C-29, S2C-30, S2A-17, S2A-18, S2A-68, S2A-69, S2A-70, S2A-72, S2E-07).
BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: Through 1997-12-31 the only dated, purchasable expansions are the Amazon.com 500 pricing, the out-of-print service, the Gift Center and a second distribution centre — **all book-adjacent or book-infrastructure**. A capability claim resting on the word "intends" should be written as an intention, dated, and left there.
RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: The size and shippability of the "small number" of discs and tapes is unknown; no count was ever filed.
CONFIDENCE: Very High (S2E-07 calls this the cleanest, most checkable finding in the adversarial review).

CLAIM A: "2.5 million titles" was Amazon's selection advantage over everyone else.
CLAIM B: Of those, "up to 400,000 are currently supplied by book distributors and wholesalers"; and B&N's stores could special-order any book from "more than 1.2 million books in print" against a 2.5m-title database.
WHY THEY DIFFER: "Titles offered" counts listings; "titles fillable" counts stock-linked supply; the incumbent's number counts bibliographic availability mediated by a store.
EVIDENCE WEIGHT: Both primary filings, internally consistent — different metrics, not competing estimates (S2C-21, S2C-32, C-21, C-22, S2E-08, S2D-52).
BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: The real and defensible edge was searchability plus wholesaler-mediated on-demand sourcing over a ~350-400k pool — roughly 3x a superstore, not 20x. **The ~2.1m-title listing/fulfilment gap is the load-bearing number of the category record.**
RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: How fast the fillable pool grew after 1997 is outside this window; the disclosure itself disappears from the FY1997 annual report.
CONFIDENCE: Very High on the gap; Low on any competitor equivalent, which is not in the record.

CLAIM A: Growth is explained by customer acquisition and repeat purchase — the identical causal sentence in three filings, naming no third factor.
CLAIM B: The same filings disclose marketing and sales rising $200k → $6.09m → $38.964m, paid banners on seven named sites, and "significant fixed payments" to Internet aggregators that might not "generate adequate revenues".

RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: The marketing-and-sales line contains all fulfilment and customer-service cost, so even the cost side cannot be read as acquisition spend.
CONFIDENCE: High that the tension exists; none on its resolution.

CLAIM A: The competitive field narrowed, and the suit was survived — by FY1997 only B&N and Bertelsmann are named, and the litigation is no longer reported.

RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: Neither the 1998 proxy nor any docket material is in the corpus.

CLAIM A: Headcount at 1996-12-31 was 151 full-time employees (S-1).

CLAIM A: The company's risk catalogue narrowed as it matured — online security, sales tax and dependence on online growth all drop out of the FY1997 annual report.
CLAIM B: The tax risk was deleted in the same year the company opened a Delaware distribution centre, which is exactly the event the deleted factor said would create exposure: "any new operation in states outside Washington could subject shipments into such states to state sales taxes".

RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: Whether legal or business judgement drove it is unrecorded.
CONFIDENCE: High on the differential; none on the motive.

CLAIM A: Amazon's asset-light, minimal-inventory, no-retail-real-estate model was its structural economic advantage.
CLAIM B: By November 1997 it had opened a 200,000 sq ft Delaware DC, expanded Seattle to 85,000 sq ft and disclosed plans "to continue to increase its merchandise inventory"; and the competitor's suit ran on the claim that Amazon stocked "only a few hundred" titles.
