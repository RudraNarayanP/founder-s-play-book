## Repeatability evidence

What the filings actually establish about model repeatability, and what each item does NOT establish.

1. **Repeat purchase, 1996 state — ">40% of orders" from repeat customers.** Attributable, by date, to: original S-1 filed 1997-03-24 (measured "Through December 31, 1996"; S-1/ORIG lines 314-320, 446-455, 1730-1738) and verbatim in S-1/A No. 5 filed 1997-05-14 (measured "Through March 31, 1997"; lines 336-341, 474-482, 1916-1922). These are ONE source (same lineage). Company-asserted, unaudited, undefined ("repeat customer" is never defined), and expressed as a share of ORDERS — a denominator the filings never publish, so the claim cannot be independently checked. **Proves:** the company told the SEC, twice, that most orders came from prior buyers, as of both 1996-12-31 and 1997-03-31. **Does not prove:** frequency, cohort retention, or that a unit order was profitable.
2. **Repeat purchase, 1997 state — ">58% of orders"** in the FY1997 10-K405 (line 243), the independent Tier-1 witness. The measurement date is only "currently" in a document filed 1998-03-30 and can be read as year-end 1997 at best. **Proves:** the ratio improved by ~18 points across the window on the company's own definition. **Does not prove:** that the 1998 definition is the 1997 definition.
3. **Cumulative account base:** 180,000 (to 1996-12-31) → 340,000 (to 1997-03-31) → ~1,500,000 (to 1997-12-31). Cumulative registered accounts, never defined or de-duplicated. **Proves:** acquisition kept outrunning revenue in absolute increments. **Does not prove:** any individual repeat — see S2B-66.
4. **Geographic spread:** "over 100 countries" at both 1996-12-31 and 1997-03-31; "over 150 countries" at 1997-12-31 (S-1/ORIG line 316; S-1/A-5 line 337; 10-K405 line 243). International as a share of net sales 39% (1995), 33% (1996), 25% (1997), and 28% for Q1-1997, with the absolute international line rising $198k → $5.1m → $36.2m; "No foreign country or geographical area accounted for more than 10% of revenue in any of the periods presented" (10-K405 lines 2179-2183; S-1/A-5 lines 4042-4045). **Proves:** demand genuinely dispersed, and the falling international *share* alongside a rising international *line* is the correct, non-contradictory reading. **Does not prove:** international unit economics — cross-border freight and processing sit in undisclosed aggregates, so country mix says nothing about contribution.
5. **Category mix:** qualitative only. The catalogue "includes primarily books but also a small number of CDs, videotapes and audiotapes" (S-1/A-5 lines 492-493), and the FY1997 document states the intent "to expand its catalog into other information-based products, such as music" (10-K405 line 236) with the warning that "Gross margins attributable to new business areas may be lower" (10-K405 lines 1422-1425). **Proves:** in Stage 2 there was one category; diversification is future tense. **Does not prove:** anything about a mix shift — no revenue is disaggregated by category (UNKNOWN above). Dating music or verticals into 1996-97 imports Stage 3.
6. **Structural repeatability of the price promise:** the Amazon.com 500 at 40% off list became a standing feature from March 1997 (S2B-59), then a 20/30/40/up-to-89% tiered schedule on 400,000+ titles by year end. **Proves:** discounting was becoming a rule-based programme rather than ad-hoc promotion. **Does not prove:** it was affordable — it cut gross margin 3.25 points the following quarter, which is the central tension of the stage.
7. **Repeatability of demand, quarter on quarter:** filed quarterly net sales 875 / 2,230 / 4,173 / 8,468 (FY1996) and 16,005 / 27,855 / 37,887 / 66,011 (FY1997), each summing exactly to its audited year, both series filed twice (S2B-67). "Compounded quarterly sales growth exceeded 100% from the first to the fourth quarter of 1996", and the same claim extended 1996Q1→1997Q1 (S-1/ORIG lines 448-451; S-1/A-5 lines 475-478). **Proves:** a repeating Q4 peak (Q4-96 = 54% of the year; Q4-97 = 45%) on a rising base — the clearest filed evidence of durable rather than one-off demand. **Does not prove:** unit-level repeatability: it is revenue, not contribution, and the filings themselves warn "Growth rates experienced to date are not sustainable."
8. **Institutional validation as a repeatability proxy:** a five-director board with a Kleiner Perkins seat from June 1996, plus Scott Cook (January 1997) and Patricia Stonesifer (February 1997) buying the same preferred at $40.00 — 2.85× the June 1996 price (S2B-26, S2B-27); then an IPO clearing at $18.00 against a $14.00-$16.00 filed range, wholly primary, in a firm-commitment ten-house syndicate. **Proves:** three independent sophisticated parties priced the equity upward inside five months, and the public market paid above the range. **Does not prove:** the model repeated — it proves belief that it would. The 10-K405's own record of the first nine months of trading (Q2-97 low $15.75, Q4-97 high $65.50) is a price path, not a business result.
9. **VERDICT ON THE STAGE BOUNDARY.** The May 1997 IPO is tested, and on this corpus it does NOT by itself constitute evidence of a repeatable model: at 1997-05-15 the company had one quarter above $16m of revenue, one warehouse through which "all sales" passed, no category beyond books, no separately disclosed shipping economics, $79,000 of pre-IPO working capital, a $27.6m annual loss run-rate forming, and no contribution margin outside the derived advertising-only line. The hard evidence of repeatability in-window is narrower and specific: **repeat orders >40% → >58% on the company's own definition, four-plus consecutive quarters of >100% sequential growth, a dispersing international base with no single geography above 10% of revenue, and a filed quarterly gross-margin series showing the pricing programme working exactly as warned.** The decisive institutionalisation events are the 14/15 May 1997 pricing (external discipline: real money at a fixed price, a 7% discount, disclosed lock-ups, Rule 144 overhang) and the 23 December 1997 Loan (internal discipline: covenants on cash, EBITDA, payables aging and capex, a first-priority lien on everything, a sweep on any future raise, and a $52.11 warrant kicker). **Stage 2 closes on the Loan, not on the IPO: the Loan is the first document in the record that constrains the operating model rather than the cap table.**

## Narrative notes

**On the shape of the money.** Stage 2 is the passage from a company financed by a founder's personal guarantees and one venture cheque to one financed by a public market and then by secured bank debt over all its assets. The distances are short — ten months from the $40.00 director round to the $75.0m Loan — and the terms get monotonically less forgiving. In June 1996 the investor took a preference at exactly cost, a non-cumulative dividend and a one-for-one conversion: the cheapest capital the company ever raised, because it was paid for with a board seat. By December 1997 the lender wanted a first-priority lien on "substantially all" assets, LIBOR + 3.50%, a sweep on any future raise, a limit on how long the company could take to pay the book suppliers who were funding it, and warrants struck near market. That progression is the institutionalisation of Amazon.com told entirely in terms of what outsiders required.

**On the payables engine, and why FY1997's "positive operating cash" is the most dangerous single figure in this stage.** Amazon lost $27.6m in 1997 and reported +$3.5m of cash from operations. The reconciliation is not subtle: +$29.8m of accounts payable, +$5.1m of other accruals, +$2.9m of accrued advertising, +$4.7m of D&A, against the $27.6m loss, less $8.4m of inventory and $3.0m of prepayments. Read plainly: the growth was financed by the distributors, and Ingram — 58-59% of purchases, under no long-term contract, with no guarantee of "the continuation of particular payment terms or the extension of credit limits" — was the counterparty. That is why the December 1997 covenant limiting "accounts payable aging" is not boilerplate; a lender had identified the actual funding source and capped it. The negative signal is not that supplier credit was used. It is that nothing in the record shows the arrangement could be extended further at a much larger revenue base.

**On what the IPO did and did not do.** It was pure primary — no founder, no Kleiner Perkins fund and no friends-and-family holder sold a share in it. Every dollar of the $54.0m came from new investors to the company, and the prospectus said, in terms, that it had "no specific plan" for the money and would use it funding "anticipated operating losses". Two features of the lineage deserve dwelling on. First, the price walk: blank, then $12.00-$14.00 on 2,500,000 shares on 9 May; $14.00-$16.00 on 3,000,000 shares on 14 May; $18.00 on 15 May. A deal re-sized up 20% and re-ranged up 29% in five days, then priced a further 12.5% above the top of its own final range — in the same week Barnes & Noble sued it over the word "bookstore", and the prospectus itself printed the wire-service line that the suit "would probably put a 'little bit of a damper' on Amazon.com's IPO" (S-1/A-5 lines 2530-2536), under a disclaimer that the remarks were "not authorized by and have not been endorsed by the Company". Second, the constraint the raise did not remove: as late as 14 May 1997 the filing still stated an *intention* to "secure releases of all of Mr. Bezos' guarantees" after closing, and the company had bound itself not to issue stock for 180 days. The IPO bought capital and a market. It did not buy near-term financial flexibility.

**On the ">40% repeat" sentence, which is where this project will be tempted.** It is a real, dated, twice-filed Tier-1 sentence belonging to specific accessions measured at specific dates. It is also an unaudited, undefined company assertion about a denominator — orders — that the company never published. Both facts must travel together. The same discipline disposes of the Series A valuation folklore: the filings give 569,396 shares, $14.05 exact to the cent on each of two lines, two named funds, one date, $8,000,013.80 — and no valuation. Multiplying the post-IPO share count by $18.00 yields a May-1997 market capitalisation of ~$429m (DERIVED: 23,858,702 × $18.00), which is a public-market fact about May 1997 and is not a June 1996 venture valuation. Running that number backwards is exactly the error this pass exists to block.

**On what was retracted and stays retracted.** The one-third leg of the 1995-96 friends-and-family sequence is not revived anywhere above. Item 13 footnote 4 states one transaction: 3,021,000 shares to 23 investors at ~$0.3333 for $1,007,000 between 6 December 1995 and 16 May 1996. That is the whole filed fact, and `2,613,000` appears in none of the five documents.

**On the two audit periods and the annual report that never happened.** Both Ernst & Young opinions in this window are unqualified, with no going-concern paragraph and no emphasis-of-matter, and the second is dated 19 January 1998 — five weeks before the 10-K405 was filed. There is no FY1996 annual report; the FY1997 10-K405 is the first, so everything before 15 May 1997 is knowable only through the registration statements. That asymmetry is why FY1996 survives in the record in three versions (original S-1, amended S-1, and the 10-K405 comparative) with disagreeing per-share numbers, and it is why the audited series above carries a filing-and-line reference on every row instead of presenting a clean panel.

**On the margin paradox, which is the real finding of this pass.** FY1997 is simultaneously better and worse than FY1996 on every axis a reader might pick up: operating margin improved 18 points while the loss quintupled; revenue per employee tripled while headcount quadrupled; every expense ratio fell while every expense line rose in dollars; operating cash turned positive while the company borrowed $75m two weeks before year end; and gross margin — the one ratio that measures the customer proposition rather than the denominator — fell, deliberately, because the company chose to. The filings are not evasive about this; they explain each move in the same paragraph. The Stage-2 finance record is therefore not a story of a model failing to be visible; it is a story of the only visibility available being revenue-side, with the unit-level economics structurally unobservable because order counts, shipping economics and processing costs are nowhere disclosed.
