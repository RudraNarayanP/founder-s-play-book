## Narrative notes

**The name.** The Tier-1 record answers only part of the question. The founding instrument is real and re-verified on disk: Exhibit 10.12 to the original S-1 is a subscription dated July 5, 1994 by "a resident of the State of Washington" for 1,700,000 shares of "Cadabra, Inc., a Washington corporation" for $10,000. The May 1997 amendment confirms the same Washington incorporation with no name at all. What the record does **not** contain is any dated, non-founder statement that a name change occurred, of when, or of why: "Cadabra" occurs exactly once across both filings (in the exhibit), the phrases "changed its name" and "renamed" occur nowhere, and the alternative form "Cadamia" occurs nowhere. So the change is knowable only indirectly, through the instruments the company filed under the new style: the amazon.com domain (1994-11-01, per the project's registry record, not re-verified here), the first sales and the "Earth's Biggest Bookstore" trade dress in July 1995, the company's own 1995-10-04 release using "Amazon.com" as "the business and domain name," and the "AMAZON" federal application filed 1995-10-23. One documentary trap deserves emphasis: Exhibit 10.13, dated February 9, 1995, recites that the company was then "Amazon.com, Inc. ... a Delaware corporation," which is impossible — Delaware arrived in May/June 1996. The exhibit was evidently conformed to the later corporate form before filing, so no one should date the name change, or the Delaware move, from that recital.

**Was there a name/trademark dispute in 1994-96?** No, and the negative is well evidenced rather than merely unproven. The register shows an application filed 1995-10-23 that grew into Reg. No. 2078496 with no opposition and no cancellation; the only proceedings on the file are suit notifications from 2020 onward. Both S-1 versions state that the company faced no pending proceedings as of 1997-03-24, and the first adverse assertion anywhere in the record is Barnes & Noble's January 1997 letter, followed by the May 12, 1997 SDNY suit — both after Stage 1, and the suit is a false-advertising/"not a bookstore" claim, not a name fight. The widely repeated "Cadabra → Cadaver" story is a founder recollection recorded later (Tier 1 *as a founder statement*, dated when recorded), and no contemporaneous 1994-96 report or register event corroborates any objection by any prior "Amazon" user. Whether unrelated "AMAZON" marks already existed in other classes was not enumerable within budget (TESS-style search unavailable; the Washington corporations registry and OpenCorporates both failed for prior agents and were not retried here); that residue is logged as a gap, not converted into a dispute.

**Corporate form and securities posture.** A Washington corporation from July 1994, taxed as a Subchapter S entity until March 31, 1995, when a Subchapter C election was made "with the consent of its stockholders" — a detail with organizational consequences, since S status limits shareholders to eligible individuals and one class of stock, and it fits a cap table built from a founder, two parents, one engineer and one outside money man. Founder paper at inception carried investment representations, restrictive legends and stop-transfer orders. Outside money came in under Section 4(2), at $0.1717 (parents, February and July 1995) and then $0.3333 in a $1,007,000, 23-purchaser placement running from December 6, 1995 into May 1996 — meaning the familiar "about $1 million of friends and family" straddles the Stage-1 boundary and cannot be booked wholly inside 1995. The investor-rights architecture ran *against* the investors' liquidity: a board-consent transfer bar to 1999-12-31, a 60-day company option and a 30-day co-shareholder option thereafter, and employee/non-employee classes. There is no trace in the filings of an investor-process dispute; the Kleiner Perkins governance package (voting agreement, investor rights, co-sale, ROFR, and a $0.0010 repurchase right over 612,000 of Bezos's own shares) arrives June 21, 1996.

**Contracts.** The most consequential legal fact about Stage-1 supply is an absence: the company bought a substantial majority of its books from two distributors, Ingram (59% of 1996 inventory purchases) and Baker & Taylor, while disclosing that it had "no long-term contracts or arrangements with any of its vendors that guarantee the availability of merchandise, the continuation of particular payment terms or the extension of credit limits." No distribution agreement and no publisher agreement appears in the exhibit set. Payment acceptance, the thing that made an online store possible at all, was secured not by contract strength but by the founder's personal credit: Seafirst from November 1994, Wells Fargo from July 1995, company cards from April 1995, all personally guaranteed, with the company promising to buy those releases after the IPO.

**Sales tax.** Amazon's Stage-1 position was deliberate and disclosed: it collected sales or similar taxes only on shipments into Washington, and warned that other states "may seek to impose sales tax collection obligations on out-of-state companies such as the Company which engage in online commerce," adding that any new operation outside Washington would itself trigger collection on shipments into that state. That is the Quill physical-presence standard operating as background law (Quill Corp. v. North Dakota, 504 U.S. 298 (1992) — decided two years before Amazon existed, so it is context, not a Stage-1 event). No in-window state assessment, litigation or investigation against Amazon could be established here: the one targeted retrieval died on an HTTP 403, and the case name that surfaced in search results is a lead, not evidence. Equally, post-2008 state "Amazon laws" and the 2018 Wayfair decision appeared in exactly these searches and are excluded as hindsight.

**Organization.** Read strictly from filings, the Stage-1 firm was two named people plus nine or so unnamable ones: Bezos (President and Chairman, not CEO until May 1996) and Kaphan (VP R&D from October 1994, an option letter effective October 24, 1994 and a shareholder agreement August 8, 1995). Every other officer in the IPO roster joined between July 1996 and March 1997. Headcount disclosure begins at 11 on December 31, 1995 and rises to 151/158 a year later and 256 by March 1997 — the entire executive and functional build-out is a post-window event, which is itself the Stage-1 finding. Around the two-person core the company layered contractors and temporary staff in editorial, operations and finance, and admitted as much. Outside governance in the window is limited to one man: Tom A. Alberg, whose shareholder agreement (November 26, 1995) and dual option grants (December 6, 1995) precede his June 1996 directorship, while the agreements he signed require "a majority vote of the Board of Directors" — a board whose pre-June-1996 composition the record never identifies. Premises show up in the filings only as rent: $2,000 in 1994, $12,000 in 1995, $257,000 in 1996, then 42,400 square feet and a single leased Seattle site holding substantially all the hardware, with no redundancy and no disaster-recovery plan. The Bellevue house, the garage desks and the Seattle Square One/SODO addresses are regional history and memoir (Tier 2/3), not filing evidence.

**Regulatory perimeter inside the window.** Of the items the brief asks about, two are dated inside 1996 and therefore admissible as tail context: Section 230 was enacted February 8, 1996 (Pub. L. 104-104, title V, § 509), and Commerce moved cryptographic commodities and software off the U.S. Munitions List onto the Commerce Control List effective December 30, 1996 after the November 15, 1996 directives, in language that names promoting electronic commerce as an objective. Neither touches the firm as the record shows it: the company said it was "not currently subject to direct regulation by any domestic or foreign governmental agency," while warning that the application of existing tax, libel and privacy law to the Internet was "uncertain and may take years to resolve." It used "encryption and authentication technology" to take cards, which makes it a user of a then-controlled class rather than an exporter of one. The crypto rules during 1994-95 (the core window) sat with State under ITAR per the 1996 rule's own recital, and no action, opinion or licence involving Amazon is evidenced in either period.

## Contradictions

**C-H1 — The company's own name and state, in its own exhibit.**
CLAIM A: Exhibit 10.12 (July 5, 1994) — "Cadabra, Inc., a Washington corporation"; Exhibit 2.1 (May 28, 1996) — the Registrant is "a Washington corporation" merging into "Amazon.com, Inc., a Delaware corporation"; Note 3 — Delaware reincorporation on May 28/June 18, 1996.
CLAIM B: Exhibit 10.13, bearing the date **February 9, 1995**, recites the counterparty as "Amazon.com, Inc. ... a Delaware corporation."
WHY THEY DIFFER: B is chronologically impossible as a contemporaneous recital; the exhibit set was assembled in 1996-97 and the 1995 agreement was evidently conformed/re-papered to the post-reincorporation form (or re-executed) while keeping its original date.
EVIDENCE WEIGHT: A wins decisively — three independent instruments of the same filing agree, and the Delaware dates are stated twice.
BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: In February 1995 the shareholder contracted with a Washington corporation then styled Cadabra, Inc. (Amazon.com in brand use); the exhibit's preamble is not evidence of the 1995 corporate style.
RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: Whether the name change was effected by amendment in 1994, 1995 or by the 1996 merger papers is still unresolvable from the record; and the possibility that Ex.10.13 replaced an earlier 1995 document cannot be excluded without the Washington registry file.

**C-H2 — Which document is "the S-1" (project-level provenance).**
CLAIM A: This project's brief and `_EVIDENCE_CACHE.md` describe accession 000089102097000839 as "the Form S-1 ... filed 1997-03-24", a 1.44 MB full text.
CLAIM B: That accession's own EDGAR header reads `CONFORMED SUBMISSION TYPE: S-1/A`, `FILED AS OF DATE: 19970514`, `PUBLIC DOCUMENT COUNT: 2`, and the retrieved body is 301,685 bytes. The 1,444,013-byte original is accession **0000891618-97-001309**, header `S-1`, `FILED AS OF DATE: 19970324`, `PUBLIC DOCUMENT COUNT: 38`.
WHY THEY DIFFER: Cache-table drift — two accessions of the same registration statement (333-23795) conflated after the `_scratch` deletion, with the size of one attached to the identifier of the other.
EVIDENCE WEIGHT: B, on headers read directly this session (both files now saved in `../sources/`).
BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: Cite 0000891618-97-001309 for exhibit-level Stage-1 documents (Ex.10.12-10.24, Ex.2.1) and 0000891020-97-000839 for the May 1997 amendment text (Litigation/Note disclosures).
RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: Whether the destroyed `s1_orig.txt` was in fact the 1.44 MB original S-1 under the wrong accession label — likely yes; dossier A used the correct one.

**C-H3 — Employees at December 31, 1996: 151 vs 158.**
CLAIM A: Original S-1 (1997-03-24): "As of December 31, 1996, the Company employed 151 full-time employees."
CLAIM B: 1996 Form 10-K, per sibling dossier E: 158 employees at 1996-12-31.
WHY THEY DIFFER: Most plausibly "full-time" (S-1) vs total employees including part-time/temporary (10-K); the same S-1 paragraph concedes unquantified contractors and temporaries. Drafting-date differences across the S-1/A cycle may also contribute.
EVIDENCE WEIGHT: Neither is wrong; both are Tier 1 for their own definition.
BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: Report 151 full-time employees at 1996-12-31 (S-1) with 158 as the 10-K total, and never average them.
RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: The 10-K's own wording was not read this session (citation inherited from dossier E).

**C-H4 — Bezos's stake: 43% vs 41% and family 10% in both.**

CLAIM B: S-1/A (1997-05-14) same risk factor: ~41% Bezos, 10% family.

RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: Low.

**C-H5 — Reincorporation dated twice (1996-05-28 vs 1996-06-18) and 1995 headcount anchored twice (1995-12-31 vs 1996-01-01).**
WHY THEY DIFFER: Agreement date vs merger-effective date; and a fiscal-year-end vs first-day-of-year employee count for the same value 11.
EVIDENCE WEIGHT: Both statements come from the same registrant; the pair is consistent once read as paper-flow vs closing.
BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: Record the reincorporation as agreed 1996-05-28 and effective 1996-06-18; record 11 employees "at 1995-12-31 (also stated as at 1996-01-01)".
RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: None material.

**C-H6 — Why the name became Amazon (motive), and whether anything prompted it.**
CLAIM A (founder-sourced, later): a lawyer misheard "Cadabra" as "Cadaver" — the Sheff interview, which the restored cache now establishes was **conducted 1999 and published 2000 in Playboy** (cache correction C-1), not a 1994 Wired profile. It is a retrospective founder statement and the project's earliest *first-person* word on the naming.
CLAIM B (reported secondary): a structured naming exercise producing multiple candidate names and several domain registrations, with a deliberate A-initial dictionary search for "biggest."
CLAIM C (this dossier's Tier-1 finding): the filings, the trademark file and the register show **no dispute, opposition, proceeding or third-party objection** in 1994-96, and show the company applying for "AMAZON" on 1995-10-23 and disclosing only "has applied for" the AMAZON.COM marks.
WHY THEY DIFFER: A and B are retrospective motive stories of different authorship; C is the documentary record, which is about effect rather than motive.
EVIDENCE WEIGHT: C for what happened (no conflict is evidenced); A and B are founder/secondary narrative and must be labelled `FOUNDER CLAIM` / `RETROSPECTIVE INTERPRETATION`.
BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: The name change is real in its consequences (instruments filed under the Amazon style from 1995) but undocumented in its timing and motive; no name or trademark dispute existed in Stage 1 on the evidence available, and the "cadaver" anecdote should be carried as founder folklore with its later date attached.
RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: A prior user who never complained would be invisible in this record — see G-H2.

**C-H7 — Was Amazon "regulated"? Company vs environment.**
CLAIM A: The company's own disclosure — "not currently subject to direct regulation by any domestic or foreign governmental agency," plus a warning that Internet applicability of existing tax/libel/privacy law was uncertain.
CLAIM B: The 1996 environment objectively changed around it — Section 230 enacted 1996-02-08; crypto export jurisdiction moved State→Commerce effective 1996-12-30, in rule text that names promoting electronic commerce.
WHY THEY DIFFER: A is a legal-position statement about direct agency regulation; B is general law-making that touched the infrastructure, not the retailer's licences.
EVIDENCE WEIGHT: Both stand; they are not in conflict once "regulation" is defined.
BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: For Stage 1, the correct description is an unlicensed, unregulated remote retailer whose binding constraints were private-ordering (guarantees, shareholder lock-ups, at-will distributor supply) and state tax exposure — with the 1996 federal changes arriving as background at the very end of the window.
RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: Whether any of the 1996 measures was internally discussed: UNKNOWN.
