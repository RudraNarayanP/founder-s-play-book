# AMAZON STAGE 1 DOSSIER — G. ADVERSARIAL REVIEW

Scope: Amazon.com Stage 1 (approx. 1993–1996, with 1997 filings used as retrospective-primary evidence). Method: every challenge below is tied to a document actually retrieved in this session. Tier convention used throughout: **T1** = SEC filings / corporate records / contemporaneous press / archived period pages; **T2** = early interview transcripts or journalism close to the events; **T3** = later books and retrospectives; **T4** = tertiary aggregators (Wikipedia, HistoryLink essays that themselves cite T3); **T5** = uncited internet/social folklore.

Headline structural finding: the single most load-bearing Stage-1 documents are (a) Amazon's own 1997 SEC registration statement and its exhibits, (b) an **October 4, 1995 company press release** preserved on the Wayback Machine, (c) a **July 20, 1997 Los Angeles Times** profile by Jonathan Littman, and (d) a **1999 David Sheff interview** with Bezos. Popular accounts written after 2013 mostly re-tell (b)–(d), and where they add colour (motel names, Excel files, "math error", "youngest VP", "$100M by design") the added colour is T3/T4 only.

## Challenges

G-01 Standard narrative: "Bezos read a statistic that the web was growing 2,300% a year and that made him decide to sell books" — Challenge: the earliest retrievable printed version of the story is the founder telling it to a journalist three years after the event, in a piece that flags it as his own self-narration ("As told by Bezos, the mercurial rise of Amazon.com already has a Horatio Alger ring"); no 1994-dated document ties the number to the decision, and Amazon's IPO filings never mention the statistic at all. — Earliest source of the popular claim: Jonathan Littman, "The Book on Amazon.com," Los Angeles Times, 20 July 1997, https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1997-jul-20-tm-14520-story.html — Founder-originated? yes (reported by journalism; no independent 1994 trace) — Contradicting or qualifying evidence: LA Times, 20 Jul 1997, same URL: "In the spring of 1994, Bezos surfed the World Wide Web for the first time and happened upon a site projecting annual Web growth at an astounding 2,300%." vs. Amazon's original S-1 (filed 24 March 1997), https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1018724/0000891618-97-001309.txt, which describes only "The Company was incorporated in July 1994 and commenced offering products for sale on its Web site in July 1995" with no decision narrative. — Class: founder-originated retrospective — Confidence in the challenge: High — Best-supported reconstruction: Bezos almost certainly did encounter very large early-web growth numbers in 1994 and used the "2,300%" anecdote as his founding myth from at least mid-1997; no contemporary evidence shows the number *caused* the decision.

G-02 Standard narrative: "The 2,300% figure was a real, if startling, growth rate he spotted" — Challenge: the best-documented provenance is a factor-of-increase, not a percentage, so the popular number appears to be a unit error repeated for decades. — Earliest source of the popular claim: Brad Stone, The Everything Store (Back Bay Books, 2013), Chapter 1, excerpted at NBC News 15 Oct 2013, https://www.nbcnews.com/id/wbna53283862 — Founder-originated? yes (Stone reports Bezos's telling; Bezos used it in early speeches) — Contradicting or qualifying evidence: Stone excerpt, same URL: "the number of bytes… transmitted over the Web had increased by a factor of 2,057 between January 1993 and January 1994" and "Bezos interpolated from this that Web activity overall had gone up that year by a factor of roughly 2,300—a 230,000 percent increase… Amazon began with a math error." Attributed underlying document: John S. Quarterman, Matrix News, February 1994 (not retrieved independently; UNKNOWN whether the newsletter itself printed "2,300%"). — Class: memoir-only / later book research — Confidence in the challenge: Medium (the arithmetic critique rests on one T3 source) — Best-supported reconstruction: the anecdote's numeric content is unreliable as stated; the traceable underlying data are 1993–94 byte/packet growth factors published in Matrix News.

G-03 Standard narrative: "He moved fast because the web was compounding at 2,300% a year — weeks were important" — Challenge: the primary record shows a slow Stage 1: no sales for roughly a year after the alleged insight, and near-zero 1994 activity. — Earliest source of the popular claim: LA Times, 20 Jul 1997 (Bezos quoted: "when it's growing 2,300% a year, weeks are important"), https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1997-jul-20-tm-14520-story.html — Founder-originated? yes — Contradicting or qualifying evidence: S-1/A, 14 May 1997, https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1018724/0000891020-97-000839.txt: "Operating expenses in 1994 were minimal." and, separately cited, "For the period from inception through July 1995, the Company had no sales and its operating activities related primarily to the development of the necessary computer infrastructure and initial planning and development of the Amazon.com site and operations." — Class: primary document (contradicting a founder retrospective) — Confidence in the challenge: High — Best-supported reconstruction: urgency was rhetorical; operationally the founding-to-launch gap was ~12–13 months with no revenue.

G-04 Standard narrative: "On the cross-country road trip he wrote down the idea / the business plan" — Challenge: the trip-and-writing story is entirely founder-told; no artifact from the trip has been located, and the earliest retrievable versions differ in what was written. — Earliest source of the popular claim: David Sheff interview with Bezos (interview conducted 1999, published 2000), https://www.davidsheff.com/jeff-bezos — Founder-originated? yes — Contradicting or qualifying evidence: Sheff/Bezos, same URL: "We flew to Fort Worth, where my dad gave us a 1988 Chevy Blazer. I wrote the first draft of the business plan in the car on the way." vs. LA Times, 20 Jul 1997: "while his wife, MacKenzie, drove west—pecked out a business plan on his laptop and punched out calls on his cellular." — Class: founder-originated retrospective (1999) with a 1997 journalistic variant — Confidence in the challenge: Medium (the trip itself is plausible and multi-attested; the "wrote the plan in the car" detail is unverifiable) — Best-supported reconstruction: a 1994 westbound trip with document-drafting is attested from 1997 onward in founder-sourced tellings; nothing dated 1994 substantiates it.

G-05 Standard narrative: "He quit, then drove west writing the plan" — Challenge: the dated primary record compresses the timeline and does not support a leisurely multi-week idea-generation trip before incorporation. — Earliest source of the popular claim: Sheff 1999 interview, https://www.davidsheff.com/jeff-bezos — Founder-originated? yes — Contradicting or qualifying evidence: S-1/A management biography, 14 May 1997: "From December 1990 to June 1994, Mr. Bezos was employed by D.E. Shaw & Co." (last month June 1994); Exhibit 10.12 of the original S-1 (filed 24 Mar 1997), https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1018724/0000891618-97-001309.txt: "subscribes for 1,700,000 shares of the common stock of Cadabra, Inc.… Dated: July 5, 1994" — i.e., subscription weeks after the end of employment. — Class: primary document — Confidence in the challenge: Medium-High — Best-supported reconstruction: departure (June 1994) → incorporation (5 July 1994) is a compressed sequence; either the trip was fast or the incorporation date reflects paperwork completed after arrival, which no retrieved record resolves.

G-06 Standard narrative: "The famous road trip is in the standard timeline" — Challenge: tertiary sources that drop the trip entirely show how unstable the detail is. — Earliest source of the popular claim: Wikipedia, "History of Amazon" (page retrieved this session), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Amazon — Founder-originated? no (editorial synthesis) — Contradicting or qualifying evidence: Wikipedia text: "…Washington, where he began to work on a business plan" (citing an Entrepreneur.com article dated 10 October 2008) — i.e., plan written after arrival, no road trip; contrast Sheff 1999 and LA Times 1997 above. — Class: unsourced internet folklore (tertiary drift) — Confidence in the challenge: High (that the story is not stable) — Best-supported reconstruction: treat the road trip as a founder motif with two competing printed versions (in-car drafting vs. post-arrival drafting), neither contemporaneous.

G-07 Standard narrative: "Amazon was founded in the garage and worked from the garage" — Challenge: no Stage-1 primary document mentions a garage; the company's own first dated commercial premises of record is an industrial sublease effective February 1996, seven months after launch, and its earliest public self-description places "headquarters" in Seattle with no garage. — Earliest source of the popular claim: LA Times, 20 Jul 1997 ("True to high-tech form, Amazon.com opened for business in July 1995 in Bezos' suburban Seattle garage"), https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1997-jul-20-tm-14520-story.html — Founder-originated? yes (journalism relaying founder account; Sheff 1999: "the garage of his rented home") — Contradicting or qualifying evidence: Exhibit 10.31, original S-1, https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1018724/0000891618-97-001309.txt: sublease dated 19 January 1996 for "2250 First Avenue South, Seattle, Washington… Commencement Date… February 1, 1996… $7,200" monthly, notarized by Bezos as "President of Amazon.com Inc."; and the URLwire company announcement of 4 October 1995, https://web.archive.org/web/20120907020813/http://www.urlwire.com/news/100495.html: "Amazon.com operates from headquarters in Seattle, Washington." — Class: primary document vs. founder-originated retrospective — Confidence in the challenge: High for "no primary garage record"; Medium for "the garage story is exaggeration" — Best-supported reconstruction: work in a residential garage/rented house before February 1996 is plausible and repeatedly founder-attested, but is documented only by retrospective telling, while the corporate record shows a modest SODO industrial space as the first address of record.

G-08 Standard narrative: "the Seattle garage" — Challenge: the location of the "garage" is internally contradicted across accounts, including within founder-linked tellings. — Earliest source of the popular claim: LA Times, 20 Jul 1997 ("suburban Seattle garage"); Sheff 1999 has Bezos saying "We flew to Fort Worth… the garage of his rented home" per Sheff's narration and "set up shop in Seattle" — Founder-originated? yes — Contradicting or qualifying evidence: HistoryLink essay 23230 (Jennie Cecil Moore, posted 7 Apr 2025), https://www.historylink.org/File/23230: "Jeff and MacKenzie Bezos set up shop in a three-bedroom rental house in Bellevue in 1994"; Wikipedia "History of Amazon": "operated out of the garage of Bezos's house on Northeast 28th Street in Bellevue, Washington." — Class: unsourced internet folklore on top of conflicting secondary accounts — Confidence in the challenge: High (the contradiction is real) — Best-supported reconstruction: "Seattle garage" is an imprecision that survives in popular tellings; the more specific Bellevue/Northeast 28th Street version is T4/tertiary and should not be treated as verified.

G-09 Standard narrative: "He started with a handful of employees in the garage" ("four employees") — Challenge: the company's own filings imply a larger headcount by/just after launch than the myth allows and never describe a garage workforce. — Earliest source of the popular claim: Sheff interview (published 2000; conducted 1999), https://www.davidsheff.com/jeff-bezos ("set up shop in Seattle in the garage of his rented home, with four employees") — Founder-originated? yes (Sheff's narration of Bezos's account) — Contradicting or qualifying evidence: original S-1, 24 Mar 1997: "From January 1, 1996 to December 31, 1996, the Company expanded from 11 to 151 employees"; S-1/A: "the Company expanded from 11 to 256 employees" (31 Mar 1995 to 31 Mar 1997). — Class: primary document — Confidence in the challenge: Medium — Best-supported reconstruction: 4–11 people is the right order of magnitude for 1995; "four" is a rounded recollection, and the "garage team" framing obscures that staffing was already double digits before the first full year of trading ended.

G-10 Standard narrative: "The garage is the origin symbol of scrappy Amazon" — Challenge: the symbolic framing is traceable to Amazon's own publicity within months of launch, i.e., the myth is a designed asset, not a neutral memory. — Earliest source of the popular claim: URLwire company announcement, 4 October 1995, https://web.archive.org/web/20120907020813/http://www.urlwire.com/news/100495.html — Founder-originated? yes (company-issued) — Contradicting or qualifying evidence: same document: "Amazon.com is the brainchild of Wall Street wunderkind Jeff Bezos." and "The company maintains a staff of programmers, editors, executives and all-around book lovers." — Class: primary document (company publicity) — Confidence in the challenge: High — Best-supported reconstruction: by month four the company was already publishing founder-centric origin publicity; any Stage-1 "origin story" citation should be labelled company messaging.

G-11 Standard narrative: "Door desks and garage frugality prove the scrappy beginning" — Challenge: the door-desk practice is documented, but by 1997 the company leased 42,400 sq ft of offices plus a 50,000 sq ft warehouse, and a 1999 published profile's space figure conflicts with the filing. — Earliest source of the popular claim: LA Times, 20 Jul 1997: "his desk, like those of many of his employees, is an unfinished door with 4-by-4s for legs" — Founder-originated? no (journalistic observation) / employee-retrospective variant via GeekWire 2019 — Contradicting or qualifying evidence: S-1/A "FACILITIES": "approximately 42,400 square feet… in Seattle" plus "approximately 50,000-square-foot facility"; vs. Sheff (publ. 2000): "the company had moved to a 17,000-square-foot office." — Class: contemporary journalism (desk) vs. primary document (space) — Confidence in the challenge: Medium-High — Best-supported reconstruction: frugality symbolism was real at the desk level and simultaneously performative; it coexisted with rapidly growing leased footprint and heavy marketing spend.

G-12 Standard narrative: "Amazon was the first online bookstore" — Challenge: Amazon's own IPO prospectus named contemporaneous online booksellers as competitors, and the phrasing "first" appears in journalism, not in the company's regulatory filings. — Earliest source of the popular claim: LA Times, 20 Jul 1997, which calls it "the world's first electronic bookstore" — Founder-originated? partly (journalism relaying Bezos's framing); the S-1/A instead claims "first mover advantages" — Contradicting or qualifying evidence: S-1/A "COMPETITION": "These competitors include (i) various online booksellers… including Book Stacks Unlimited, Inc., a subsidiary of CUC, and other small entrants… that may be aided by partnering with existing distributors, such as Ingram" — plus Wikipedia "CUC International" listing CUC's acquisition of Book Stacks Unlimited on "September 17, 1996… $4.85MIL". — Class: primary document (refuting) — Confidence in the challenge: High — Best-supported reconstruction: Amazon was not the first web bookstore; its own lawyers told investors otherwise only in softened form ("first mover").

G-13 Standard narrative: "Other early online book sites existed, so Amazon's assortment claim was the real novelty" — Challenge: the assortment claim was a listing claim built substantially on licensed data, and only a fraction of listed titles were actually procurable. — Earliest source of the popular claim: URLwire release, 4 Oct 1995: "currently offers more than one million different titles, 40 times more than typical mall bookstores, and more than 5 times as many as the country's largest book superstores" — Founder-originated? yes (company) — Contradicting or qualifying evidence: S-1/A: "Of the more than 2.5 million titles offered by the Company, up to 400,000 are currently supplied by book distributors and wholesalers" and "The Company licenses some of its catalog and other information from third parties." — Class: primary document qualifying a company claim — Confidence in the challenge: High — Best-supported reconstruction: the differentiator was the assembly and searchability of licensed title data plus a distribution-adjacent fulfilment stack, not ownership of a unique catalogue or inventory.

G-14 Standard narrative: "Customer experience and reliability were the breakthrough" — Challenge: the company's own prospectus conceded multi-week availability windows, and the founder's slogan overstated it. — Earliest source of the popular claim: URLwire release, 4 Oct 1995: "Our motto is 'If it's in print, it's in stock,'" (Bezos quoted) — Founder-originated? yes — Contradicting or qualifying evidence: S-1/A "Availability and Fulfillment": "the remainder of in-print titles are generally available within four to six weeks, although some titles may not be available at all." — Class: primary document contradicting founder slogan — Confidence in the challenge: High — Best-supported reconstruction: the value proposition at launch was selection-on-paper and convenience, with delivery times materially slower than the slogan implied.

G-15 Standard narrative: "The first sale, to a stranger, in July 1995, was Hofstadter's Fluid Concepts and Creative Analogies" — Challenge: this is Amazon's own retrospective company history, first found in an archived corporate-IR timeline, and it conflicts with Bezos's own account of a paid six-week beta among friends and family. — Earliest source of the popular claim: Amazon corporate timeline (company IR page, captured 27 Oct 2007), https://web.archive.org/web/20071027122712/http://phx.corporate-ir.net/phoenix.zhtml?c=176060&p=irol-corporateTimeline: "1995 July / Amazon.com Sells First Book, 'Fluid Concepts & Creative Analogies…'" — Founder-originated? yes (company-authored) — Contradicting or qualifying evidence: Sheff interview, 1999/2000: "Before we opened for business, we did a six-week beta test. About 300 friends and family members ordered real stuff and we charged their credit cards and tested the system." — Class: company-originated retrospective — Confidence in the challenge: High — Best-supported reconstruction: the "first sale" is a curated PR event; real first transactions were insider beta purchases, and the identity of "first customer" cannot be established from retrieved evidence.

G-16 Standard narrative: "We sold books in all 50 states in the first 30 days" — Challenge: the claim is real and early, but it is a self-reported press-release number that was later re-dated ("first month", then "two months") and never independently verified. — Earliest source of the popular claim: URLwire release, 4 Oct 1995, https://web.archive.org/web/20120907020813/http://www.urlwire.com/news/100495.html: "In just its first four weeks of operation, the company shipped books to customers in all 50 states and more than 45 countries." — Founder-originated? yes (company) — Contradicting or qualifying evidence: Seattle Times, 10 Jul 2005, https://www.seattletimes.com/business/amazon-at-10-will-it-keep-clicking/: "The company shipped books to all 50 states and to 45 countries" (framed as "In the first month"); Wikipedia "History of Amazon": "In the first two months of business, Amazon sold to all 50 states and over 45 countries. Within two months, Amazon's sales were up to $20,000 per week." (citing an undated Inc./Josh Spiro piece); S-1/A: 1995 net sales "$511,000" with "International sales represented approximately 39%… in 1995". — Class: primary document (company publicity) with later mutation — Confidence in the challenge: Medium-High — Best-supported reconstruction: the geographic-breadth claim originated as October-1995 company publicity, is arithmetically compatible with the audited $511K year but cannot be verified, and the widely quoted "30 days" phrasing is the earliest (and self-serving) version, not a later embellishment.

G-17 Standard narrative: "Sales hit $20,000 a week within two months — explosive start" — Challenge: measured against the only audited figure, 1995 sales averaged about $21,000/week across the whole launch year, so the anecdote describes an ordinary ramp, not a detonation. — Earliest source of the popular claim: Wikipedia "History of Amazon" quoting Inc./Spiro (undated), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Amazon — Founder-originated? unknown (company-adjacent) — Contradicting or qualifying evidence: S-1/A: "Net sales grew from $511,000 in 1995 to $15.7 million in 1996" and "Average daily visits (not 'hits') have grown from approximately 2,200 in December 1995"; HistoryLink 2025 gives "orders in the first week and $14,000 in the second week". — Class: primary document (qualifying) — Confidence in the challenge: Medium — Best-supported reconstruction: 1995 traction was tiny in dollars and real only in growth rate; the "explosive" framing borrows 1996–97 numbers and projects them back onto the launch.

G-18 Standard narrative: "He walked away from a big hedge-fund bonus" — Challenge: the bonus exists only as an unspecific founder recollection; no retrieved source quantifies it, and the amount varies by telling. — Earliest source of the popular claim: Sheff interview (1999/2000), https://www.davidsheff.com/jeff-bezos: "I was walking away from my Wall Street bonus." — Founder-originated? yes — Contradicting or qualifying evidence: S-1/A biography gives only "From December 1990 to June 1994, Mr. Bezos was employed by D.E. Shaw & Co.… becoming Senior Vice President in 1992"; LA Times, 20 Jul 1997 says "A few weeks after his epiphany, Bezos quit D. E. Shaw" with no compensation figure; Stone (2013) via NBC News has Bezos referring to "my 1994 Wall Street bonus right in the middle of the year at the worst possible time." — Class: founder-originated retrospective — Confidence in the challenge: High on "no verifiable amount"; Low on "no bonus was forgone" — Best-supported reconstruction: an unrealised 1994 bonus is founder-attested and plausible; every dollar figure circulating online is unsupported by anything retrieved here.

G-19 Standard narrative: "David Shaw walked him through Central Park and told him to sleep on it" — Challenge: the iconic scene is T3 book-only (2013); an independent account of the same conversation describes a different substance. — Earliest source of the popular claim: UNKNOWN in retrieved evidence except Stone's The Everything Store (2013) as relayed by HistoryLink's source list, https://www.historylink.org/File/23230 — Founder-originated? yes (attributed to Shaw by Stone) — Contradicting or qualifying evidence: HistoryLink (2025), same URL: "Shaw understood his ambition and desire to strike out on his own, but asked Bezos to consider the well-paying job he currently had, and that DESCO could potentially be a competitor if he chose to leave." — Class: memoir-only — Confidence in the challenge: Medium-High — Best-supported reconstruction: the meeting happened and Shaw tried to talk him out of it; the park-walk staging is a book-era dramatisation.

G-20 Standard narrative: "The 'regret minimization framework' is the documented decision mechanism (and a later invention)" — Challenge: it is neither late-invented nor independent: it is founder-said, and retrievable at least as early as 1999. — Earliest source of the popular claim: Sheff interview (1999/2000): "I call it regret minimization. You project yourself to age 80 and look back on your life." — Founder-originated? yes — Contradicting or qualifying evidence: Stone (2013) via NBC News repeats the same framing; no 1994-dated evidence exists that the framework guided the 1994 choice. — Class: founder-originated retrospective (early instance, 1999) — Confidence in the challenge: Medium — Best-supported reconstruction: the framework is a genuine mid/late-1990s founder formulation used to explain a 1994 decision; it is evidence about Bezos's self-presentation, not about the decision.

G-21 Standard narrative: "The idea was Bezos's alone" — Challenge: the "everything store" concept and an assigned internet-strategy role are attributed to work inside D.E. Shaw, and the same firm spun off other internet ventures in 1994–95. — Earliest source of the popular claim: Stone, The Everything Store (2013), via NBC News excerpt, https://www.nbcnews.com/id/wbna53283862: "Shaw and Bezos discussed another idea as well. They called it 'the everything store.'" and "In early 1994, several prescient business plans emerged from the discussions between Bezos and Shaw… DESCO would develop that idea into a company called Juno… FarSight Financial Services" — Founder-originated? partly (Bezos also tells it solo) — Contradicting or qualifying evidence: HistoryLink (2025): "Shaw was mulling various profitable applications of the burgeoning internet; Bezos was tasked with exploring the ideas and mapping their potential." — Class: memoir-only / later book (with a second secondary source) — Confidence in the challenge: Medium — Best-supported reconstruction: Amazon's founding sat inside a live institutional e-commerce programme; the solo-genius framing is a later simplification, though Bezos's exit and execution were his own.

G-22 Standard narrative: "Investors didn't get it; nobody believed in it" — Challenge: the same founder who reports rejection also reports the money arrived fast and from insiders, and the corporate record shows prominent investors paying sharply rising prices within 18 months. — Earliest source of the popular claim: Bezos on Charlie Rose ("60 Minutes Extra"), reported by GeekWire, 1 Dec 2013, https://www.geekwire.com/2013/jeff-bezos-60-meetings-raise-1m-amazoncom-giving-20-early-investors/: "I had to take 60 meetings to raise $1 million, and I raised it from 22 people at approximately $50,000 a person." — Founder-originated? yes — Contradicting or qualifying evidence: S-1/A "CERTAIN TRANSACTIONS": Kleiner Perkins funds bought 569,396 Series A shares at "$14.05 per share" (agreements dated 21 June 1996) while "Scott D. Cook and Patricia Q. Stonesifer" bought 2,500 Series A shares each at "$40.00 per share"; director "Tom A. Alberg" bought 150,000 common shares in December 1995; GeekWire same URL quotes Bezos: "it was nip and tuck… That was 1995, and the first question every investor asked me was: 'What's the Internet?'" — Class: founder-originated retrospective vs. primary document — Confidence in the challenge: High (both narratives are true; the "nobody believed" one is the incomplete one) — Best-supported reconstruction: a hard-to-explain consumer-web pitch to non-tech angels, offset by fast, competitive, escalating institutional money once metrics appeared.

G-23 Standard narrative: "Amazon was nearly rejected by everyone and only got $8M in May 1996 from Kleiner Perkins" — Challenge: dates and amounts in popular accounts do not match the filing record, which also shows a competing bidder. — Earliest source of the popular claim: UNKNOWN for "May 1996"; LA Times, 20 Jul 1997, gives the earliest print version, with an error: "Venture capital jump-started the company, first $2 million and then $8 million last spring from the vaunted John Doerr" — Founder-originated? no (journalism) — Contradicting or qualifying evidence: S-1/A lists Co-Sale and Voting Agreements "dated June 21, 1996" with KPCB entities; HistoryLink: "Connecticut-based General Atlantic showed interest and offered a $10 million valuation… They ultimately closed a deal granting $8 million for a 13 percent stake in Amazon, which they valued at $60 million." — Class: primary document + T4 — Confidence in the challenge: Medium-High — Best-supported reconstruction: an $8M institutional round closed in June 1996 at roughly a $60M valuation after a competing offer; the seed money was not venture capital, contrary to 1997 press.

G-24 Standard narrative: "His parents invested $245,000 / the family put in about a quarter-million" — Challenge: the figure is derivable from the filing but secondary sources disagree with each other and with the primary math. — Earliest source of the popular claim: UNKNOWN in retrieved evidence for the widely used exact figure; LA Times (1 Aug 2018, Tom Metcalf) is cited by Wikipedia for "almost $245,573" — Founder-originated? no — Contradicting or qualifying evidence: S-1/A "CERTAIN TRANSACTIONS": "In February 1995, the Company sold 582,528 shares of Common Stock to Miguel A. Bezos at a price per share of $0.1717. In July 1995, the Company sold 847,716 shares of Common Stock to the Gise Family Trust" (≈$99,999 + ≈$145,553 ≈ $245,552); HistoryLink states instead: "Then the growing business received a $150,000 infusion from his parents in 1995." — Class: primary document (arithmetic) vs. conflicting secondary figures — Confidence in the challenge: High — Best-supported reconstruction: parental investment of roughly $245.5K across two 1995 share purchases is the best-supported figure; "$150,000" is an unreconciled competing number in circulation.

G-25 Standard narrative: "He risked everything he had" — Challenge: he risked $10,000 of his own money plus $84,000 of his own loans to the company, then was repaid; the framing of total personal risk is not what the record shows. — Earliest source of the popular claim: Sheff 1999/2000 and Bezos's 2013 Charlie Rose remarks ("The riskiest moment… was at the very, very beginning") — Founder-originated? yes — Contradicting or qualifying evidence: S-1 Exhibit 10.12: "subscribes for 1,700,000 shares of the common stock of Cadabra, Inc.… the sum of $10,000," dated 5 July 1994; S-1/A: "Mr. Bezos made interest-free loans to the Company in the principal amounts of $15,000, $29,000 and $40,000 in July 1994, November 1994 and November 1995, respectively, which were fully repaid." — Class: primary document — Confidence in the challenge: High — Best-supported reconstruction: personal capital at risk was modest in absolute terms; the scarce resource was outside equity, not Bezos's savings.

G-26 Standard narrative: "Amazon was the $100M-by-design 'everything store' from the start" — Challenge: no retrieved Stage-1 document contains a $100M target, and the first employee retains projections that were far lower; the only early-1994 "everything store" language is DESCO talk reported in 2013. — Earliest source of the popular claim: UNKNOWN (the specific $100M/2004–$2.4B/2015 formulation could not be traced to any retrieved source) — Founder-originated? unknown — Contradicting or qualifying evidence: HistoryLink (2025) quoting Shel Kaphan (2011): "I have spreadsheets of his projections from when he was trying to hire me… it was a lot, lot smaller than it turned out to be"; S-1/A strategy language never states a revenue goal and only notes "An increasingly broad base of products is being sold successfully online, including computers, travel services, brokerage services, automobiles and music." — Class: unsourced internet folklore vs. primary document — Confidence in the challenge: High for the numbers, Medium for the concept — Best-supported reconstruction: broad-catalogue ambition is genuinely present in 1994–97 thinking (DESCO conversations; Bezos's own category list of 20; LA Times 1997 whiteboard "music, cars, computers, real estate"), but the quantified "$100M by design" is untraceable and probably folklore.

G-27 Standard narrative: "Breadth of selection was documented as company strategy in Stage-1 materials" — Challenge: partly true, but the filings frame expansion as market context and category-adjacency (music/video/CDs), not as a plan to sell everything; the explicit cross-category ambition surfaces in company-adjacent tellings later. — Earliest source of the popular claim: S-1/A, 14 May 1997 (company's own words) — Founder-originated? yes (company document) — Contradicting or qualifying evidence: S-1/A: "The Company also sells a smaller number of CDs, videotapes and audiotapes"; LA Times 1997 (reporting a Bezos job-interview topic): "affinity marketing" plus the journalist's note: "I scrawl down a few of the couple dozen words on his white board: music, cars, computers, real estate." — Class: primary document + contemporary journalism — Confidence in the challenge: Low-Medium (this cuts mostly to *how* strong the claim is, not whether it existed) — Best-supported reconstruction: category breadth was an active Stage-1 idea, contemporaneously observable, but not a written master plan.

G-28 Standard narrative: "Cash was never a problem / or conversely it nearly died" — Challenge: the near-run-out story is founder-and-journalism sourced, but the primary record supports the substance: founder loans, personal guarantees, and a $21,600 security deposit on a $7,200/month sublease. — Earliest source of the popular claim: Seattle Times, 10 Jul 2005: "Amazon was almost out of money when it received a $1 million infusion from mostly private investors." — Founder-originated? yes (Bezos's 2013 Charlie Rose "nip and tuck") — Contradicting or qualifying evidence: S-1/A: "From November 1994 to December 1996, Mr. Bezos personally guaranteed the obligations of the Company under a merchant account with Seafirst Bank… Since April 1995, Mr. Bezos has personally guaranteed company credit cards."; Exhibit 10.31 (19 Jan 1996): "shall deposit Twenty-One Thousand Six Hundred and No/100 Dollars ($21,600.00) to be held as the 'Security Deposit.'" — Class: primary document corroborating a retrospective claim — Confidence in the challenge: Low (the challenge here is to sourcing, not to the fact) — Best-supported reconstruction: Stage-1 liquidity was genuinely thin and credit-weak, which the standard narrative under-weights.

G-29 Standard narrative: "The model was brilliant from day one" — Challenge: the disclosed Stage-1 economics were structurally weak: ~20% gross margin, marketing at 39% of sales, and dependence on two distributors with no contracts. — Earliest source of the popular claim: retrospective business-press mythmaking (S-1/A itself already claimed "first mover advantages and momentum") — Founder-originated? yes (for "brilliance"); the numbers are company-disclosed — Contradicting or qualifying evidence: S-1/A: "The Company's gross profit margin was approximately 20% of net sales in 1995 and approximately 22% of net sales in 1996"; "Marketing and sales expenses as a percentage of net sales were 39% in each of 1995 and 1996"; "The Company has no long-term contracts or arrangements with any of its vendors that guarantee the availability of merchandise." — Class: primary document — Confidence in the challenge: High — Best-supported reconstruction: Stage-1 advantage was execution and demand capture, not unit economics, which were worse than a mall bookseller's on a marketing-adjusted basis.

G-30 Standard narrative: "Early customers were delighted; service was the moat" — Challenge: a contemporary test by a period journalist found a rival faster at the same price, and the company's own account conceded week-plus delivery. — Earliest source of the popular claim: URLwire release, 4 Oct 1995 ("shopping experience that would be impossible without the Internet") — Founder-originated? yes — Contradicting or qualifying evidence: LA Times, 20 Jul 1997: "$25.42 from Amazon.com, $25.41 from Barnes & Noble… Electronic bookselling is only significantly cheaper if you're buying a book that's not discounted in stores," with the B&N order "delivered it on Saturday" against Amazon's which "trudged in Monday afternoon"; HistoryLink: "It could take a week or longer to receive the book." — Class: contemporary journalism + T4 — Confidence in the challenge: High — Best-supported reconstruction: delight was relative to a small early-adopter baseline; on price and speed the 1997 evidence shows parity or loss to incumbent online entrants.

G-31 Standard narrative: "The press got it early / was supportive" — Challenge: period coverage carried explicit doubt and mockery alongside enthusiasm, and included a claim that Amazon's early base was narrow. — Earliest source of the popular claim: n/a (this challenge is to the "inevitable" framing) — Founder-originated? no — Contradicting or qualifying evidence: LA Times, 20 Jul 1997: "depending upon whom you believe, is either the next light bulb or another Edsel"; "So far, Amazon.com has built a large portion of its business on selling nerd books to nerds"; "as of March, had lost $9 million real dollars to generate sales of $32 million"; and Sheff (1999/2000) reports Forrester's George Colony "dubbed the company Amazon.toast." — Class: contemporary journalism — Confidence in the challenge: High — Best-supported reconstruction: Stage-1 media coverage was mixed and instrumental; Publishers Weekly's technology editor told the LA Times that paid advertising, not web chatter, was the differentiator: "What Amazon.com did differently was to take out newspaper ads and start spreading money around."

G-32 Standard narrative: "Customer reviews made the site feel like a community" — Challenge: the first reviews were written by staff and friends, and contemporaneous reporting already noted the absence of any verification. — Earliest source of the popular claim: URLwire release, 4 Oct 1995: "Amazon.com allows readers to share their thoughts on particular books" — Founder-originated? yes (company) — Contradicting or qualifying evidence: HistoryLink (2025): "employees and friends wrote the first posted reviews"; LA Times, 20 Jul 1997: "You could be a personal friend or an enemy of the author and weigh in without ever having read the book in question; nobody cared and nobody seemed to be checking." — Class: T4 + contemporary journalism vs. company claim — Confidence in the challenge: High — Best-supported reconstruction: the review feature was seeded inauthentically at launch and unmoderated, which is a design fact the celebratory telling suppresses.

G-33 Standard narrative: "Traction came from the idea and the product" — Challenge: documented traction drivers were discovery and distribution mechanics plus paid promotion: alphabetical position in web directories, directory features, list servers/Usenet, a 300-person insider beta, banner advertising, newspaper ads, and the Associates affiliate programme. — Earliest source of the popular claim: Sheff (1999/2000) is the earliest retrieved source for the founder's own account of these drivers — Founder-originated? yes — Contradicting or qualifying evidence: Sheff: "Because the name started with the letter A, it was at the top of the 'What's Cool' page"; "Some professor somewhere maintained a mailing list that went out to 50,000 people."; S-1/A: "The Company places advertisements on various high-profile and high-traffic conduit Web sites, including CNET, Yahoo!, Pointcast, Excite, Lycos, Quote.com and CNN"; "the Company has been featured in… the 'What's New' and 'What's Cool' sections of Netscape and Yahoo!"; HistoryLink: "It was a priority for the name to start with the letter 'A' because the first web directories listed sites alphabetically." — Class: primary document + founder retrospective — Confidence in the challenge: High — Best-supported reconstruction: Amazon's early traffic was substantially manufactured by directory placement, bought advertising, affiliate distribution and a pre-existing personal network; the "idea" explains little of the curve.

G-34 Standard narrative: "He chose Seattle for its talent and logistics" — Challenge: two founder accounts give different reasons, and a third (tax arbitrage) is documented in period journalism and later books; the sales-tax motive is the one with a legal anchor. — Earliest source of the popular claim: Sheff (1999/2000): "The two primary factors… near a major book warehouse and near a pool of technical talent. Seattle had both." — Founder-originated? yes (two incompatible versions) — Contradicting or qualifying evidence: LA Times, 20 Jul 1997: "Sales taxes dictated a small state. California and its 28 million book buyers were out"; Stone (2013): "Bezos later learned of a 1992 Supreme Court decision that upheld a previous ruling that merchants did not have to collect sales tax in states where they did not have physical operations"; the decision is Quill Corp. v. North Dakota, 504 U.S. 298 (1992), https://www.law.cornell.edu/background/internet/Quill.htm — Class: contemporary journalism vs. founder retrospective — Confidence in the challenge: High — Best-supported reconstruction: location was primarily a tax-and-distributor arbitrage decision with a talent-pool overlay; Bezos's later "talent and warehouse" version downplays the arbitrage.

G-35 Standard narrative: "The asset-light per-order buying model was a clever innovation" — Challenge: it was a dependency with documented operational costs, including wholesaler minimum-order rules that had to be gamed, and a supplier that became a competitor. — Earliest source of the popular claim: S-1/A, 1997 (company's own description: "The Company carries minimal inventory") — Founder-originated? yes — Contradicting or qualifying evidence: Sheff (1999/2000), Bezos on the ten-book minimum: "But we found an obscure book about lichens that they had in their system but was out of stock. We began ordering the one book we wanted and nine copies of the lichen book."; S-1/A: "Ingram is the single largest supplier and accounted for 59% of the Company's inventory purchases in 1996."; LA Times, 20 Jul 1997: "Ingram recently signed up Crown Books… and cbs.sportsline.com to sell books online." — Class: founder retrospective + primary document — Confidence in the challenge: Medium-High — Best-supported reconstruction: the model was a workaround-driven adaptation to distributor terms, and its main weakness was strategic dependence on the same wholesaler that armed rivals.

G-36 Standard narrative: "Legal and channel fights came later, after scale" — Challenge: litigation and channel hostility are already in the Stage-1/early-Stage-2 record, including a false-advertising suit attacking the very origin claims. — Earliest source of the popular claim: n/a (challenge to omission) — Founder-originated? no — Contradicting or qualifying evidence: S-1/A "LEGAL PROCEEDINGS": "On May 12, 1997, B&N filed suit against the Company… alleging that the Company has made false advertising claims because it is not a 'bookstore' and does not physically warehouse most of the titles it offers," and quoting a Dow Jones release that "a 'syndicate source characterized the lawsuit as petty and spiteful.'" — Class: primary document — Confidence in the challenge: High — Best-supported reconstruction: the "Earth's Biggest Bookstore" positioning was contested as misleading while the garage-and-vision narrative was being built, and Amazon also received a B&N trademark claim letter in January 1997.

G-37 Standard narrative: "Amazon's 1996 story is that it was offering an unbeatable catalogue at unbeatable prices" — Challenge: the company's own dated claims move across years in ways that suggest marketing arithmetic rather than a fixed discovery: 1,000,000 titles (Oct 1995), 1.1 million (Jan 1997), 2.5 million (Apr–May 1997). — Earliest source of the popular claim: URLwire release, 4 Oct 1995 ("more than one million different titles") — Founder-originated? yes — Contradicting or qualifying evidence: Amazon press release, "Amazon.com Announces 1996 Bestsellers," 9 Jan 1997, https://press.aboutamazon.com/1997/1/amazon-com-announces-1996-bestsellers: "the earth's biggest bookstore and the leading online bookseller" with "catalog of 1.1 million titles"; vs. S-1/A (May 1997): "The Company offers more than 2.5 million titles." — Class: primary documents in tension — Confidence in the challenge: Medium — Best-supported reconstruction: the catalogue number is a marketing metric whose growth reflects licensed-data additions (including an out-of-print service launched only in March 1997), not a standing inventory of the kind the slogan implied.

G-38 Standard narrative: "The novelty was per-order wholesaler buying plus data plus experience — one of these was the real trick" — Challenge: what the record actually supports is that no single element was unique; the filing's own candid rationale is demographic. — Earliest source of the popular claim: n/a — Founder-originated? no — Contradicting or qualifying evidence: S-1/A: "the demographic overlap between frequent book buyers and Internet users is high"; corroborated by LA Times, 20 Jul 1997 ("a lot of the 'books' Amazon.com sells are pseudo manuals for programmers"). — Class: primary document — Confidence in the challenge: Medium-High — Best-supported reconstruction: Amazon's Stage-1 edge was being first at scale in the one retail category whose buyers most overlapped with 1995's web population, executed with bought distribution — not a proprietary mechanism.

## Claims that survived the challenge

1. **Founding date, original name and founder's $10,000**: T1 corporate records. Exhibit 10.12 of the original S-1 (subscription for 1,700,000 Cadabra, Inc. shares for $10,000, dated 5 July 1994) plus S-1/A ("incorporated in July 1994"; "purchased 10,200,000 shares of Common Stock for an aggregate price of $10,000"). Nothing contradicts this.
2. **Launch month and first trading year**: T1. Original S-1 (24 Mar 1997), https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1018724/0000891618-97-001309.txt: "commenced offering products for sale on its Web site in July 1995"; S-1/A (14 May 1997): "Net sales grew from $511,000 in 1995 to $15.7 million in 1996." Contemporaneous company publicity (4 Oct 1995) and the July 1997 LA Times agree on July 1995; 16 July 1995 (HistoryLink, Guardian 2014, Seattle Times 2005) is consistent, though the day itself is T3/T4-sourced.
3. **"All 50 states / 45+ countries in roughly the first month"**: survives as a *contemporaneous company claim* (URLwire, 4 Oct 1995), not as an independently verified fact. Its late mutations to "two months" weaken it, but the "30 days" version is not an invention of later tellers.
4. **Cadabra-to-Amazon name change with the "cadaver" anecdote**: T2 founder-sourced as early as the 1999 Sheff interview ("I called my lawyer… He said, 'Cadaver?' I knew that would be a bad name right away") and T1 for the Cadabra name itself. The "because a lawyer misheard" motive is founder-only but early.
5. **The road trip as a real 1994 event**: multi-attested (LA Times 1997; Sheff 1999) — the *trip* survives; only the "wrote the plan in the car" detail and its dramatised set-pieces remain unverifiable.
6. **Door desks / visible frugality as a Stage-1 practice**: contemporary observation (LA Times, 20 Jul 1997) plus employee recollection (GeekWire, 2019) — survives, though the "Home Depot origin" narration is retrospective.
7. **A $8M Kleiner Perkins round in mid-1996 that put John Doerr on the board**: T1 (Series A at $14.05; agreements dated 21 June 1996) plus T4 (valuation $60M/13%). The "nobody would fund it" framing collapses here.
8. **Deliberate loss-taking as stated strategy, in Bezos's own words at the time**: T1 (S-1/A Amendment No. 4, Amazon.com, Inc., filed 9 May 1997, accession 0000891020-97-000755, https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1018724/000089102097000755/; the sentence also occurs verbatim in the S-1/A of 14 May 1997, https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1018724/0000891020-97-000839.txt, and in the original S-1 of 24 March 1997, https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1018724/0000891618-97-001309.txt): "The Company also intends to offer attractive pricing programs, which will reduce its gross margins. Because the Company has relatively low product gross margins, achieving profitability given planned investment levels depends upon the Company's ability to generate and sustain substantially increased revenue levels.") and period press quoting him in the New York Times in 1997 via HistoryLink: "We are not profitable. We could be… It would also be the dumbest."
9. **Time named Amazon one of the 10 "Best Websites of 1996"**: stated in the company's own S-1/A — a rare externally published, contemporaneous third-party validation with T1 provenance.
10. **Amazon was not first**: this negative claim is strongly supported (S-1/A naming Book Stacks Unlimited; NCSA Mosaic "What's New" August 1995 listing multiple commercial web book sites; LA Times 1997 noting "Several other Web bookstores exist").

## Narrative notes

- **Two narratives, both founder-authored.** Stage 1 supplies us with an *origin* story (statistic → trip → garage → first sale → disbelief) and a *traction* story (word of mouth → vision). The first is mostly post-1997 founder retrospection amplified by 2013 book research; the second is contradicted by the company's own IPO filing, which lists paid banner advertising on Yahoo!/CNET/Excite/Lycos/Pointcast/Quote.com/CNN, print advertising in the New York Times Book Review and Wired, feature placement in Netscape's "What's New" and Yahoo!'s "What's Cool," a several-thousand-member affiliate programme, and "an ongoing public relations campaign." Amazon's disclosed marketing spend (39% of net sales in both 1995 and 1996) is the quantitative fingerprint of that.
- **The October 1995 press release is the Rosetta stone of the corpus.** Most "amazing factoids" about Amazon's first months (50 states in four weeks, 45 countries, one million titles, 10–40% discounts, "If it's in print, it's in stock," "brainchild of Wall Street wunderkind Jeff Bezos") first appear as company publicity, not as journalism or audited data. The dataset should record their origin as *advertising copy with a date*, which is both more interesting and more defensible than repeating them as history.
- **The filing record is unusually candid** for a Stage-1 company: it concedes 20–22% gross margins, marketing at 39% of sales, a 59% single-supplier dependence with no contracts, four-to-six-week title availability, "no long-term contracts… that guarantee the availability of merchandise," a founder who personally guaranteed merchant accounts and credit cards and lent the company $84,000 interest-free, and reliance on "first mover advantages." That candour is the best available antidote to the myth, and it comes from the company's own lawyers.
- **Bezos's own retellings shift in ways that are diagnostic, not damning.** 1997: he "happened upon a site"; 1999: a "report" whose "methodology" he checked; 2013 (via Stone): a newsletter chart that actually showed a *factor* of ~2,300. Likewise the location rationale moves from sales-tax arbitrage (1997) to "talent plus book warehouse" (1999). These are a founder optimising a story for each audience; the dataset should log variants side-by-side rather than reconciling them.
- **Omitted actors.** MacKenzie Scott's Stage-1 roles (cheque-writing, bookkeeping, a freight contract, packing orders) and Shel Kaphan's (hired 1994, described by peers as a co-founder; retains the founder's own small projections) are documented only in T4/tertiary sources quoting earlier interviews; Peter Laventhol (Shaw's first employee) introduced Bezos to his first engineer, and David Shaw's own e-commerce programme produced Juno and FarSight. The "solo founder in a garage" frame is cheap to dismantle and no one appears to have done it in the standard tellings.
- **Almost-partnership.** HistoryLink reports that once press coverage drew Barnes & Noble's attention, the two companies met to discuss possible co-operation before the 1997 trademark and false-advertising fight. If a T1/T2 source surfaces (Publishers Weekly, Apr 1997 is cited in that essay's media list), that single thread would rewrite Stage 2's competitive narrative; currently it rests on a T4 essay citing a T3 book.
