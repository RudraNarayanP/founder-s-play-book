## Knowability split

Rule applied: an item is KNOWABLE only if a 1994-observable artifact, a dated filing, or a contemporaneous record shows the information existed and was reachable by someone in his position. Later analysis that merely proves something was true then is NOT automatically knowable; it is classified accordingly.

### KNOWABLE in 1994 (evidenced)
1. The growth rate of the Web, from a named newsletter he actually read — John Quarterman's Matrix News, February 1994, charting Web bytes up by a factor of 2,057 and packets up by 2,560 between January 1993 and January 1994 (B-84; stone.txt:190). Knowable; note that the "2,300 percent" he then derived is his own interpolation and is wrong by two orders of magnitude (B-83).
2. That mail-order and catalogue retailing could not carry book selection — the in-print catalogue being unmail-able at scale, "larger than 50 New York City phone books" (B-10), and that the largest superstores stocked on the order of 170,000 titles against millions in print (B-11, B-103).
3. The structure of book distribution — two primary wholesalers, Ingram and Baker & Taylor, so a new retailer would not have to face thousands of publishers individually (B-103), and the wholesaler minimum-order practice that made single-copy drop shipping impractical (B-20).
4. That online bookselling already existed — Book Stacks Unlimited (Cleveland), WordsWorth (Cambridge), Future Fantasy Books (Palo Alto), Internet Shopping Network — and he and Charles Ardai placed test orders to measure it, one purchase record surviving (B-102, B-8).
5. A tax arbitrage with a date and a citation — the 1992 Supreme Court decision holding that merchants need not collect sales tax in states where they have no physical operations, which is what moved him away from Santa Cruz and California (B-74, B-46, and stone.txt:210).
6. The supply of technical talent in the Seattle area — Microsoft's presence and the University of Washington computer-science pipeline (B-12, B-46).
7. His own employer's parallel work — DESCO was already incubating internet ventures (Juno, FarSight) with Shaw chairing both, so the direction of travel was visible from the inside and the ownership problem was visible too (B-86, B-87).
8. A binding constraint on his recruiting — his DESCO contract barred him from hiring DESCO people for two years, which he honored (B-91).
9. The legal-infrastructure immaturity of the market — his own statement that commerce over the Internet was prohibited in spring 1994 with a pending plan to lift it (B-3). Classified CONTEMPORARY OBSERVATION retrospectively reported: it is a period condition he describes, not a later inference.
10. The incumbent shakeout reshaping book retail — the superstores' scale and 40% gross margins against a would-be online entrant's ~20% (B-132), and the fact that Barnes & Noble and Borders had already taken over the physical space while independents disappeared, which a 1997 reporter documented as a live industry transformation (B-77, B-80).

### NOT KNOWABLE in 1994
1. Whether consumers would buy anything online at all. Still an open question in a national newspaper in July 1997 — "Nobody really knows whether large numbers of consumers will take to shopping by computer" — three years after launch, with IBM's Web mall already dead (B-81). If unknowable in 1997 by print, it was unknowable in 1994.
2. Whether the growth rate would persist. The 2,057x/2,560x figures are one-year deltas off a tiny base (B-84); nothing in the record shows a 1994 forecast of duration, only of level.
3. Whether his wholesalers would stay neutral. Ingram supplying 59% of titles in 1997 while beginning to enable other online sellers was a live dependency, not a knowable future (B-80).
4. Whether "one million titles" was deliverable as a customer promise. It was a homepage slogan (B-49) and a catalogue-data claim, not demonstrated fulfillment capability, and the audited 1995 cost of sales shows a thin reseller margin, not an inventory advantage (B-132).
5. Whether incumbents would enter, and when. Barnes & Noble went online in May 1997 and sued over the slogan (B-30, LAT); no 1994 record anticipates this.
6. His own revenue path. He produced revenue projections on the drive west that Stone records as "radically inaccurate," and Kaphan, shown the hiring spreadsheets, says the numbers were "a lot, lot smaller than it turned out to be" (B-93, B-44). Two independent witnesses confirm that his own 1994 model was wrong in the direction of underestimating.
7. Whether a two-person-plus-friends payroll could service orders, or whether the wholesaler workarounds would scale (B-20, B-128).
8. Whether the "A-listing" advantage in alphabetical directories would survive the arrival of search (B-17, B-49).
9. Whether the e-book/downloading threat to paper was timed anywhere within his horizon; in 1999 he put it as "eventually," gated by display quality and piracy (Sheff).
10. Whether family money would arrive, and on what terms he could have counted on it — his own 1997 telling is that at the moment of the move he "had no idea... how he would fund his scheme" (B-73).

## Narrative notes

**What the corpus actually is.** Four lineages, not dozens. (i) Bezos's own telling, at four dates: LA Times July 1997, Playboy interview conducted 1999/published 2000, Academy of Achievement May 2001, Princeton 2010, Business Insider 2016. (ii) Brad Stone's 2013 book, which is the only on-disk source that interviews the parents, Kaphan, Ardai, Lovejoy and MacKenzie. (iii) SEC filings of 1997-98, which are the only documents written under penalty while the founding was recent memory — and which say almost nothing about the founding. (iv) HistoryLink 2025, which is a careful tertiary essay that footnotes (ii). Every popular claim about the "garage, the $10,000, the parents' $250,000, the 2,300 percent, the blind-date criteria, the regret framework" traces to Bezos himself, restated later. The dossier's real content is therefore the divergence between his tellings, and the places where a filing contradicts all of them.

**The single most important correction to the brief.** The file described as "the single most valuable founder-state document" is not contemporaneous. Sheff's interview was conducted in 1999 and published in 2000 (B-01). Everything in it about 1993-95 is post-outcome testimony from the most valuable founder alive at the time. It remains the richest source of his own words — but its class is FOUNDER CLAIM, not CONTEMPORARY OBSERVATION, and it has been labelled that way in every record drawn from it.

**The Stage-1 state, as the record supports it, without adjectives.** A 30-then-31-year-old senior vice president of a quantitative investment firm, paid well enough to be offered a mid-year bonus he knew he would forfeit, living in an Upper West Side apartment with his wife, a 1992 Princeton English graduate who worked in the same office; he had been placed by his own boss in charge of scouting internet applications, had already watched two such ventures be born inside the firm and owned by it, and concluded that ownership required exit. He incorporated on July 5, 1994 with one other participant in the paperwork, himself, as sole stockholder. For the next six months the company spent $52,000 total and sold nothing. For the next twelve it sold nothing. He recruited one senior engineer, from outside his own firm because his firm's contract barred him from recruiting inside it, and paid him with options dated October 1994. His wife drove with him, and later wrote cheques and kept books. Audited 1995, the launch year, recorded $511,000 of sales, $406,000 of operating expense, and no interest expense at all. There is no evidence in any source consulted that he predicted any of what followed; two witnesses specifically record that he did not (B-44, B-93).

**Where the money question stands.** What is primary: family members and family trusts held ~10% of post-IPO stock; he personally held 48.3% pre-offering; he drew $64,333 in 1996 with no options; the company had one stockholder in September 1994; the company recorded no interest expense in 1994 or 1995; a confidentially-treated subrogation agreement between him and the company is dated June 19, 1996; Kleiner Perkins held ~16.7% by early 1997 under a June 21, 1996 agreement. What is not primary: every dollar figure for the family round, the $10,000 of his own money, the $84,000 in loans, the $150,000 (or "almost $250,000") parental infusion, and the $1.1m/22-investor/$50,000-each angel round. The founder's own versions quantify the parental contribution only as "a large fraction of their life savings" (2001) and "a significant portion" (2016).

## Contradictions

### C-1 — The parental money: primary block versus invented number
- CLAIM A: Bezos's parents invested $150,000 in 1995 (HistoryLink, citing Stone p.33), restated as "almost $250,000" (Wikipedia, citing an LA Times analysis of August 1, 2018).
- CLAIM B: The SEC filings state no amount at all; they disclose only that family members and family trusts held ~10% of post-IPO stock, and Bezos himself, in 2001 and again in 2016, quantified it only as "a large fraction"/"a significant portion" of their life savings.
- WHY THEY DIFFER: The dollar figures are journalist reconstructions — one from a book, one reverse-engineered from later share counts — while the founder never supplied a number and the filings never had to. Different precision norms; the press figures also differ from each other by two-thirds.
- EVIDENCE WEIGHT: The 10% family block is Tier 1 and dispositive that family capital was real and material. Every dollar figure is Tier 3-of-Tier-2 or a 2018 inference.
- BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: Family money existed, was large relative to a salaried Exxon-engineer household's savings, and took the form of equity or equity-linked paper held partly in trusts. The specific figure is not established and should be reported as untraced.
- RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: Whether the parents' paper was the same instrument reported as "$84,000 in loans," whether it was ever a loan at all, and how much of the 10% block belongs to whom.

### C-2 — Whose consent, and from when
- CLAIM A: MacKenzie told him to go ("MacKenzie... told me I should go for it," Princeton 2010; "count me in 100 percent," Achievement 2001).
- CLAIM B: In the 1999/2000 Sheff interview, asked directly whether she was pleased to come, he answers only "Yes," and no words of hers are reported at all.
- WHY THEY DIFFER: Both are the same speaker; the quoted-dialogue versions postdate 2000, when the founding story had become a motivational genre and he was telling it at graduations.
- EVIDENCE WEIGHT: No 1994 statement by her exists in any source. The earliest her own voice appears in anything on disk is Stone's 2013 "MacKenzie later said it was she who targeted Bezos."
- BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: She consented and went; the scripted dialogue supporting the "supportive wife" beat is founder-originated and accretes over time.
- RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: Whether she had any documented reservation, and what her own contemporaneous position was on the forfeited income.

### C-3 — The David Shaw conversation
- CLAIM A (Sheff 1999/2000): Shaw said it "might be a good idea for somebody who didn't already have a good job," asked for 48 hours of thought, and Bezos "went away to be alone."
- CLAIM B (Stone 2013): they walked two hours; Shaw sympathized because he had done the same leaving Morgan Stanley, noted DESCO's growth and that Bezos already had a great job, warned the firm might compete, and they agreed on "a few days."
- WHY THEY DIFFER: One is a compressed soundbite the subject chose for a magazine; the other is a reconstructed scene from a book interviewer talking to both sides.
- EVIDENCE WEIGHT: HistoryLink independently carries the competition warning, so CLAIM B's substance has two carriers; the "48 hours" has one.
- BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: A discouragement-plus-competitive-threat conversation, with the duration unspecified. The "48 hours" is a founder-supplied detail that later reporting repeated.
- RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: Whether any of this conversation happened in the form either version gives; no 1994 note exists.

### C-4 — Solo insight versus employer-sanctioned research
- CLAIM A: He found the growth statistic himself while working a normal job, then acted on individual initiative (Sheff; LAT's "surfed the Web for the first time and happened upon a site").
- CLAIM B: Shaw put him in charge of DESCO's Internet effort; they met weekly; Juno and FarSight came out of it; the "everything store" was a shared DESCO concept, and DESCO owned the internet ventures he helped design (Stone, with multiple DESCO witnesses; HistoryLink's "tasked with exploring the ideas").
- WHY THEY DIFFER: A founder-originated myth of the lone discovery serves the ownership narrative; the corporate-incubation version explains why he had to leave at all — which his own stated motive (B-88) confirms.
- EVIDENCE WEIGHT: CLAIM B, on multiplicity of witnesses and on internal consistency with the equity motive.
- BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: He was the designated in-house explorer of internet commerce at a firm already building two internet companies; his contribution was the retail-application judgment and the decision to exit for ownership, not privileged access to a fact nobody else had.
- RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: How much of the books-specific analysis was his alone; Stone credits him with the research.

### C-5 — Seattle versus Bellevue, and "rented" versus "his"
- CLAIM A: Sheff's narration and several later accounts place the start in Seattle; the encyclopedia calls the Bellevue house "Bezos's house."
- CLAIM B: HistoryLink, its photo caption, and the encyclopedia's own body text place the 1994 workspace in a three-bedroom rented house in Bellevue, with the move to SODO, Seattle coming months later.
- WHY THEY DIFFER: "Seattle" is the branding answer to "where was Amazon founded"; the municipal boundary was immaterial at the time and material to a sales-tax analysis that turned on physical presence.
- EVIDENCE WEIGHT: CLAIM B, three-fold, including a 2025 photo caption of the actual house.
- BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: Founded in Bellevue; relocated to Seattle SODO before the July 1995 launch. Neither appears in the filings, which give no address for the founding period.
- RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: Whether the garage or the house interior was the primary workspace, and whether the office predated the launch move.

### C-6 — Launch date
- CLAIM A: Sheff/Bezos: the email went out July 15; "we sent e-mail to those 300."
- CLAIM B: HistoryLink and its sources: July 16, 1995 official launch, $12,000 in the first week; the company media kit says only "1995 July"; the S-1 says "July 1995."
- WHY THEY DIFFER: One cites the notification email, the other the opening of public sales.
- EVIDENCE WEIGHT: Neither is contradicted by a filing; the filings deliberately avoid the day.
- BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: Launch fell in mid-July 1995, probably the 15th or 16th, and the distinction has no evidentiary consequence.
- RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: Unresolvable from what is on disk.

### C-7 — Founding year, 1993 versus 1994
- CLAIM A: The brief's scope begins in 1993, and some chronologies date the idea or the founding to late 1993.
- CLAIM B: The S-1 dates incorporation to July 1994 (July 5 in the financial-statement header), the 10-K says he founded the company in 1994, the company's own media kit starts at "1994 July," and even Stone's exit from DESCO is June 1994.
- WHY THEY DIFFER: A 1993 start is inferred from "he was thinking about it in 1993," which no document on disk supports. Note that one Stone footnote quotes a DESCO-era line from November 15, 1993 about "one stop shopping" — which is evidence of the concept circulating at his employer, not of Amazon existing.
- EVIDENCE WEIGHT: Overwhelmingly 1994, and primary.
- BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: Stage 1 opens in mid-1994. Anything dated 1993 belongs to his employment at D. E. Shaw, and the dossier's earliest founder-state evidence is therefore the 1993-94 DESCO work, not an Amazon.
- RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: Whether he began preliminary research in late 1993.

### C-8 — The 1986-88 gap, Fitel, Bankers Trust and "youngest" anything
- CLAIM A: Sheff's narrator and the 2001 Achievement bio: Fitel first, as "associate director of technology and development," then Bankers Trust, then DESCO. Bezos himself in Sheff never names Fitel, describing only "a company with 11 people" doing post-trade clearing that "wasn't a great success."
- CLAIM B: The 10-K bio: Bankers Trust from April 1988, DESCO from December 1990 — no first employer at all, and "Interscience" appears in none of the sources consulted, including the filings, Sheff, Stone, HistoryLink and the Achievement bio.
- WHY THEY DIFFER: A securities bio compresses to the two most impressive employers; press accounts supplied a title no filing confirms; "Interscience" is likely a confusion with the 1994 Cadabra incorporation or with Fitel's successor entity.
- EVIDENCE WEIGHT: Filing dates are authoritative for 1988-94. The 1986-88 window is attested by three non-filing sources agreeing on Fitel.
- BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: 1986-88 Fitel, 1988-90 Bankers Trust (VP from Feb 1990), 1990-94 DESCO (SVP from 1992). "Interscience" should be recorded as NOT DOCUMENTED.
- RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: His actual title at Fitel; whether Fitel failed around him.

### C-9 / C-16 — The drive west, three tellings
- CLAIM A (Sheff 1999/2000): his father gave them a 1988 Chevy Blazer in Fort Worth and "I wrote the first draft of the business plan in the car."
- CLAIM B (LAT 1997): MacKenzie drove west while he "pecked out a business plan on his laptop and punched out calls on his cellular," with a detour to interview VP candidates in Northern California.
- CLAIM C (Stone 2013): they borrowed the Blazer, he sat in the passenger seat typing revenue projections into an Excel spreadsheet — "numbers that would later prove to be radically inaccurate" — and MacKenzie drove.
- WHY THEY DIFFER: Same underlying event, restated for three audiences across sixteen years, with the artifact escalating from "business plan" (a document that would have implied more preparation) to "spreadsheet" (which is what the audited 1994 result of $52,000 spent and nothing sold actually resembles).
- EVIDENCE WEIGHT: The 1997 version is earliest; the 2013 version is most granular and is the one that concedes the projections were wrong. "Gave" versus "borrowed" matters: borrowing is the less favorable and better-corroborated verb.
- BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: A borrowed family Blazer, MacKenzie driving, and him working numbers on a laptop en route. Nothing supports a completed business plan produced on that drive.
- RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: Whether a plan draft existed at all before the move; the S-1 says the first six months were "initial planning," which is consistent with improvisation.

### C-10 — Deliberate strategist versus no plan at all
- CLAIM A: Two-factor site-selection method (warehouse, talent) plus sales-tax calculation plus a pre-drive recruiting trip — i.e., a sequence of decisions.
- CLAIM B: "He still had no idea where he was going to live or how he would fund his scheme"; the moving van was told to head west and was given Seattle by phone the next day (LAT 1997; independently in Stone 2013 and echoed by the blind-heading story).
- WHY THEY DIFFER: The rational-compressor version is the retrospective reconstruction of a decision that was, in the telling he gave a reporter in 1997, made late and under time pressure ("when it's growing 2,300% a year, weeks are important").
- EVIDENCE WEIGHT: Two independent tellings preserve the head-first version, and it is the less flattering one, which raises its weight.
- BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: Criteria existed (tax, talent, distribution) and were applied after the fact of leaving; the departure itself preceded the resolution of both location and funding.
- RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: How much was performance for a reporter who asked, and how much was true.

### C-11 — Which warehouse
- CLAIM A: He chose Seattle partly to be near a major book warehouse/distributor (Sheff).
- CLAIM B: The LA Times has him choosing Seattle for high-tech workers and for being six hours from Roseburg, Oregon, "a major book-distribution center" — i.e., the distribution advantage lay elsewhere and Seattle was the talent and tax answer.
- WHY THEY DIFFER: Either he adjusted the rationale, or the reporter conflated. Note both agree on Ingram/Baker-Taylor centrality (B-103).
- EVIDENCE WEIGHT: Thin; both are 1994-97 tellings of the same decision by the same informant.
- BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: Talent and taxes drove the choice; distribution access was satisfiable from the region and later rationalized as a Seattle factor.
- RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: High. Not resolvable without a 1994 document.

### C-12 — "Youngest senior vice president"
- CLAIM A: Widely repeated, and present in the 1997 LA Times ("its youngest senior vice president") and in Sheff-era press.
- CLAIM B: The 10-K says only that he became Senior Vice President in 1992, with no superlative; the S-1 says nothing; Sheff's own answers never claim it; Stone's narrator writes "one of the youngest vice presidents at the firm."
- WHY THEY DIFFER: A superlative attaches to titles easily and travels; the firm's own filing does not need it and does not use it.
- EVIDENCE WEIGHT: Title and year are Tier 1. "Youngest" is Tier 2 press, softened to "one of the youngest" in the Tier 2 book.
- BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: He was a young SVP promoted within roughly two years of joining; "the youngest" is not established and, when used, must be labelled press.
- RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: DESCO's officer roster is not in any source consulted.

### C-13 — 2,300 percent versus a factor of 2,300
- CLAIM A: Bezos, at every date from 1997 to 2016: "web usage was growing at 2,300 percent a year," cited as the fact that started Amazon.
- CLAIM B: Stone, naming the source document: the February 1994 Matrix News showed bytes up 2,057-fold and packets up 2,560-fold in a single year; he interpolated "roughly 2,300" as a factor, i.e., 230,000%, and Stone states plainly that "Amazon began with a math error."
- WHY THEY DIFFER: One number is repeated because it is quotable; the other is the underlying data. A "2,300 percent" growth claim is 23-fold; the charts were ~2,000-2,500-fold.
- EVIDENCE WEIGHT: Stone's identification of a checkable named publication with specific figures, plus the arithmetic.
- BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: The founding-era observation is real — Web traffic did grow by orders of magnitude in 1993-94 — but the number he has always used is wrong, and its wrongness has been reproduced by every downstream source including HistoryLink and Time.
- RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: Only Matrix News itself can settle what he read; it is not on disk.

### C-14 — When he first used the Internet
- CLAIM A: "In the spring of 1994, Bezos surfed the World Wide Web for the first time" (LAT 1997, from his own account to that reporter).
- CLAIM B: He first encountered the Internet in a Princeton astrophysics class in 1985, and DESCO staff used Gopher, Usenet, e-mail and Mosaic daily, with DESCO registering deshaw.com in 1992 (Stone).
- WHY THEY DIFFER: The 1994 "first time" makes the discovery sudden and the convert improbable; the 1985/DESCO version makes him a long-term insider of a network his employer was already exploiting.
- EVIDENCE WEIGHT: CLAIM B, on institutional detail and multiplicity.
- BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: He was not a web novice in 1994, but the Web specifically — as opposed to Gopher and Usenet — was new to him commercially.
- RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: His actual hands-on Mosaic/web experience before spring 1994.

### C-15 — Why "Amazon"
- CLAIM A: A lawyer misheard "Cadabra" as "Cadaver" (his answer to Sheff, repeated in the encyclopedia's founding section).
- CLAIM B: A structured naming session — Holden filling a notebook page with alternatives, MakeItSo.com his actual favourite — plus a deliberate dictionary search for an A-word and multiple domain registrations (Stone; HistoryLink).
- WHY THEY DIFFER: The mishearing is an anecdote; the list is a process. Both can be true in sequence, and the anecdote's function is to make the name accidental rather than engineered.
- EVIDENCE WEIGHT: Both are founder-sourced; Stone's version has a second living participant.
- BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: He had candidate names, liked Star Trek references, and the Cadaver episode explains why the A-word had to be found in a dictionary.
- RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: Whether the lawyer episode happened when he says it did relative to the naming list.

### C-17 — Who invented regret minimization, and when
- CLAIM A: He devised it on the spot in 1994, walking in Central Park, as an isolated decision procedure.
- CLAIM B: Stone dates the mental posture to an ongoing joint project — he and MacKenzie were videotaping oral histories of her relatives and had just interviewed her eighty-year-old grandfather, "so looking back on life's important junctures was on Bezos's mind."
- WHY THEY DIFFER: Attribution of a framework to a lone moment is better branding than attribution to a shared domestic activity with his wife's family.
- EVIDENCE WEIGHT: Single-source, but the only source that interrogates the moment.
- BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: The tool is founder-authored and Stage-1-dated, but its immediate stimulus was collective and marital. Treat the framework as a real 1994 decision device whose origin story has been solo-ed.
- RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: Whether the phrase existed in 1994 at all; its earliest print appearance on disk is the 1999/2000 Sheff interview and the December 27, 1999 Time profile.

### C-18 — Where he grew up and whose ranch it was
- CLAIM A: Houston, Texas, with summers at his maternal grandfather's ranch at Cotulla (Sheff's Bezos answer and the 1997 LA Times, which adds "from the age of 4 to 16").
- CLAIM B: Albuquerque to age four, Houston, then Miami for high school, with ranch summers "near San Antonio" (Academy of Achievement bio, 2001).
- WHY THEY DIFFER: Two autobiographies of the same childhood, one told to a magazine in 1999, one assembled by the Achievement interviewers; Cotulla is south of San Antonio, so the "near San Antonio" gloss is an imprecision rather than a different place, but Miami is not in any other account.
- EVIDENCE WEIGHT: The 1997 and 1999/2000 sources agree on Houston; the high-school location is unresolved.
- BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: Born New Mexico, raised principally Houston, with Texas ranch summers at a maternal-grandfather property around Cotulla; treat the Miami claim as single-sourced.
- RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: Which schools he attended and where he was when he built the Radio Shack kit and took the astrophysics class he credits with his first Internet contact.

### C-19 — The risk appetite he claims to have had
- CLAIM A: 2001: "I knew that if I failed I wouldn't regret that, but I knew the one thing I might regret is not ever having tried" — a statement that prices failure at zero.
- CLAIM B: 1999/2000: the framework as a regrets-count minimizer with no failure-tolerance clause, plus his insistence in the same interview that founding straight out of school is "a losing gamble" — an explicitly loss-averse founder narrative.
- CLAIM C: Behavior in the record: he forfeited a mid-year bonus, accepted $64,333 with no options, waited out a non-compete before hiring anyone, ran a six-month company on $52,000, refused to recruit a capable subordinate who had debt, and asked his first outside investors for only ~$50,000 each.
- WHY THEY DIFFER: The 2001 clause is added after failure had become implausible; the earlier version is hedged.
- EVIDENCE WEIGHT: CLAIM C — actions documented in filings — outweighs all verbal versions.
- BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: His Stage-1 risk posture was bounded and staged, not cavalier: the exposure he accepted was a known, recoverable pay cut plus a year of near-zero spend, not ruin.
- RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: Whether he had personal savings or family obligations that made that boundedness possible; neither is in the record.

### C-20 — Round sizes
- CLAIM A: Angels at 22 x $50,000 = $1.1m (HistoryLink); "first $2 million and then $8 million last spring from... John Doerr" (LAT 1997).
- CLAIM B: The filings prove only that Kleiner Perkins vehicles held 16.7% by February 1997 under a June 21, 1996 Investor Rights Agreement, and that the LA Times' "$2m then $8m" ordering does not fit the KPCB date.
- WHY THEY DIFFER: Press round-size reporting in 1996-97 was notoriously approximate; HistoryLink's angel total is derived from Bezos's own count of investors multiplied by a per-check figure he did not supply.
- EVIDENCE WEIGHT: The filing dates and Doerr's share count.
- BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: Institutional money arrived mid-1996 and its size is not established by anything on disk; the angel round's existence and roughly-20% dilution are moderately supported, the totals are not.
- RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: Complete; the preferred-stock capital history that would settle this is not printed in the S-1 text on disk.
