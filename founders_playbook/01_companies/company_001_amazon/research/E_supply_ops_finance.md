## Narrative notes

### N1. Reconstructing ONE Stage-1 order, end to end

The S-1 is the only document that describes the pipeline as the company itself characterised it. Stitching the disclosed steps together, and separating each step by evidence strength:

1. **Capture.** Customer searches a licensed bibliographic catalog by author, title, subject, keyword, publication date or ISBN (E-81, E-42), adds items to a "virtual shopping basket", and clicks buy, supplying "shipping and credit card details, either by e-mail or by telephone" (E-39). Telephone ordering matters at Stage 1: the company ran "a toll-free line for customers who are reluctant to enter their credit card numbers through the Web site" — so part of Stage-1 order volume was captured by hand, off-platform, at 11 employees.
2. **Confirmation.** The system "automatically confirms each order by e-mail to the customer within minutes" (E-39). Automated; cheap; the S-1 emphasises "negligible incremental cost".
3. **Card authorisation.** Internal systems "manage the process of accepting, authorizing and charging customer credit cards" (E-40-equivalent, Business—Technology), through merchant accounts at Seafirst and then Wells Fargo, both personally guaranteed by Bezos (E-25). Reject handling is not described. UNKNOWN.
4. **Title location and routing.** Proprietary software sorts the order: items fillable through "electronic interfaces with vendors" go straight out to a distributor; the rest is "forwarded... to its special order group" staffed by "dedicated ordering personnel that specialize in sourcing hard-to-find books" (E-19).
5. **Inbound leg.** Under distributor arrangements, electronically ordered books "often are shipped by the distributor within hours of receipt of an order from Amazon.com" (E-20). Bezos's 1999 recollection is that ordered books "would show up the next day on our loading dock" (E-62). Either way, Amazon did not own the book when the customer ordered it.
6. **Consolidation and pack.** Everything was routed through one building: "The Company currently processes all sales through its warehouse in Seattle" (E-21). At Stage 1, packing is attested only retrospectively — "we would do our normal jobs and then go down in the basement and pack and ship books" (E-61), and HistoryLink's "purchase the book from a distributor, repackaged it, and then shipped it to the customer" (E-77). Who performed the step at 11 headcount: everyone.
7. **Outbound leg.** "via UPS or Airborne Express" (E-73), with customers choosing among "overnight and various international shipping options, as well as gift-wrapping", and choosing single or split shipments by availability (E-73, S-1 Summary).
8. **Notification.** E-mail "shortly after orders are shipped" (E-39). Note the revenue-recognition consequence: the account records the sale at shipment, not at order (E-35).

**Actual elapsed time to the customer.** The honest answer is a band, because the S-1 discloses processing lead times but never delivery lead times: best case immediate-shipment titles (S-1: "available for immediate shipment"), through "48 to 72 hours" for the next tier, to "generally available within four to six weeks" for the remainder of in-print titles, and "two to six months" for out-of-print — with the explicit admission that in the last two tiers "some titles may not be available at all" (E-41). The only dated first-hand benchmark anywhere in the corpus is NOT an Amazon order at all: Stone's reported 1994 competitor test where a $6.04 book took two weeks and arrived shredded (E-68). Treat Stage-1 elapsed time as **four days to six weeks for an in-print order that could be sourced, with a non-trivial tail that could never be filled** — and note that this band is from the 1997 filing describing the 1996-97 operation; no 1995 order-timing measurement exists in these sources.

### N2. What made "millions of titles" possible without stock

Three disclosures interlock and should be read together, because the marketing claim is often quoted without them. (a) The catalog data was **licensed from third parties** (E-42), not created by Amazon. (b) Only **up to 400,000** of the offered titles were then actually supplied by distributors (E-18), against an industry-wide distributor carrying capacity of "up to 350,000 of the best-selling titles" (E-57). (c) Amazon **bought on demand** rather than to stock (E-44). So the advertised number is a *catalog* count, not an *availability* count — and at launch the company's own October 1995 press release claimed only "more than one million" titles (E-71), which grew to the 2.5 million quoted in 1997. The gap between advertised and physically orderable was therefore real from the first month, and the S-1's "some titles may not be available at all" is the company conceding it in a signed filing.

### N3. Documented bottlenecks and failures, Stage 1

Five are on the primary record, and it is worth noting how mundane they are: (i) **unfillable demand** — the availability tiers that end in "may not be available at all" (E-41); (ii) **special-order price risk** pushed back onto the customer, since Amazon had to phone or mail a customer to "seek approval for sale at the higher price" (E-43) — a manual step on a partially manual order channel; (iii) **no buying power and no contract protection** with either supplier (E-17), while the same supplier was arming competitors (E-80); (iv) **manual bookkeeping** — the transaction systems were "not integrated with the remainder of the Company's accounting and financial systems", requiring "a significant amount of manual effort", which the company itself said "may make it difficult for management to obtain accurate financial statements and reporting information on a timely basis" (E-22); (v) **single-site fragility** — periodic interruptions, no redundancy, no disaster recovery plan, insufficient business-interruption insurance, all hardware in one Seattle building (E-52). Also relevant: the FY1995 cash-flow narrative names "increases in inventories" as a use of cash during a supposed zero-inventory year (E-11) — Amazon carried stock from the start.

### N4. Premises and equipment trajectory

There is no Stage-1 lease disclosure in the S-1; the filing describes only the 1997 position: ~42,400 sq ft of administrative/engineering/marketing/customer-service space (lease expiring 31 July 1999) plus an ~50,000 sq ft warehouse and merchandising facility (lease expiring 31 October 1999), all in Seattle, with no owned real estate. The audited Note 2 shows the entire plant at 31 December 1995 was **$81,000 gross, $57,000 net** — $73,000 of computers and equipment and $8,000 of purchased software, and **zero leasehold improvements**, which is itself evidence that as late as the 1995 year-end Amazon occupied leased space it had not built out. The 1996 jump is the real step-change: gross equipment $1,295,000 and $130,000 of leasehold improvements, with $1.2M of investing outflow in FY1996 versus $52,000 in FY1995. Headcount tracks the same break: **11 employees at 31 December 1995** (E-31), 158 at 31 December 1996, 256 at 31 March 1997.

### N5. Burn and runway, as evidenced

FY1995 is the only year in this window for which the S-1 permits a genuine runway calculation, and it is uncomfortably thin: cash at 31 December 1995 was $996,000 against $232,000 of operating cash burn and $52,000 of investing outflow, i.e. **$284,000 of total cash consumed in 1995** (DERIVED), implying roughly **3.5 years of runway at the 1995 rate** (DERIVED: 996/284) if the rate had held. It did not hold: FY1996 operating burn was $1,735,000 and investing $1,214,000, so **$2,949,000 consumed** (DERIVED from the audited cash-flow lines), against the $8.0M that arrived in June 1996.

The 1995 cash bridge ties exactly and is worth setting out, because it is the whole of Stage-1 money in six numbers: opening cash $52,000; operating $(232,000); investing $(52,000); financing +$1,228,000, being $1,272,000 of common-equity proceeds and advances LESS the $44,000 repayment of Bezos's loans; net increase $944,000; closing cash **$996,000**. There was no other source and no other use. Amazon therefore funded its first six trading months entirely out of private common-stock sales to individuals, with zero bank debt outstanding at year end, $0 of long-term obligations, ~$17,000 of inventory, $57,000 of net equipment, 11 people, and $83,000 of payables growth.

The correct Stage-1 statement is not "Amazon was running out of money" — on the audited cash it plainly was not, and it ended 1995 with nearly $1M in the bank and no borrowings. The correct statement is that **FY1995's burn was suppressed**: revenue recognition on shipment, an un-built-out facility ($0 leasehold improvements), no leasehold spend, 11 employees, $30,000 of advertising, and a company that had only been a C-corporation since 31 March 1995 with $107,000 of cumulative losses still being reclassified out of the tax accounts. Any runway estimate for Stage 1 built on the 1996 run-rate is a category error.

### N7. What traces to primary evidence, and what does not — Stage-1 money

**Traces to the S-1 read directly (Tier 1, primary):** Bezos's $10,000 for 10,200,000 shares in July 1994; the three interest-free founder loans of $15,000 / $29,000 / $40,000 and their repayment dates, reconciling to the audited $44,000 notes-payable draw and $44,000 repayment; the $295,568 of named related-party common purchases in February 1995, July 1995 and December 1995 at $0.1717 and $0.3333; the $1,272,000 and $60,000 common-equity cash totals for 1995 and 1994; the $8,000,014 / 569,396-share June 1996 Kleiner Perkins Series A at $14.05; the $200,000 January/February 1997 Cook-Stonesifer tranche at $40.00; every audited 1995 and 1996 revenue, cost, margin, expense, balance-sheet and cash-flow figure in this file; Bezos's personal guarantees on the Seafirst and Wells Fargo merchant accounts and the company credit cards; the FY1996 salary of $64,333; and the ~10% family-and-trusts ownership block at IPO.

**Does NOT trace to any primary document examined:** the round's status as a single event; the $1.1 million headline as a "friends and family round" total (only the order of magnitude survives, via the $1,272,000 audited line); the number of participants, the $50,000 cheque size, the identity of the residual ~$976,432 of purchasers, any $5 million valuation or 20% equity split; any post-money valuation for the Kleiner Perkins round, which the S-1 never states; Bezos's 1994 and 1995 salary; the size of the exposure under his personal guarantees; any bank line of credit; distributor discount rates, payment terms or credit limits; all weekly and monthly sales curves; the 400- or 200-square-foot warehouse; the D.E. Shaw foregone-bonus figure as anything other than the founder's own later recollection; and any D.E. Shaw or David Shaw investment in Amazon, of which there is no trace at all in the financing disclosure.

## Contradictions

**C-1 — When Amazon existed as a going concern.**
CLAIM A: the company was "founded" and incorporated in July 1994, and Bezos was publicly describing an operating "Amazon Bookstore" to David Sheff by 1994.
CLAIM B: the S-1 states zero net sales from inception (1994-07-05) to 1994-12-31 and that the store "commenced offering products for sale on its Web site in July 1995".
WHY THEY DIFFER: A conflates legal existence and publicity with commercial operation; the company existed, built, and talked for a year before it sold anything. Note also the correction at E-SRC-A: the Sheff piece is 1999, not 1994, so it cannot anchor 1994 publicity at all.
EVIDENCE WEIGHT: B is audited and twice-stated in a signed filing; A is a press profile plus a legal-formation fact.
BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: **incorporated and building from 1994-07-05; selling from 1995-07.** Use 1994 for decisions and staffing, 1995 for anything revenue-shaped.
RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: whether any pre-July-1995 transactions occurred off-book; the audited statements assert none, and nothing contradicts that.

**C-2 — How many titles Amazon advertised.**
CLAIM A: ">1,000,000 different titles" (Amazon's own press release, 1995-10-03).
CLAIM B: "more than 2.5 million titles" (S-1, 1997-05).
WHY THEY DIFFER: not a conflict at all once dated — it is 21 months of catalog growth. The real issue is that both numbers were quoted against a distributor-suppliable reality of at most ~400,000 titles, so in BOTH years the advertised figure outran the physically orderable figure by a wide margin.
EVIDENCE WEIGHT: both Tier 1, both the company's own statements, each authoritative for its date.
BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: Stage-1 advertised selection = **>1 million**; 2.5 million is a 1997 number and must never be used for Stage 1.
RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: the exact 1995-12-31 catalog count is not disclosed; it lay somewhere between 1M and 2.5M.

**C-3 — Stage-1 sales.**
CLAIM A: weekly first-run sales of $12,000 (week one) and $14,000 (week two) (HistoryLink, Tier 3).
CLAIM B: audited FY1995 net sales of $511,000 (S-1).
WHY THEY DIFFER: three ways at once. (i) Basis: "returns" is an order-taken measure, whereas the S-1 recognises revenue when products are **shipped** (E-35), so late-December orders sit in 1996. (ii) Period: the HistoryLink weeks are undated and were almost certainly the launch weeks, not a run-rate. (iii) Metric: net-of-returns versus gross-of-discount.
EVIDENCE WEIGHT: B is audited by Ernst & Young; A is a tertiary local-history page with no cited primary.
BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: use **$511,000 for CY1995** and treat the weekly figures as unfalsifiable colour. Note that $12K+$14K over the first two weeks is not arithmetically hostile to $511K over roughly 26 weeks — the mismatch is about basis and dating, not magnitude.
RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: high — without an order count or a monthly series, no reconciliation is possible.

**C-4 — The friends-and-family round.**
CLAIM A: "$1.1 million from 22 people at $50,000 each"; frequently accompanied by "for 20% of the company at a $5 million valuation".
CLAIM B: the S-1 records only (i) three named related-party common purchases totalling ~$295,568 (DERIVED: 100,020+145,553+49,995), (ii) an FY1995 financing inflow of $1.2M "primarily from the issuance of Common Stock and the exercise of stock options", and (iii) no round total, no participant count, no valuation, and no percentage sold.
WHY THEY DIFFER: A is memoir/press compressed into a single event; B is a filing that reports cash received and named insiders and has no reason to bundle the rest.
EVIDENCE WEIGHT: B for anything numerical; A survives only as an order-of-magnitude corroboration.
BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: **approximately $1.2M of common-equity cash reached Amazon during calendar 1995, of which about $296K traces to three named insiders.** The 22-person/$50K/$5M-valuation packaging is NOT primary-evidenced and should be tagged memoir in this dataset. Also note that the Kleiner Perkins $8.0M arrived in **June 1996**, a year later and a different investor class — collapsing the two into "the first $1 million raise" is the most common error in this space.
RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: the identity and count of the residual ~$0.9M of purchasers; option-exercise proceeds are commingled with issuance proceeds in the $1.2M and cannot be split.

**C-5 — Share prices.**
CLAIM A: $0.1717 and $0.3333 per share (Certain Transactions).
CLAIM B: $0.172 and $0.333 per share (Note 3 Common Stock).
WHY THEY DIFFER: rounding convention in the notes versus precision in the related-party disclosure.
EVIDENCE WEIGHT: A is the more precise figure and is the one tied to share counts, so it is arithmetically usable.
BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: use **$0.1717 / $0.3333** for all derived dollar amounts; flag that any total built from $0.172 drifts (e.g. 582,528 x $0.172 = $100,195 vs $100,020).
RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: none material.

**C-6 — Why Seattle.**
CLAIM A: Bezos, 1999 — "I wanted to be near a major book warehouse and near a pool of technical talent. Seattle had both."
CLAIM B: Stone, 2013 — the driver was sales-tax nexus; Bezos dropped Santa Cruz after learning of the 1992 Supreme Court Quill decision and avoided populous states.
WHY THEY DIFFER: both are retrospective, both are plausibly complete, and neither is contemporaneous. A is a founder optimising his own origin story after $22 billion of outcome knowledge; B is a journalist's reconstruction from interviews with Kaphan and others.
EVIDENCE WEIGHT: neither is Tier 1 for 1994. The S-1 supplies the only primary corroboration that tax geography was real — Amazon collected no sales tax outside Washington (E-58) — but it says nothing about why the office was where it was.
BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: treat the site decision as **multi-causal and unproven in its weighting**; the tax point is independently corroborated as a condition, the talent/warehouse point as a claim.
RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: high. No 1994 document in this corpus discusses location.

**C-7 — Premises size during the garage/office period.**
CLAIM A: Bezos, 1999 — a basement, then "a real office with a 400-square-foot warehouse... about the size of a one-car garage".
CLAIM B: HistoryLink — a "200-square-foot warehouse" in Seattle's SODO district.
CLAIM C: S-1, 1997 — ~42,400 sq ft administrative plus ~50,000 sq ft warehouse, all leased in Seattle, no owned real estate.
WHY THEY DIFFER: A and B are two unfenced recollections of a space nobody measured and that was not material enough to appear in any filing; C describes a different, later facility.
EVIDENCE WEIGHT: C is the only one under securities law. A is first-person but self-serving in its smallness.
BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: **premises square footage during Stage 1 is UNKNOWN**; what IS evidenced is that at 31 December 1995 the company had $0 of leasehold improvements on the balance sheet (E-32), i.e. it had still not built out occupied space — a genuinely independent check that the operation was still physically small at year-end 1995.
RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: high on the number, low on the conclusion.

**C-8 — Inbound speed from distributors.**
CLAIM A: S-1 — books "often are shipped by the distributor within hours of receipt of an order from Amazon.com".
CLAIM B: Bezos, 1999 — books "would show up the next day on our loading dock".
WHY THEY DIFFER: they measure different legs. A is the distributor's despatch time from its own facility; B is Amazon's receipt time at Seattle. "Within hours" of despatch and "next day" of arrival are consistent.
EVIDENCE WEIGHT: A is the filing and is hedged with "often"; B is memory.
BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: **distributor despatch in hours, physical arrival next-day for stocked titles** — and note that both statements describe only the ~400,000 distributor-held titles, not the special-order tail.
RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: whether either applied in July 1995 at 11 employees; the "within hours" language is a 1996-97 description.

**C-9 — The size of the universe Amazon was indexing.**
CLAIM A: Stone — "three million books in print worldwide".
CLAIM B: S-1 — "an estimated 1.5 million English-language books believed to be in print", plus "more than one million out-of-print titles believed likely to be in circulation", and "approximately 50,000 publishers" in Books In Print.
WHY THEY DIFFER: different universes (worldwide/all languages vs English-language in print) and different confidence levels; the S-1 hedges with "estimated" and "believed", Stone does not.
EVIDENCE WEIGHT: B, which is the diligence-vetted figure.
BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: for Stage-1 reasoning about selection depth, use **~1.5M English-language in print**, and note that Amazon's own 1995 launch claim of ">1M titles" was therefore roughly the whole in-print English-language universe, not a boast about magic.
RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: moderate — both are third-party estimates (Euromonitor/Books In Print) restated by the company.

**C-10 — "2,300% Web growth".**
CLAIM A: Bezos's repeated public figure, and the LA Times' "annual Web growth at an astounding 2,300%" (1997), is what triggered the founding decision.
CLAIM B: Stone reconstructs the underlying charts — bytes up 2,057-fold, packets up 2,560-fold over twelve months — and states flatly: "Amazon began with a math error."
WHY THEY DIFFER: A is the number the founder used; B is what the source document actually said, conflating a cumulative multiple with a percentage growth rate.
EVIDENCE WEIGHT: B is a journalistic reconstruction of a specific February 1994 newsletter; A is a founder refrain that appears in at least two independent later tellings, which are not independent sources.
BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: record the **2,300% as a FOUNDER CLAIM of unstated provenance**; the defensible statement is that a 1993-94 Web traffic series exhibiting roughly 2,000-fold annual growth convinced him the medium was inflecting. Do not carry 2,300% into the metrics table as a fact.
RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: the Quarterman newsletter itself has not been examined.

**C-11 — The Cook/Stonesifer preferred purchase and the June 1996 round.**
CLAIM A: reading Certain Transactions alone, Cook and Stonesifer bought Series A at $40.00 in what appears to be the same 1996-06 sentence as the Kleiner Perkins money, and a naive extraction would book $8.2M as the June 1996 round.
CLAIM B: Note 3 dates that tranche to "January and February 1997... to each of two new directors", the director biographies independently date Cook from January 1997 and Stonesifer from February 1997, and Note 3 fixes the June 1996 issuance at 569,396 shares = ~$8.0M.
WHY THEY DIFFER: a disclosure-drafting artefact — Certain Transactions groups by transaction type, not by date.
EVIDENCE WEIGHT: B, triangulated from three separate places in the same filing.
BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: **June 1996 = $8.0M, Kleiner Perkins only.** The $200K at $40.00 is 1997 and is outside Stage 1. This also means the $40.00 preferred price is a 1997 datapoint and must not be used as a 1996 valuation marker.
RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: none.

**C-12 — Reincorporation date.**
CLAIM A: S-1 body — "reincorporated in Delaware in June 1996".
CLAIM B: Note 3 — "On May 28, 1996, the Company reincorporated in the state of Delaware".
WHY THEY DIFFER: rounding to the month in the narrative versus the exact effective date in the audited note.
EVIDENCE WEIGHT: B (audited, specific).
BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: **1996-05-28**.
RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: none material.

**C-13 — Whether Amazon held inventory at Stage 1.**
CLAIM A: "The Company carries minimal inventory"; the model is described as ordering on customer demand (E-44, E-15).
CLAIM B: FY1995 cash flow was negatively affected by "increases in inventories" — audited at exactly $17,000 for FY1995 and $0 for the 1994 stub — with a further $554,000 build in FY1996; Note 1 sets an inventory valuation policy of lower of average cost or market; the 1997 outlook contemplates "commit[ting] to... stock inventories" at new warehouses.
WHY THEY DIFFER: A is a marketing-adjacent relative claim (minimal versus a bookstore's stock); B is an absolute accounting fact (there was inventory, and it grew).
EVIDENCE WEIGHT: both are the same filing; B is the cash-flow statement and is the harder evidence.
BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: **Amazon was a buy-on-order business that nonetheless carried inventory from its first trading year — but so little of it that the entire FY1995 position was about $17,000, roughly 3.3% of that year's net sales.** The "no inventory" myth is unsupported, and so is any suggestion that Stage-1 Amazon was inventory-intensive.
RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: the ~$17,000 balance is DERIVED from cumulative period movements rather than lifted from a balance-sheet line, so it excludes any reclassification; the FY1995 inventory figure was also expressly noted by the company as being subject to "certain reclassifications to conform to the current-year presentation" (Note 1, Reclassifications).
