## Narrative notes

**What can be stated plainly, and what cannot.** The primary record (S-1 1997-03-24; 10-K 1998-03-30) describes Amazon's Stage-1-era system *functionally*, never *technically*. It names what the software did — accept and validate orders, organize and place orders with suppliers, assign product to orders, manage shipment, accept/authorize/charge cards, drive automated e-mail, run searches across title/subject/author/keyword/publication-date/ISBN, split fillable from non-fillable orders at the vendor interface — and it names its neighbors: two ISPs (UUNet, Interconnected Associates) over multiple dedicated T1 lines, one leased Seattle facility, third-party-licensed encryption, licensed catalog data, Ingram and Baker & Taylor as the two major vendors. It never once names a programming language, an operating system, a web-server product, a database product, or a machine type. Those fields are UNKNOWN and stay UNKNOWN on this evidence.

**Who wrote the first code.** Two claims survive contact with the record, and they are not the same claim. The S-1 — Tier 1, sworn — dates Sheldon J. Kaphan's start to October 1994 as VP of Research and Development, making him the earliest documented technical employee by a wide margin, and the only engineer whose Stage-1 start date is audited. Bezos's own account, in a 1999 interview published in 2000, is that *he* "wrote the software for the bookselling operation as the rented furniture was being delivered." HistoryLink's 2025 essay, quoting Kaphan in 2011, says Kaphan "began building and designing a rudimentary website" and "was Amazon's first employee," with some in the industry calling him a co-founder. All three statements can be partly true — a founder who prototyped in the garage and a hired VP of R&D who designed the actual site are not mutually exclusive — but nothing on file adjudicates it. The defensible Stage-1 statement is: **Kaphan is the only engineer with a filing-documented Stage-1 start date; Bezos's authorship claim is founder-recalled and uncorroborated by any contemporaneous artifact.**

**The 1995 site as customers met it.** This dossier cannot describe the launch home page from artifact. The one archived Amazon page on file (`wb_amazon.html`) is a 22 May 2006 capture, not a Stage-1 one, and the Internet Archive holds **no capture of amazon.com's root before 12 December 1998** — confirmed by CDX query and by following the widely-circulated "May 1996" timestamp, which silently redirects to the 2006 page. That null is itself a finding: a large fraction of published "Amazon's first website" claims rest on an artifact that does not exist. What we can say about the launch page comes from the S-1's functional description plus two secondary retellings: a search-driven catalog (not a browsed shelf), a shopping basket, a buy button, a password-based account on a "secure server," a "one million titles" claim, and content features (reviews, interviews, editors' picks) that were cheap to produce and expensive to imitate. What the customer could **not** do: browse a stocked shelf, see true availability, get an instant recommendation, check order status online before the account system matured, or buy in a click.

**The Stage-1 system was a human organization with a website attached.** Every layer that a later reader would expect to be automated is documented as manual. Supplier ordering required exploiting a wholesaler's minimum-quantity rule by hand. Fulfillment was packing on knees in a basement until someone proposed tables. Order arrival was announced by an audible bell that staff turned off because it rang too much. Financial reporting was not integrated with the transaction system "and requires a significant amount of manual effort" — the company's own words to the SEC — while the founder's wife wrote the checks. Site publishing appears to have been a manual flip. Even the traffic-acquisition mechanism (alphabetical directory position) was a human read of how Yahoo worked. The genuinely automated Stage-1 systems were few and narrow: e-mail confirmation and notification, the account/password store, catalog search, and the order-splitting logic against vendor interfaces.

**What the filing's silences tell us.** The S-1 lists as a competitive risk that "companies that control access to transactions through network access or Web browsers could promote the Company's competitors or charge the Company a substantial fee for inclusion." That is a 1995-shaped vulnerability: Amazon's entire addressable market was whatever Mosaic and then Netscape chose to render, and the NCSA "What's New" archive for August 1995 — 3,084 newly listed sites in the launch month — is the scale of the discoverable Web it was selling into. Amazon's own mitigation, documented and dated, was the Associates Program: rather than depend on directories, it made 4,800 third-party sites into storefronts by December 1996, using nothing more than an embedded hyperlink plus a commission rule. That is the single most consequential Stage-1 technical system that popular retellings leave out.

**Security, honestly.** The filings state the dependency — "encryption and authentication technology licensed from third parties" — and stop. No protocol, version, vendor, or certificate authority appears in any local document, and the August-1995 Mosaic archive, searched exhaustively, lists no card-security vendor at all. Export-restriction facts for 1995 cryptography belong to US policy history, not to anything on file here; they are recorded as a data gap rather than asserted.

**Retrospective hygiene.** The three most vivid Stage-1 technical statements in this dossier — that Bezos wrote the first software, that the order bell was switched off, and that the wholesalers' minimum was beaten with a book about lichens — all come from founders and a 2025 regional essay, not from 1995. They are kept because they are the best available and because they cohere with Tier-1 disclosures about manual processes, but each is labeled, and none is used to establish a date.

**RESOLUTION, recorded 1997-05-14 evidence read this session (see F-86):** this conflict is now closed, and both parties were partly right. The two accessions name two different real filings in one series — **0000891618-97-001309** is the original Form S-1 (38 documents, filed 1997-03-24, the 1.44 MB text mined here); **0000891020-97-000839** is a Form **S-1/A** amendment (2 documents, filed 1997-05-14, now in `../sources/`). **X-02 — Secure online card entry vs. e-mail/telephone card capture.**
CLAIM A: the S-1 states order details "are stored on the Company's secure server" and that its applications "manage the process of accepting, authorizing and charging customer credit cards." CLAIM B: the *same* S-1 paragraph says customers are "prompted to supply shipping and credit card details, either by e-mail or by telephone." WHY THEY DIFFER: the prospectus describes a system in transition — an online path that existed and an off-line fallback that had not been retired, in a period when consumers distrusted sending card numbers. EVIDENCE WEIGHT: both are Tier 1 in one document; neither can be discarded. BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: through the end of Stage 1, Amazon accepted card numbers through both channels, with automation on the online path incomplete for hard-to-find or re-priced items. RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: the split of orders by channel is not disclosed anywhere.

EVIDENCE WEIGHT: the beta claim is Tier 2 recollection; the filing is silent on timing. BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: a card-charge path existed at launch in some form, strengthened by the same-month opening of the Wells Fargo merchant account (F-22) — but "automated end-to-end authorization in 1995" is not established.
