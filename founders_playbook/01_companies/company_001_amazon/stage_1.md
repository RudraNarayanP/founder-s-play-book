## A. EXECUTIVE STATE SUMMARY

At 31 December 1995 the company was a Washington corporation with **5.5–6.0 months** of trading behind it (the
convention is stated once, at §P22 / §P.2 d24: the filing dates the opening only to "July 1995", so the span to
1995-12-31 is 184 days = 6.0 months at the earliest admissible start and ≈5.5 at a mid-July start). Audited: net
sales **$511,000** (ship-basis, including outbound shipping and handling), cost of
sales $409,000, gross profit $102,000, operating expenses $406,000 (marketing $200,000; product development
$171,000; G&A $35,000; advertising expense **incurred** $30,000 — the filing's word is "incurred", which is not
the same assertion as "expensed"), operating loss $(304,000), cash $996,000, assets $1,084,000,
equity $977,000, inventory **$17,000 filed on the audited balance sheet** ("Inventories … 17", orig. l.3429 — no
longer an inference), gross equipment $81,000, no long-term obligations, **accumulated deficit $(248,000)**
(orig. l.3461), **14,555,244 common shares issued and outstanding** at 1995-12-31 (orig. l.3455–3457),
**11 employees at 1995-12-31** (filed at that date in S-1/A No. 3/No. 5; the original S-1 states the same 11 as its 1996-01-01
count), international sales $198,000 (S-1 (orig.), 1997-03-24, Selected Financial Data, Notes
1–2, 5, Risk Factors [T1 · FACT] [E-04–E-10, E-31, E-32, E-36, E-37, E-87]). All sales moved through one
Seattle warehouse. The store advertised "more than one million different titles" at 10–40 percent off list,
shipped via UPS or Airborne Express, ran a free email notification service, Eyes & Editors, and solicited
reader reviews (press release, dated 1995-10-04 (indexed 1995-10-03) [T1 · FACT as claim] [D-06,
E-71–E-74]).

The founder held little a 1995 observer would call strength. He carried the President and Chairman titles —
**CEO only from May 1996** [H-25] — 10,200,000 shares bought for $10,000, and a position still carrying a
company repurchase right over 612,000 of those shares [E-50]. Card acceptance at a two-then-eleven-person
firm existed on his personal guarantee, not the company's standing (S-1 (orig.), 1997-03-24, Management —
Certain Transactions [T1 · FACT] [H-07–H-09]).

Still broken: an availability tail the company conceded — "some titles may not be available at all" [E-41];
no vendor contract guaranteeing merchandise, terms or credit, with one distributor at 59% of purchases on a
**1996** basis and the 1995 share undisclosed [E-16, E-17]; transaction systems "not integrated" with
accounting, needing "a significant amount of manual effort" [E-22]; interruptions with no redundancy or
disaster-recovery plan [E-52]; and a cost structure in which, on the audited ratios, no order size was
profitable (DERIVED, arithmetic at P.2).

**Survivorship of the record (C-14 / RD-032; method §2 record-selection null).** Every load-bearing Stage-1
document is a 1997 registration filing, and it exists because this company went public: failed 1995
contemporaries filed no S-1, so a filing-centred Stage 1 over-represents the survivor's side of the record —
including risk factors drafted for liability rather than history, which are candid here because the securities
laws made candour cheap. Nothing in this volume may be read as the average 1995 internet retailer's documentary
condition, because the average one left no audited statements to read. What is unrecoverable *specifically
because the survivor's archive is the one that was kept*: the internal deliberations behind every §N decision
(no 1994 founder-state document exists — COR-06, N-1); the options rejected before entry (the ~20-category
screen survives only as a §N row whose list never surfaces, and the planning document stands at §C.2 rung 3); the failures nobody printed (§M.9's rejected,
failed and unverifiable experiments, and §M.10's untraceable artifacts); and any independent count standing
behind the company's self-reports — the ~2,200 visits, the 50-states/45-countries reach and every repeat share
originate with the seller (§D.2, §L). A 1995 entrant that died leaves no counterpart set at all, so its
deliberations, rejections and failures are not merely unretrieved here but unarchived, and this file's density
of evidence is itself an outcome of survival, not a measure of the period.

Evidence that existed: the audited filing family; one dated company release; two dated outside press
observations (Tallahassee Democrat 1995-10-22; Knight Ridder Nov 1995), both aggregator-sourced, so the chain
is incomplete [D-18, D-19; RD-011]. **No archived amazon.com page from inside the window exists**; the
earliest root capture is a 1998-12-12 bare 302 and the earliest rendered page 1999-08-28 (Internet Archive
CDX and availability API, retrieved 2026-09-23 [T1 · FACT] [A-84, D-21, J-01–J-03]). The S-1's four
"[PICTURES OF THE COMPANY'S WELCOME, SEARCH, REVIEW AND ORDERING WEB PAGES]" are a printer's art instruction,
not lost images: **zero images exist in any of the eight 1997 accessions** (COR-04 → U.21).

Unknown: order counts, average order value, the effect of every acquisition channel, 1994–95 headcount, his
Stage-1 salary, the identity and terms of the non-family investors in the 23-purchaser program, the launch
day, the first customer, and whether any 1994 planning document existed.

Failure remained entirely plausible. The measurable audience was low thousands of visits a day against 24.1%
of U.S. households owning a PC in 1994 (CPS [T1 · FACT] [I-16, I-21]); card-security fear was the
registrant's own named barrier [I-30]; a rival direct-book operation reported "we haven't sold many books"
(Christian Science Monitor 1996-09-18 [T1 · CONTEMPORARY OBSERVATION] [D-52]); a 1995 printed skeptic's case
held there was no trustworthy way to move money online (Newsweek 1995-02-26 [T2 · CONTEMPORARY OBSERVATION]
[I-50]); period press called the firm "either the next light bulb or another Edsel" and noted it "had lost $9
million real dollars to generate sales of $32 million" (LA Times 1997-07-20 [T2 · RETRO] [G-31]) — a 1997
judgment on 1996, recorded as evidence about the climate, not about Stage 1.

### D.2 Assessment

The test showed a narrow, real thing, and the geography is not part of what it showed: a firm of two, then
eleven, people could put a searchable licensed book catalog before a few thousand daily visitors and take
payment from strangers — **with the company itself reporting** all fifty states and 45-plus countries (§L:
"High as claim; **Low as fact**"; → U.1), while the audited statements independently establish ≈38.7% of sales
from outside the United States — on a buy-per-order chain, with almost no inventory and almost no advertising
money, closing the year with ~$1M cash and no debt. It did not show a business. The audited $511,000 covers 5.5–6.0 months (§P.2 d24) and is a
total, not a trend; quarterly disclosure begins at Q1-1996, so every 1995 weekly or monthly curve in the
literature is non-primary by construction [E-93]. The demand signals are self-measured — visits, countries and
repeat share all originate with the seller — and the only independent 1995 evidence shows a journalist testing
a search, not a delivered order (RD-011 stays open). Unit economics were conceded as weak in the company's own
filing: marketing at 39% of sales against 20% gross margin means 1995 growth cost more than it earned.
Success was unsettled on evidence available to anyone then alive — buyers were a minority of households,
payment trust a named industry barrier, a rival direct-book operation reported selling almost nothing, a
general-purpose Web mall had already been shut down, and the largest physical chains had announced online
programs. The most consequential fact is that this experiment's best-known datum — the identity, date and
price of the first sale — cannot be substantiated from any document made while it was happening.

## M. NEGATIVE SIGNALS / FAILURES

1. **A full year of no revenue.** $0 sales in the 179-day 1994 stub on $52,000 of expense, and $0 "from
   inception through July 1995" — about twelve months of paying for nothing [B-128, E-03]. That calendar
   contradicts the founding rhetoric "when it's growing 2,300% a year, weeks are important" [G-03] — a founder
   claim whose statistic is untraced → **U.6**.
2. **Losses were the whole record, and every order lost money by construction.** $107,000 of cumulative losses
   ended the S-election; op. loss $(304,000); **accumulated deficit $(248,000) as filed** (orig. l.3461) — the two loss years sum to
   $355,000 but $107,000 of that was reclassified to common, so 52 + 303 − 107 = 248 (§P.2 d26). The smaller deficit does not
   soften the signal: it is the residue of a $304,000 operating loss and $303,000 of net loss inside a single trading year,
   and it is the same $248,000 that sits on the audited balance sheet next to the $996,000 of cash. A ≈20.0% margin against a 39.1%
   marketing ratio gives $(0.19) contribution per revenue dollar, and **no average order value makes a
   Stage-1 order profitable** (margin would have needed ≥ ~79%). Marketing $200,000 exceeded gross profit
   $102,000. Evidenced arithmetic, not hindsight.
3. **Supply capped the core claim.** Only **up to 400,000** of >2.5M listed titles were distributor-supplied;
   distributors combined "carry up to 350,000 of the best-selling titles" [E-18, E-57]. Firewall: 2.5M is
   **1997**; Stage-1 advertising was **>1,000,000** [E-71], so the advertised-versus-obtainable gap was real
   from month one. Catalogue data was **licensed from unnamed third parties** [E-42; licensor UNKNOWN] → U.3,
   U.40.
4. **Unfilled demand was disclosed.** "Four to six weeks" for the in-print remainder, "two to six months"
   out-of-print, both with "some titles may not be available at all" [E-41]; no fill, back-order or unfillable

5. **Card acceptance is evidenced only in personally guaranteed form** — Seafirst from **Nov 1994, before the

## O. COUNTERFACTUAL OPPORTUNITIES

**No counterfactual below is claimed to have succeeded.** "Considered" = evidence someone looked at it.
**Record-selection warning (`adversarial_review.md` §5 hazard (i); §A "Survivorship of the record"):** the
alternatives named here are visible because the survivor's own filing and the period press that survived with it
recorded them; failed 1995 entrants left no comparable list. Nothing below may be read as "the options that
successful companies took", nor as the menu available to any 1994 firm — the comparison population is
unarchived, not small, and no entry above is scored as a winning move.

**O.1 Enterprise / consumer software.** *Visible:* his career was financial software — an 11-person post-trade
clearing firm, then Bankers Trust's BT World [B-21, B-23]; DESCO's Juno and FarSight were employer-owned
software ventures [E-69]; sellable-online in 1994 as he named it: ISPs, Netscape, HotWired, Yahoo [B-08].
*For:* deepest demonstrated competence, already funded. *Against:* the employer owned the output — his stated
reason for leaving [E-69] → U.32. *Considered:* yes, software is one of four named items on the
twenty-category list [A-16]. *Others:* yes, including DESCO. Medium (RETRO).

**O.2 CD-ROM / interactive multimedia.** *Visible:* the first technical hire came from Kaleida Labs, the
Apple/IBM multimedia JV [F-28]; HotWired as "first content company" [B-08]. *Against:* **the retrieved dossiers
contain no CD-ROM evidence at all** — no market size, no title price, no place on the category list.
*Considered:* **UNKNOWN**. *Others:* **UNKNOWN** on this evidence. Recorded as **UNKNOWN, not as a rejected
alternative**, and must not be written as one.

**O.3 Acquired by, or partnering with, a distributor or incumbent.** *Visible:* the S-1's risk factor discloses
Ingram "help[ing] establish and operate online sites for booksellers" [E-80]; Ingram reported supplying Crown
Books and cbs.sportsline.com [B-80]; Book Stacks was CUC-owned [E-79]. *For:* a supplier-funded route removes
the capital and contract problems the filings concede [E-17]. *Against:* it removes the control he left DESCO
to obtain [B-88] and hands pricing to the supplier. *Considered:* no document shows Bezos pursuing it; a 1996
B&N co-operation meeting is Tier-4 quoting Tier-3, an open research item. *Others:* yes — Crown Books,
cbs.sportsline.com, Book Stacks/CUC. Medium (mechanism); **UNKNOWN (founder consideration)**.

**O.4 Direct-mail / catalogue hybrid.** *Visible:* mail-order was the established mechanism for buying
sight-unseen [I-43, I-44 — structure solid, **quantities UNKNOWN or disallowed** → U.38]; Amazon's launch
language adopted the catalogue form [I-41]. *For:* it sidesteps the barrier the S-1 names, "the secure
transmission of confidential information over public networks" [I-30]. *Against:* the founder's stated reason
for the Web at all was that a book catalogue cannot be mailed, an argument the S-1 repeats on
printing-and-mailing cost [B-10, F-54]. *Considered:* yes, as the incumbent format being displaced. *Others:*
yes — B&N was the largest direct-mail book supplier. Medium.

**O.5 Launch as a niche retailer.** *Visible:* Future Fantasy, WordsWorth, Book Stacks Unlimited
(425,000–450,000 titles) and Britain's Internet Book Shop (912,000–915,000) pre-date or coincide [B-102, D-69,
E-79]; NCSA's Aug-1995 "What's New" listed several commercial book sites [F-71]. *For:* a niche does not
promise availability it cannot deliver, and 1996 demand was niche-shaped (post-boundary) [D-40, D-49].
*Against:* breadth was the whole stated proposition. *Considered:* he placed himself late by his own account —
online bookselling "started 17 years ago… way too early" [B-9]. *Others:* demonstrably yes. Medium → U.11.

**O.6 Staying at D. E. Shaw.** *Visible:* senior VP since 1992, a live mid-1994 bonus, an employer funding
internet ventures [B-63, B-06, B-86]; Shaw tried to keep him, citing the good job and warning DESCO could
compete [B-04, B-98]; his mother counseled running the company nights and weekends, which he refused [B-100
→ U.33]; 1994–95 credit was expensive [I-39]. *For:* salary, bonus, in-house capital. *Against:* ownership
economics [E-69]. *Considered:* yes, the part-time version, and refused. *Others:* DESCO peers. Medium-High on
facts, **all retrospective**; the forfeited amount remains UNKNOWN.

**O.7 Boundary note (not a counterfactual).** Bezos placed himself late, not first: the S-1 named Book Stacks
Unlimited among competitors and claimed only "first mover advantages" [E-79, G-12]; period press stated Amazon
"isn't the first or necessarily the cheapest online book merchant" [D-69, with Wired 1996-12-16 and the Seattle
Times 1997-01-05]. The Stage-1 differentiator the evidence supports is aggregation and discoverability —
directory placement, licensed catalogue data, e-mail notification, tiered discounting — not chronology
[D narrative; G-33] → **U.11**.

*One conflict, not three (COR-11.5); this absorbs p4's U1, claims_KU's U.1 and claims_AJ's U.10.*
**CLAIM A:** the filings state the company "was incorporated in July 1994 and commenced offering products for
sale on its Web site in July 1995", that it "began selling books on its Web site in July 1995", and that "for
the period from inception through July 1995, the Company had no sales" (S-1 (orig.), 1997-03-24; S-1/A No. 5,
1997-05-14).
**CLAIM B:** Amazon's own press release, dated 1995-10-04 (indexed 1995-10-03), reports that "in just its first
four weeks of operation, the company shipped books to customers in all 50 states and more than 45 countries" —
counting back to circa early September 1995, not to 16 July (which would be eleven weeks). Later restatements
drift to "first month" (2005) and "first two months" (Wikipedia): mutations of the same release, not
independent checks.
**CLAIM C:** the canon fixes a day — **16 July 1995** — from a 2005 anniversary piece and a 2025 essay citing a
2013 book; and one buyer claims **3 April 1995** (→ U.2). Kaphan separately reports a soft launch "a couple
months earlier" than July and a six-week closed beta (~300 invited users, cards charged).
**WHY THEY DIFFER:** three candidate readings, none excluded. (i) *Public launch vs commencement of sales* —
the release may define "operation" as unrestricted public ordering, with July a restricted or soft-opened
trading month. (ii) *Loose PR arithmetic* off an unstated base. (iii) *Both A and B are company self-reports*
with no independent verification of either, so the tension is between two statements by the same author, not
between a claim and a measurement. Note also the internal ambiguity in A: "through July 1995" can mean "up to
the start of July" or "through the end of July", and the second reading would put **zero** recognised sales in
July 1995.
**EVIDENCE WEIGHT:** both Tier 1. A is audited, appears in two separately-accessioned filings, and controls
revenue dates. B is contemporaneous and is the only dated 1995 statement about when outside demand appeared.
C is Tier 2/3 and ten-plus years downstream, with **no primary support for the day** (COR-09).
**BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION:** report two distinct facts and keep them apart — recognised trading from
**July 1995** (High), and the company's own claim of 50-state/45-country shipping within **four weeks as of
1995-10-04** (High that it was claimed, Low that it was verified). Amazon could transact in July 1995; the
only dated company statement about when outside demand appeared is the four-weeks claim. State the launch as
"July 1995 per SEC filings, publicly announced 4 October 1995" and **refuse a day**. Do not compute a launch
date from the release.
**RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY:** which event "operation" counts from; whether any July orders were recognised in a
later period; whether the release understated its own age for effect; and whether an April/May insider beta
transaction predates every official dating (which the audited statements do not exclude, since an immaterial
insider order need not appear as revenue).
**CONFIDENCE:** High (in the reconciliation and in the month); **Low (on any day)**; High (that the conflict is
unresolved).

**CLAIM A:** the first non-employee order was placed **3 April 1995** by John Wainwright, a software engineer at
Kaleida Labs and friend of Shel Kaphan, for Douglas Hofstadter's *Fluid Concepts and Creative Analogies* at
**$27.95**, after Kaphan e-mailed him to "create an account and order some books" (MarketWatch 2015-07-15;

**CLAIM B:** every company statement places the first sale in **July 1995** at the earliest — the S-1's

**CLAIM C:** the object itself mutates: "a science textbook" (Seattle Times 2005-07-10); "Mr. Wainwright" vs
"John Wainwright"; an "Artificial Intelligence" book in one rendering; and Amazon's own 2007-archived IR
timeline reads "1995 July / Amazon.com Sells First Book, 'Fluid Concepts & Creative Analogies'".
**CLAIM D:** a prospectus sentence circulated online — "we officially launched our Web site in July 1995 and
shipped our first book in August 1995" — is **absent from all five retrieved SEC documents**: a documented null,

**EVIDENCE WEIGHT:** A is Tier 2, internally self-contradicting (the same Atlantic article prints both 3 April
1995 and Amazon's own July 1995 dating), and its three tellings descend from **one** interview cycle. B's month
is Tier 1 and audited; B's day is Tier 2/3 and unreferenced. C's company entry is Tier 1 as a 2007 artifact and
Tier 4 as 1995 evidence. A alone is tied to a physical packing slip and the buyer's own order history.
**BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION:** the "first sale" is a **company- and customer-originated legend with variant
details**, presentable only as a variant list. Hofstadter's book is the best-attested first *non-employee*
order; "science textbook" is a degraded variant of the same story; "shipped our first book in August 1995" is
untraced. The launch is stated at the precision the record supports — **July 1995** — and **no individual may
be certified as the first customer**, because the real first transactions were probably insider beta
purchases. The only figure common across versions is $27.95, and that is a list-derived price, not a measured
one.
**RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY:** total on the day, the identity, the title; no 1995-97 Amazon document names a first
customer, title or day, and the company never identified one during Stage 1.
**CONFIDENCE:** High (that the legend is unresolved); **Low on every specific**; Medium on A alone.

most load-bearing non-filing witness for the 1994-95 build — but it is also the **only** source for the ordering.
**BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION:** "first technical hire with dated in-window paper" is supportable;
"employee #1" is not, because Paul Davis's arrival "a month later" rests on Kaphan's own recollection and no
filing discloses 1994 headcount at all. **Legal co-founder status is not established by any corporate
instrument**, and no co-founder appears in the record.
**RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY:** whether non-technical staff preceded him; whether contractors count.
**CONFIDENCE:** High (Kaphan's dates); **UNKNOWN** (ordinal position, co-founder status).

**U.28 — MacKenzie Tuttle: documented role versus a corporate silence that is not proof.**
**CLAIM A:** retrospective accounts put her inside the founding — driving while he typed the plan, signing
cheques and doing bookkeeping in 1994-95, described by Sheff as "an administrative assistant working on her first
novel" at the D. E. Shaw period; HistoryLink supplies biographical particulars.
**CLAIM B:** **neither the original S-1 nor the FY1997 10-K405 names her anywhere** — her surname does not appear
in the S-1 text at all — and she is absent as officer, director, principal holder and named related party; the
10-K405's Part III related-party item **defers to a 1998 proxy statement that is not on disk**.
**WHY THEY DIFFER:** the filings' disclosure thresholds and categories, not the history. A filing that need not
name a non-officer, non-5% holder will be silent whether or not she did the work.
**EVIDENCE WEIGHT:** B is a Tier 1 **established null** — high confidence *that* the filings are silent;
A is Tier 2/3 retrospective and partly single-lineage (Stone, whom HistoryLink and Wikipedia both follow).
**BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION:** state the null and its limit together, as §B.1 does: **high that the filings
omit her — not proof of absence of contribution** (RD-015). Title, pay, equity and dates are UNKNOWN.
**RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY:** the missing 1998 proxy is the primary that could change this and is recorded as a gap,
not as a finding.
**CONFIDENCE:** High (of the silence) / Low (of the substance).

**U.29 — Founder ownership percentage: which basis is being quoted.**
**CLAIM A:** the S-1's Principal Stockholders table — **9,885,000 shares, 48.3% pre-offering and 43.1%
post-offering as of 1997-02-28** — with Risk Factors stating ~41% for Bezos and ~10% for family and trusts.
**CLAIM B:** retrospective tellings round his stake to ~41% or ~43% and speak of it as though it described 1995.
**WHY THEY DIFFER:** share counts are split-restated (4:1 in Nov 1996, 3:2 in Apr 1997) and the percentages are
pre- versus post-offering; the founding instrument itself names **1,700,000** shares against a filing body that
says **10,200,000**, a factor of exactly six.
**EVIDENCE WEIGHT:** A, decisively, for its own dates — all Tier 1 and under securities law.
**BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION:** any Stage-1 ownership statement must name its basis (instrument vs
split-restated filing) or be marked UNKNOWN; the ~41%/~10% figures are **out of stage** and appear only as
consequences. Control in Stage 1 is evidenced differently and better: he was **the sole stockholder** as of
1994-09-15 (original S-1 only).
**RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY:** the exact 1995 percentage is unknowable from the filing because the 1995 issuances are
reported in shares and cash, not in percentages.
**CONFIDENCE:** High (filed figures) / **UNKNOWN** (any 1995 percentage).

**U.30 — Regret minimisation: who framed it, and when.**
**CLAIM A:** Bezos decided by a self-invented heuristic — projecting himself to age 80 and looking back — and
says this framework, not analysis, decided it (Sheff/Playboy, published 2000).
**CLAIM B:** no in-window document states the framework; the earliest retrieval is 1999/2000, and the same
founder gives different rationales in different years (→ U.14, U.13).
**WHY THEY DIFFER:** A is a mature retrospective rationalisation offered decades after the act; the 1994 record
contains no decision memo.
**EVIDENCE WEIGHT:** A proves only **that he said it in 1999/2000**. It cannot be weighted as a 1994 mental state.
**BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION:** §N may cite regret minimisation as the founder's own later account of the
departure, at Medium confidence, tagged `RETROSPECTIVE SOURCE`. Firewall: it explains a **choice**, not an
outcome, and must not be recast as foresight or as evidence that success was discounted. Attribution of the
idea to Bezos personally is also unverified — no second party is documented hearing it in 1994.
**RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY:** when the phrase entered his repertoire; whether he used it before 1997.
**CONFIDENCE:** High (that it was said) / **UNKNOWN** (that it guided the 1994 act).

**U.31 — The drive west: three tellings of one trip.**
**CLAIM A:** the laptop business plan typed while MacKenzie drove (LA Times 1997-07-20).
**CLAIM B:** "I wrote the first draft of the business plan in the car on the way" — prose, spoken 1999,
published 2000.
**CLAIM C:** projections typed into an **Excel spreadsheet** in the passenger seat, "radically inaccurate"
(Stone 2013), with Kaphan separately reporting he heard the account second-hand from Bezos.
**WHY THEY DIFFER:** one event, three incompatible physical artifacts, in three tellings 1997 / 1999-2000 / 2013.
Under §3's independence rule these are **one source**, and the underlying artifact question is adjudicated at
**U.7**.
**EVIDENCE WEIGHT:** equal on the trip (it plainly happened: the marriage, the mid-1994 departure from Manhattan
and the Seattle arrival are separately attested); unequal on what was produced during it.
**BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION:** the journey may be narrated; the **document may not**. Recorded separately
from U.7 so the appendix can key the trip itself rather than the roadmap legend.
**RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY:** route, duration and who drove are retrospective detail; none is load-bearing.
**CONFIDENCE:** High (the trip) / Low (each artifact claim).

**U.32 — Solo insight versus employer-sanctioned research.**
**CLAIM A:** the lone-founder framing — he saw the opportunity, told no one at first, and left to build it.
**CLAIM B:** Stone's reporting that David Shaw **anointed** Bezos in 1994 to spearhead DESCO's Internet effort,
with Shaw and Bezos meeting a few hours each week to brainstorm; and Kaphan's account that Bezos had been
researching business plans **for** D. E. Shaw on the Internet, with the concept already quantitatively narrowed
to books while he was still employed there.
**WHY THEY DIFFER:** two retrospective framings of the same months, one valuing the founder myth, one valuing the
process. Neither has an in-window document.
**EVIDENCE WEIGHT:** B carries more independent attestation (two non-Bezos sources) but is Tier 2/3; A is Tier 2
and founder-originated. The only *structural* fact with a text behind it is the **two-year DESCO non-recruit
bar**, itself reported from a book with the contract unretrieved (Medium).
**BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION:** record both; §N's rationale column already attributes the exit to
**ownership economics** ("the employer owned the output"), which is the founder's own retrospective statement and
is consistent with either framing. Do not present the idea as either purely private or purely institutional.
**RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY:** what DESCO formally commissioned; who owned any resulting work product; whether the
20-category list was a firm document (no 1994 document survives, §S).
**CONFIDENCE:** Medium (B) / Low (A) / **UNKNOWN** (which framing is closer to the event).

**U.33 — Whose consent narrative, and from when.**
**CLAIM A:** the popular sequence — he asked his wife and his parents for money and got agreement, with the
mother advising nights-and-weekends and the father investing — told with quoted lines.
**CLAIM B:** the most-quoted spousal-consent line exists only as a **2010-05-30 Princeton commencement address**
(relayed via HistoryLink quoting Wired), and **Sheff, in 1999/2000, reports no quoted reaction from her at all**;
the parents' review of the plan is known only through the founder's own later accounts.
**WHY THEY DIFFER:** a speech is a genre that supplies dialogue; an interview that omits it is evidence the
dialogue was not yet in circulation.
**EVIDENCE WEIGHT:** the **fact of family investment** is Tier 1 and strong — two dated 1995 Shareholder's
Agreements (father 1995-02-09; Gise Family Trust 1995-07-24) and the Item 5 disclosure of three founder-related
investors. The **quoted consent** is Tier 4 and 2010.
**BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION:** separate the two. §B may state that family members invested and that Bezos
says they reviewed the plan; it may not present quoted consent as a 1994 scene. Firewall: the consent narrative
must not be used to argue the money was "smart" — nor, conversely, to frame the investors as fools (method §2).
**RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY:** amounts (→ U.8), dates of any conversation, and whether MacKenzie held any interest
(→ U.28).
**CONFIDENCE:** High (that family money came in) / **UNKNOWN** (the conversations).

**U.34 — When Bezos first used the Internet.**
**CLAIM A:** the 1997 record of the trigger event — he surfed the Web **for the first time in spring 1994** and
"happened upon" a site projecting 2,300% annual growth, then drafted a list of 20 ventures before choosing books
(LA Times 1997-07-20).
**CLAIM B:** Stone reports he first encountered the Internet in an **astrophysics class at Princeton in 1985**,
and had not thought about its commercial potential until DESCO.
**WHY THEY DIFFER:** different definitions of "used" — a workstation-connected course exercise versus
world-wide-web browsing — plus the usual compression of a research process into a moment of discovery.
**EVIDENCE WEIGHT:** both Tier 2, both retrospective, and neither independent of the founder. No in-window
document dates either session.
**BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION:** classify as **FOUNDER CLAIM / date UNKNOWN**, and keep it out of §P entirely.
The claim matters only because the "first surf → instant decision" form of the story is what the 2,300% legend
attaches to (→ U.6, U.7); the defensible statement is that a 1993-94 traffic series convinced him the medium was
inflecting, at some point in spring 1994 he describes as decisive.
**RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY:** the month, the site, and whether the sequence was browsing-then-research or
research-then-browsing.
**CONFIDENCE:** Low (each specific); High (that none is documented).

**U.35 — Pre-Amazon employment record and the superlatives attached to it.**
**CLAIM A:** the filed bio — **D. E. Shaw & Co., December 1990 to June 1994, senior VP from 1992; Bankers Trust
Company, April 1988 to December 1990, VP from February 1990**.
**CLAIM B:** the surrounding embellishments — "**youngest** senior vice-president at a division of American
Express"; and Sheff's narrator line that between Bankers Trust and Shaw he worked at **Fitel fiber optics as
"associate director of technology and development"**, a title Bezos does not claim for himself in the filings.
**WHY THEY DIFFER:** A is Tier 1 disclosure; B is journalism and a magazine's bridging prose. The Fitel
employment itself is plausible and uncontradicted, but the **title** appears only in the narrator's voice, and
the "youngest" superlative appears in **no** filing.
**EVIDENCE WEIGHT:** A decisively on employers and dates; B is a **filing null against a secondary assertion**,
which is the relevant test, not a corroboration.
**BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION:** print A as fact; carry the superlative as **journalism-carried, Low**; note
Fitel as an unfiled employer attested only retrospectively, with its title unverified.
**RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY:** his exact last day at D. E. Shaw (RD-010, open); the Fitel dates and title.
**CONFIDENCE:** High (tenure) / Low (epithets).

**U.36 — The adoption baseline: how many households were online in 1995.**
**CLAIM A:** circulating figures that a small share of US households — "6.8% of households online in 1994" style
numbers — were on the Internet in the founding moment.
**CLAIM B:** **no comparable 1995 measurement exists in this corpus.** Home PC ownership is 24.1% of US households
in 1994 (CPS) and is used in §H **as a proxy, and labelled as one**; NTIA/CPS household-Internet tracking begins
in 1997 (18.6%), with 26.2% in 1998; and the headline user totals compiled by Nua ("How Many Online": 101 million
world, 64 million "Canada & USA", attributed only to "Various") are aggregates, not measurements, while the IDC
"Web users" figure inside the S-1 (~35 million at end-1996, projected ~163 million by 2000) has an undisclosed
definition and is not comparable to anything.
**WHY THEY DIFFER:** A is an unsourced number of later vintage; B is what the primary series actually support.
**EVIDENCE WEIGHT:** B — documented nulls and named series. RD-018 stays OPEN.
**BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION:** §H must use PC ownership as the proxy and say so in the cell; access cost and
speed are UNKNOWN (no in-window ISP or modem survey retrieved); the "6.8%" may not be printed.
**RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY:** any 1993-95 host or user count on an agreed unit.
**CONFIDENCE:** High (on the proxy and on the nulls); **UNKNOWN** (the underlying quantity).

**U.37 — 1994 CPI: 2.6 versus 2.7 percent.**
**CLAIM A:** annual-average CPI inflation of **2.6%** for 1994 in one secondary compilation of BLS data.
**CLAIM B:** **2.7%** for 1994 in another, with the 1993-96 range given as 2.6–3.0% across both.
**WHY THEY DIFFER:** annual-average versus calendar-year, and revision/rounding between republished tables — the
underlying series is the same and one source's own publication date is UNKNOWN.
**EVIDENCE WEIGHT:** equal and immaterial: nothing in Stage 1 turns on a tenth of a point.
**BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION:** print the **band (2.6–3.0% for 1993-96)** rather than a false decimal, and
carry the discrepancy here so no appendix row is orphaned.
**RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY:** which vintage of the BLS series each compiler used.
**CONFIDENCE:** High (that the difference is immaterial).

**U.38 — The size of the direct-mail and catalogue precedent.**
**CLAIM A:** mail order was a $211 billion business by 1990, growing more than 9.9% a year to 1996 and "more than
3 percent of retail sales" — the numbers that make Amazon's catalogue framing legible.
**CLAIM B:** that figure comes from an industry profile with **no disclosed basis** (Gale's *Encyclopedia of
American Trade Profiles*) and is **disallowed**; what survives scrutiny is **structural, not quantitative** — the
catalogue was the recognised American mechanism for buying without seeing, B&N self-described as the **world's
largest supplier of books through direct-mail catalogues**, and Amazon's launch-era language deliberately claimed
that identity.
**WHY THEY DIFFER:** a trade-encyclopedia total, probably spanning several definitions of "mail order", is being
read as a measured market.
**EVIDENCE WEIGHT:** B — a Tier 1 self-description plus the documented absence of any in-window (1994-96) trade or
government measurement.
**BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION:** the precedent may be used to show the format was **mass-scale and
culturally normal**, never to size it; catalogue circulation and depth at B&N were **not retrieved**. Quantities
**UNKNOWN** (§S).
**RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY:** the whole of the mail-order denominator.
**CONFIDENCE:** High (structure) / **UNKNOWN** (dollars).

**U.39 — Observed fulfilment speed and damage.**
**CLAIM A:** a distributor ships "often … within hours" (S-1) against Bezos's 1999 "next day on our loading
dock" — read side by side, they look contradictory.
**CLAIM B:** independent period observation: customers "could wait a week or longer", with only several hundred
bestsellers on hand (trade/press, 1996-97); and Stone's report that Bezos and DESCO's recruiting head Charles
Ardai bought a copy of Asimov's *Cyberdreams* from the Future Fantasy bookstore's site in Palo Alto for **$6.04**,
which arrived **two weeks later, badly tattered** — the only per-order price/duration/damage datapoint in the
founding material.
**WHY THEY DIFFER:** A is **two different legs of one chain** (distributor dispatch versus inbound receipt), not
two measurements of the same interval; B mixes Amazon's own service level with a **competitor's** delivery.
**EVIDENCE WEIGHT:** the S-1 leg is Tier 1; the 1999 leg is Tier 2 retrospective; the Asimov order is a Tier 2
anecdote about a different merchant, usable as evidence about the 1994 competitive experience, not about Amazon.
**BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION:** keep the leg labels; **do not compute an end-to-end time**. The operative
finding is §G's: elapsed time is a **band, not a number**, and no 1995 timing measurement exists (→ U.22).
**RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY:** 1995 inbound and outbound intervals; damage rates; carrier terms (UPS/FedEx 1995
tariffs not retrieved).
**CONFIDENCE:** High (that these are different legs) / Low (each duration).

**U.40 — Whether any vendor or distribution agreement exists in the record.**
**CLAIM A:** the relationship must have been contractually secured — Ingram's 59% share implies a supply
agreement, and the offer of a million titles implies guaranteed availability.
**CLAIM B:** **no distribution or publisher agreement is filed as an exhibit anywhere in the original S-1's
38-document set**, and the filing affirmatively disclaims any arrangement "that guarantee[s] the availability of
merchandise, the continuation of particular payment terms or the extension of credit limits", stated in the
present tense across the audited period. Ingram's 59% is **FY1996 inventory/book purchases**; the 1995 share was
never disclosed; and Ingram is separately disclosed as helping others "establish and operate online sites for
booksellers".
**WHY THEY DIFFER:** a purchase-concentration statistic is being read as a contract.
**EVIDENCE WEIGHT:** B — an exhibit-set null plus the registrant's own disclaimer, both Tier 1.
**BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION:** disclosed Stage-1 fragility, not hindsight: the >1,000,000-title 1995 offer ran
on **unguaranteed per-order terms against the filed $17,000 of stock**. Discount rate, payment terms and credit limits are
**UNKNOWN**, and this is the COGS leg of unit economics that has **no primary number**.
**RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY:** whether unfiled letters of intent existed (gaps G-H6, E-Contradictions); the 1995
concentration figure.
**CONFIDENCE:** High (the null and the disclaimer) / UNKNOWN (terms and 1995 share).

**U.41 — The Associates Program: 1996 technology mislabelled as Stage-1.**
**CLAIM A:** the program "had over 4,800 registered members as of December 31, 1996, making it a shipped, scaled
**Stage-1** system rather than a later innovation".
**CLAIM B:** Amazon itself dates the program's start to **July 1996**, and **no Stage-1 revenue share is
attributable to it**; the 4,800+ count is a 1996-12-31 figure.
**WHY THEY DIFFER:** a record-keeping slip inside the appendix — one dossier row applied the "Stage 1" label to a
1996 launch because the S-1 describes it in the same business section as the 1995 site.
**EVIDENCE WEIGHT:** B, from the company's own dating (Tier 1), against A's wording.
**BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION:** the Associates Program is **Stage-2/1996 material** — the mechanism (an
embedded hyperlink plus curated per-partner lists; Netscape Developer's Bookstore, The Village Voice,
Upside.com named) is recorded in §E's version table **and labelled post-boundary**, appearing only as a
consequence of the transition. §R's Distribution cell carries the same correction explicitly.
**RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY:** none on the date; the open question is what proportion of 1996 growth it caused
(→ U.12).
**CONFIDENCE:** High.

**U.42 — Fortune's self-conflict on the Walmart streak (universe-level precedent; not Amazon evidence).**
*p4's U8, re-keyed. Retained because deleting it would lose the method ruling it licenses.*
**CLAIM A:** Fortune's 2026 **Fortune 500** text credits Walmart with **13** years at #1.
**CLAIM B:** Fortune's own **Global 500** text describes a **12-year** run.
**WHY THEY DIFFER:** different universes and different count conventions (US-only versus global, and whether the
just-published year is counted) inside one publisher's edition.
**EVIDENCE WEIGHT:** equal — both are the same authority, so neither can be preferred on source standing; the
discrepancy is internal to the source.
**BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION:** **record the conflict rather than smooth it**, exactly as done for the
Wikipedia-versus-Fortune rank conflicts and Home Depot's fiscal-year boundary irregularity in the universe freeze.
This is the precedent that licenses the same treatment in U.1–U.41: **an unresolved conflict in the record is a
finding, not an embarrassment.** It is explicitly **not** Amazon evidence and is excluded from every Amazon cell
in this volume — and it is itself a firewall test, since a 2026 ranking says nothing about 1995.
**RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY:** which streak convention each Fortune text used; unresolved, and immaterial to Amazon
Stage 1 except as method.
**CONFIDENCE:** High (that the conflict exists); UNKNOWN (which is right).
