In  presenting  this  report  of  the  Travelers 
Aid  Society  of  Boston,  the  Board  of  Directors 
wish  to  call  attention  to  the  rapid  development 
of  the  work  during  the  six  years  of  the  exis¬ 
tence  of  the  Society.  They  especially  wish 
to  thank  those  whose  gifts  have  made  this 
expansion  possible. 

Acknowledgement  is  also  due  to  the  mem¬ 
bers  of  the  staff  for  their  untiring  efforts 
under  difficult  conditions.  The  work  requires 
a  high  grade  of  intelligence  and  character,  and 
great  tactfulness  in  approaching  the  persons 
who  need  the  aid  of  the  Society.  Our  workers 
have  performed  their  arduous  and  yet  delicate 
work  with  great  skill  and  devotion. 

JAMES  ARNOLD  LOWELL, 

President. 


4 


The  year  1022  brought  no  change  in  the  character 
of  the  work  to  the  Travelers  Aid  Society  of  Boston, 
either  at  stations  or  docks.  The  society  has 
continued  to  care  for  the  immigrant,  the  stranger, 
the  elderly,  the  sick;  the  young  girl,  when  be¬ 
wildered  or  in  difficulty,  and  the  runaway. 

During  the  last  five  years  the  Travelers  Aid 
movement  has  experienced  a  rapid  expansion 
throughout  the  country,  until  high  grade  service 
has  been  established  in  most  of  the  larger  cities. 
Every  day  more  people  come  into  contact  with 
Travelers  Aid  work,  and  as  a  consequence  look  for 
the  “worker  with  the  badge”  on  arrival  in  a 
strange  city.  If  she  is  not  at  the  train  or  seated 
under  the  Travelers  Aid  sign  at  the  desk,  there 
is  a  breakdown  in  the  service.  And  yet  to  maintain 
uninterrupted  desk  assistance,  it  requires  two 
workers  on  duty  at  the  same  time  in  order  that 
one  worker  may  be  free  to  meet  trains,  do  scout 
work  about  the  station,  and  occasionally  accom¬ 
pany  travelers  to  destinations.  The  Boston  society 
regrets  that  at  the  present  time  it  is  unable  to 
meet  this  fundamental  demand  of  adequate  Trav¬ 
elers  Aid  service.  As  soon  as  funds  are  available, 
the  society  should  place  two  workers,  instead  of 
one,  on  duty  at  each  station.  Increased  funds  are 
hoped  for  in  order  to  meet  this  compelling  need. 

Comment  is  frequently  made  to  the  effect  that 
the  turnover  among  social  workers  is  unfortunately 
large.  Particularly  is  this  true  in  the  Travelers 
Aid  field,  because  of  the  difficult  early  morning 
and  night  hours,  and  the  strenuous  and  public 
nature  of  the  work.  We  therefore  note  with 
especial  satisfaction  the  record  of  the  Boston 
society.  Out  of  a  staff  of  twelve  people  consisting 
of  eight  station  and  dock  workers  and  four  office 
employees,  two  have  been  with  us  six  years,  two 
five  years,  one  four,  and  one  three.  The  others 
have  been  with  us  from  periods  of  two  years  to 
three  months.  This  is  one  of  the  most  important 
factors  in  efficient  service  and  continuity  of 
standards. 

OLIVE  M.  JAQUES, 
General  Secretary.

Sixteen-year-old  Josephine  was  restless  and 
dissatisfied,  and  consequently  left  her  New  Hamp¬ 
shire  home,  clad  in  sneakers  and  sweater,  to  hike 
for  New  York.  Fortunately,  in  Boston,  she  came 
into  the  care  of  the  Travelers  Aid.  She  explained 
that  she  had  great  artistic  talents  which  were 
being  smothered  in  her  small  home  town.  So, 
with  her  imagination  fired  by  the  movies,  she  had 
decided  to  seek  her  fortune  before  she  was  too 
old  to  succeed. 

Long  conferences  followed  with  her  widowed 
father,  an  aunt,  and  a  specialist  on  child 
psychology.  The  verdict  was  that  Josephine  was 
suffering  from  adolescent  restlessness  and  too  great 
freedom  from  discipline.  The  perplexed  father 
decided  to  give  her  into  the  care  of  an  intelligent 
aunt  of  unusually  fine  character,  and  in  her 
home  in  a  near-by  suburb  Josephine  is  learning 
to  shoulder  responsibility.  Her  cherished  artistic 
ability  is  being  stimulated  under  a  good  high  school 
art  course.  From  a  peevish,  unhappy  girl,  Jose¬ 
phine  is  developing  into  a  stable  and  useful  woman. 


Late  one  Sunday  night  the  Travelers  Aid  worker 
noticed  two.  young,  countrified-appearing  girls 
with  a  couple  of  sailors  coming  from  the  New 
York  express.  As  they  hesitated  a  moment  she 
approached  and  found  the  sailors  planned  to  find 
the  girls  a  room  for  the  night.  The  girls  gladly 
went  instead  to  a  home  for  women  with  our 
worker,  and  on  their  way  confided  that  they  had 
never  been  in  Boston  before  and  were  here  by 
mistake.  They  had  been  advised  by  the  sailors  to 
take  the  express  from  Providence,  where  they 
had  spent  the  day,  to  their  home  in  Mansfield, 
but  found  too  late  that  the  train  did  not  stop 
at  Mansfield.  Only  the  appearance  of  the  Travelers 
Aid  workers  prevented  their  taking  the  further 
and  perhaps  disastrous  advice  of  their  sailor 
companions.
