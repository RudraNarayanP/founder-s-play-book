REPORT  OF  GENERAL  SECRETARY 


Although  the  Travelers  Aid  Society  of  Boston 
is  one  of  the  newest  social  organizations  in  the 
city,  the  need  for  its  existence  has  been  demon¬ 
strated,  and  the  society  has  become  an  established 
factor  in  the  community.  From  a  financial  stand¬ 
point  these  first  seven  years  have  been  lean  ones; 
however,  one  dares  to  hope  that  the  coming  seven 
years  will  be  at  least  less  lean.  Up  to  the  present 
time  funds  have  not  been  available  for  a  staff 
sufficiently  large  to  cover  the  ground  thoroughly. 
The  society  is  conscious  of  not  doing  all  the  work 
there  is  for  it  at  the  stations  and  at  the  docks. 

It  is  only  because  of  the  constant  cooperation 
given  by  the  hundreds  of  station,  dock  and  govern¬ 
ment  officials  that  so  much  has  been  accomplished. 
The  courtesy  and  efforts  of  this  large  group  of 
men,  who  have  so  many  opportunities  to  observe 
social  situations  and  to  direct  the  people  in  trouble 
to  the  Travelers  Aid  Society,  are  daily  components 
of  the  work. 

The  Boston  Terminal  Company  has  generously 
given  an  office  to  the  society  and  through  the  con¬ 
sideration  of  Mr.  William  H.  Wright,  the  manager, 
many  favors  have  been  granted.  Since  the  closing 
of  the  Temporary  Home  for  Working  Women  last 
spring  to  our  young  girls  and  women,  the  Franklin 
Square  House  and  the  temporary  home  of  the 
Overseers  of  the  Public  Welfare,  although  not 
established  to  give  such  special  temporary  care, 
have  made  most  happy  and  unlimited  efforts  to 
meet  the  needs  of  this  society. 

The  year  1923  brought  an  increase  of  work  over 
that  of  the  preceding  year.  Over  50  percent  more 
runaway  boys  and  girls  were  found  at  our  busy 
stations.  Whenever  possible  these  boys  and  girls 
were  referred  to  organizations  working  with  be¬ 
havior  problems,  or  when  necessary  to  societies 
equipped  to  protect  children  from  unsuitable  home 
conditions. 

There  has  also  been  a  tremendous  increase  in 
immigrant  work.  During  the  quota  periods  of  July, 
August,  September,  October  and  November,  the 
society  was  taxed  to  its  capacity.  Each  month 


[5] 


brought  more  appointments  to  meet  immigrants 
than  the  preceding  one,  until  in  November  when 
there  was  an  unprecedented  number.  To  meet  this 
emergency  some  most  helpful  volunteers  were 
secured  from  the  Boston  University  School  of  Reli¬ 
gious  Education  and  Social  Work.  Even  with  this 
assistance  the  workers  were  obliged  to  be  on  duty 
from  ten  to  sixteen  hours  daily  and  in  many  cases 
worked  far  beyond  the  limit  of  their  strength. 

Appointments  by  mail  and  wire  from  other 
Travelers  Aid  Societies,  relatives  and  friends  from 
all  over  the  United  States  asked  assistance  for 
the  arriving  immigrants.  We  located  these  people 
in  the  lines  in  the  immigration  rooms,  gave  them 
special  messages  and  money,  assisted  in  inter¬ 
preting  and  with  their  tickets  and  baggage,  united 
them  with  waiting  relatives  or  telegraphed  ahead 
to  relatives  or  Travelers  Aid  Societies  in  distant 
cities.  It  was  gratifying  to  note  how  many  ap¬ 
pointments  came  from  friends  or  relatives  who 
had  themselves  been  helped  by  Travelers  Aid 
workers  on  landing  in  the  United  States  of 
America. 

Most  interesting  were  the  young  girls  coming 
to  be  married.  Some  were  referred  from  their 
point  of  destination  and  others  by  the  immigra¬ 
tion  officials.  When  referred  by  the  latter,  tele¬ 
graphic  investigations  were  made  of  the  fiances 
and  when  the  results  were  satisfactory  to  the 
government  the  girls  were  discharged  to  us  and 
sent  in  the  care  of  the  Travelers  Aid  Societies 
at  destinations,  where  the  marriage  ceremonies 
were  witnessed.  Frequently  when  fiances  came 
to  claim  the  newcomers,  the  girls  were  discharged 
to  our  society  to  witness  the  ceremony.  The 
workers  indeed  became  temporarily  their  closest 
friends. 

There  have  been  no  changes  in  the  general 
character  of  the  work.  The  elderly  and  sick  have 
been  given  all  assistance  possible  on  the  many 
difficult  occasions  which  so  easily  arise  during 
travel.  The  young  girl,  a  stranger  to  the  city, 
has  been  directed  to  good  lodgings,  and  helped  to 
find  suitable  work  and  to  become  adjusted  to  new 
and  trying  conditions  of  life.  Less  important  ser¬ 
vices  have  been  rendered  to  thousands  every 
month.  Friends  have  been  located,  strangers 


[6] 


directed,  room  reservations  procured,  and  general 
information  given. 

The  function  of  the  society  to  be  a  distinctly 
referring  agency  where  all  social  problems  are  in¬ 
volved  has  been  strictly  carried  out  during  the 
year.  In  this  way  the  society  is  not  duplicating 
the  work  of  the  ones  organized  for  special  kinds 
of  work  and  the  clients  have  the  advantage  of 
the  advice  of  workers  specializing  in  their  particu¬ 
lar  need.  As  in  all  social  work,  we  are  not  doing 
for  people  what  they  can  do  for  themselves  or 
what  is  the  function  of  the  railroad  and  steam¬ 
ship  officials.  Whenever  there  are  telephone  or 
telegram  charges,  the  clients  able  to  pay  for  them 
are  asked  to  do  so. 

In  giving  consideration  to  the  general  aspects 
of  the  work  the  fact  which  stands  out  most 
strongly  from  my  viewpoint  as  general  secretary 
is  that  the  accomplishments  of  the  society  have 
been  made  by  great  sacrifices  on  the  part  of  the 
workers.  There  are  many  difficult  features  about 
the  duty  of  the  station  worker,  the  first  of  which 
is  the  unusually  early  and  late  hours  which  in 
addition  to  being  arduous  do  not  permit  her  to 
have  the  normal  life  with  her  family  and  friends. 
The  Sunday  and  holiday  duty  accentuates  this 
condition.  There  is  never  an  opportunity  for  a 
worker  to  go  away  for  a  few  minutes  to  get  her 
bearings.  During  the  unusually  busy  periods,  such 
as  holidays  and  during  the  immigration  rushes,  it 
is  necessary  for  all  the  workers  to  give  overtime. 
Also  during  the  winter  months  the  workers  are 
frequently  kept  hours  over  time  because  of  the 
delay  in  trains  caused  by  the  heavy  storms. 

Although  these  difficulties  are  fully  recognized, 
yet  in  adjusting  salaries  no  adequate  compensation 
is  made  on  their  account,  because  of  the  lack  of 
money.  However,  from  the  workers  themselves 
comes  no  word  of  complaint.  It  is  their  loyal, 
steady,  efficient  work  which  made  it  possible  for 
a  customs  inspector  to  say  when  shown  a  Travelers 
Aid  badge:  “That  badge  is  as  good  as  gold.” 
It  is  our  job  to  make  that  emblem  of  safety, 
good  judgment,  accuracy  and  courtesy  one  of 
“gold”  to  all  people  passing  through  the  stations 
and  docks  of  the  City  of  Boston. 

OLIVE  M.  JAQUES.

Tear-stained  Edward 


When  he  was  brought  into  our  office,  seven  year 
old  Edward  presented  a  tear-stained  countenance 
liberally  smeared  with  the  coating  of  a  chocolate 
jelly  apple  which  he  clutched  in  one  small  fist. 
In  the  other  was  a  paper  bag  filled  with  the  es¬ 
sentials  of  life,  a  salt  box  containing  small  childish 
treasurers,  a  clean  shirt  and  a  pair  of  socks.  This, 
he  claimed,  had  been  packed  by  his  mother  pre¬ 
paratory  to  a  trip  to  the  Cape.  She  had  preceded 
and  arranged  for  him  to  follow  that  day.  Failure' 
on  our  part  to  purchase  immediately  a  ticket  for 
his  use  ended  in  such  a  gale  of  tears  and  sobs 
as  only  an  angry  and  spoiled  little  boy  can  pro¬ 
duce. 

Edward  gave  his  address  unsuspiciously,  how¬ 
ever,  and  was  taken  home  to  a  nearby  suburb 
by  one  of  our  workers.  There  his  mother  told 
us  that  he  had  had  infantile  paralysis  some  time 
ago,  and  while  physically  he  had  made  a  very 
good  recovery,  he  lacked  mental  and  emotional 
balance.  The  parents  were  discouraged  with 
the  difficulties  he  presented,  and  were  very  thank¬ 
ful  for  a  chance  to  receive  the  help  of  the  Children’s 
Aid  Association  in  solving  Edward's  peculiarities. 


The  Prosperous  in  Deep  Trouble 

It  is  not  only  the  poor  and  unfortunate  in  this 
life  to  whom  the  Travelers  Aid  Society  is  of  as¬ 
sistance  Frequently  prosperous  intelligent  people 
find  themselves  in  deep  trouble  and  in  need  of 
our  help  Such  was  the  case  with  Mrs.  William 
Harris  who  with  her  husband  was  returning  from 
their  summer  home  in  New  Hampshire  to  Con¬ 
nection' 

Mr.  Harris  had  a  sudden  asthmatic  attack  and 
died  within  a  few  minutes.  The  shock  was  so 
great,  that  Mrs.  Harris  was  unable  to  plan  at  all 
and  turned  to  the  Travelers  Aid  worker  for  com¬ 
fort  and  advice.  It  was  she  who  telephoned  the 
Connecticut  home  and  arranged  with  the  son  and 
daughter  to  come  at  once  and  it  was  she  who 
finally  quieted  the  grieved  wife  and  remained  with 
her  until  midnight  when  the  family  came.  Again 
it  was  the  Travelers  Aid  worker  who  found  what 
arrangements  had  been  made  by  the  medical 
authorities  and  told  the  family  just  what  to  do. 

Although  all  were  well  educated  and  capable  and 
the  daughter  a  college  instructor,  yet  at  such  a 
time  they  found  our  help  almost  indispensible. 
Later  Elizabeth  Harris  wrote  us  a  grateful  note 
saying  that  the  comfort  and  help  of  the  Travelers 
Aid  in  their  time  of  grief  would  never  be  forgotten.

A  Revolt  against  Cruelty 

Beatrice,  an  Italian  girl  of  fifteen,  had  been 
beaten  at  home  by  her  father,  who  also  on  one 
occasion  had  broken  her  stepmother’s  jaw.  Bea¬ 
trice  left  home  and  went  to  live  with  her  grand¬ 
mother,  but  the  old  woman  lived  in  a  hovel  and 
she  was  brutal  too,  so  Beatrice  decided  she  had 
better  run  away  from  such  misery  and  unhappi¬ 
ness.  Desperate  and  bareheaded  she  appeared  in 
the  South  Station  one  afternoon  where  she  begged 
a  ticket  for  Providence  from  a  man  whom  she 
met  in  the  station. 

Just  here  the  worker  interfered  and  took  Bea¬ 
trice  away  to  a  comfortable  home  for  girls  until 
the  Travelers  Aid  Society  could  find  out  just  what 
the  matter  was.  The  result  was  that  the  Society 
for  the  Prevention  of  Cruelty  to  Children  became 
interested  in  Beatrice  and  her  difficulties  and  ob¬ 
tained  a  court  order  for  her  transfer  to  the  home 
of  a  respectable  and  kindly  relative  where  she 
ought  to  be  happy. 


The  Intricacies  of  an  Address 

Late  one  evening  last  fall  a  pretty  young  Italian 
immigrant  girl  was  brought  to  the  Travelers  Aid 
desk.  She  spoke  no  English  and  the  only  address 
she  had  was  58  “Aver”  Street,  to  which  a  per¬ 
fectly  strange  man  was  offering  to  take  her.  Our 
worker  decided  that  by  “Aver”  Street  was  meant 
Havre  Street,  East  Boston,  and  telephoned  the 
police  to  locate  her  relatives.  The  report  came 
back  that  the  family  had  moved  to  365  Highland 
Avenue,  Somerville,  but  the  Somerville  police  re¬ 
ported  that  there  was  no  such  number  on  that 
street. 

The  little  Italian  was  cared  for  over  night,  and 
the  next  morning  another  worker  went  personally 
to  58  Havre  Street,  but  could  only  learn  that  the 
family  had  moved  to  Somerville  where  they  had 
bought  a  house  on  Highland  Avenue.  The  post 
office  was  then  consulted,  but  the  family  had  not 
thought  to  leave  a  forwarding  address.  Next  in 
order  of  consultation  was  the  assessors’  office  in 
Somerville,  but  they  had  no  record  of  the  trans¬ 
action  as  it  had  been  completed  since  April  first 
of  this  year  when  their  records  were  made  out. 

On  their  suggestion,  however,  the  worker  con¬ 
sulted  the  Registry  of  Deeds  of  Middlesex  County 
who  found  the  transfer  of  property  to  the  girl’s 
uncle  on  Highland  Avenue.  They  did  not  have 
the  number,  but  were  able  to  give  a  sufficient  des- 


[12] 


cription  so  that  the  assessors’  office  could  state 
that  the  house  must  be  one  of  two  numbers  on 
Highland  Avenue. 

Within  five  minutes  the  aunt  was  reached 
through  the  cooperation  of  the  Somerville  telephone 
information,  and  within  an  hour  a  very  excited 
family  group  came  to  claim  the  young  immigrant, 
quite  uncomprehending  as  to  how  so  much  con¬ 
fusion  could  have  been  caused  by  so  simple  a  thing 
as  a  change  of  address.

Travelers  Aid  Society  of  Boston: 

Perhaps  it  may  interest  you  to  know  of  the  safe 
arrival  at  her  sister’s  of  —  — ,  a  Finnish  girl 
of  eighteen  years  of  age.  I  had  driven  to  Duquoin 
to  meet  some  of  my  people,  and  she  had  become 
acquainted  with  them  on  the  train.  She  had  no 
money  and  was  worried  about  how  to  get  to  Zeig- 
ler,  as  she  could  get  no  further  than  Christopher 
tonight  and  had  no  funds  to  pay  for  lodgings. 

We  made  her  understand  that  we  would  see  that 
she  got  to  her  sister’s,  and  drove  down  there  and 
inquired  around  till  finally  I  located  her  sister  and 
asked  if  she  had  a  young  sister  and  took  her  out 
to  the  auto  to  be  sure  she  was  the  right  party. 
The  girl  was  greatly  pleased  to  find  herself  safely 
at  her  sister’s.  All  I  had  to  go  by  was  your 
printed  slip  to  conductor  with  her  name  and  the 
No.  124  Maple  Street,  Zeigler,  which  by  the  way 
was  not  the  correct  address,  but  I  found  some 
Finnish  people  who  directed  me  to  the  right  place. 

Zeigler  as  well  as  other  coal  camps  is  rather 
a  tough  town;  and  I  thought  it  safest  to  see  the 
girl  to  her  sister’s  and  make  sure  that  your  in¬ 
tentions  and  directions  were  carried  out. 

I  have  traveled  considerable  and  have  always 
observed  that  your  society  does  a  great  amount 
of  good  to  travelers;  girls  and  women  especially; 
and  I  wish  to  say  that  as  an  American  born  citizen 
of  our  good  old  U.  S.  A. — Hats  off  to  the  Travelers 
Aid  Society. 

Very  respectfully, 

(Signed)  WM.  V.  WALKER.

A  Runaway  Author 

Late  one  evening  the  Travelers  Aid  worker  in 
the  North  Station  saw  a  pretty  bobbed  haired  girl, 
of  about  seventeen,  come  from  a  Portland  train. 
She  confided  in  the  worker  that  she  was  an  orphan 
and  had  just  nursed  her  mother  during  a  long  ill¬ 
ness  with  tuberculosis.  For  the  short  time  since 
her  mother’s  death  she  had  lived  with  an  aunt, 
who  could  not  afford  to  keep  her.  She  therefore 
decided  to  try  her  fortune  in  a  large  city. 

She  was  an  ambitious  youngster  who  had  almost 
finished  high  school  and  had  vague  ideas  of  fame 
as  a  lawyer  or  short  story  writer — she  wasn’t  sure 
which. 

Investigations  in  Portland  proved  that  all  the 
names  and  addresses  which  she  had  given  were 
fictitious,  but  finally  Catherine’s  inadvertent  men¬ 
tion  of  an  uncle  who  had  lost  one  hand  and  worked 
for  the  railroad  proved  the  clew  which  unraveled 
the  mystery.  This  man  was  located,  and  through 
him  her  mother,  who  came  for  her  daughter  the 
next  day. 

The  truth  of  the  story  was  that  Catherine  had 
been  playing  truant  from  school  and  spending  the 
days  at  a  friend’s  house  making  cake  and  candy. 
The  report  cards  were  due  the  next  day,  which 
would  reveal  this  fact.  Moreover  a  note  had  been 
discovered  in  her  handwriting  in  school  which 
contained  reference  to  some  boys.  This  had  caused 
trouble  and  the  teacher  had  threatened  to  inform 
the  mother  the  next  day.  Catherine  decided  the 
time  to  attain  fame  and  fortune  had  arrived  and 
left  Portland. 

She  was  very  glad  to  return  home  with  her 
mother  and  agreed  not  to  be  truant  again.  The 
note,  it  seems,  was  an  innocent  affair,  being  simply 
a  part  of  one  of  the  short  stories  which  she  in¬ 
tended  to  place  with  the  “Saturday  Evening  Post.’’ 
Catherine  had  done  well  in  her  studies  and  was 
working  every  afternoon  and  giving  such  satis¬ 
faction  to  her  employer  that  the  latter  agreed  to 
try  to  give  her  a  little  supervision  and  help  her 
over  these  temporary  difficulties.
