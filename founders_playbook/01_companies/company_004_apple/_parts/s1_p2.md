## E. PRODUCT RECONSTRUCTION

STATUS: WRITTEN 2026-09-26

**Scope rule for this section, and why it is stated before the evidence.** The Stage-1 window closes at 1977-01-03 (argued in `s1_p1`, §STAGE BOUNDARY JUSTIFICATION). Every artifact that prints a maker's configuration, price ladder or delivered form post-dates that edge — the designer's own article is May 1977, the corporation's first advertisement June 1977, the re-run and the order form July 1977. This section therefore reconstructs the product in two registers and never blends them: **E.1–E.3, what the in-window record attests**; **E.4–E.6, what the maker printed after the edge, admitted as a labeled later witness to the shape of the thing the window produced** (the boundary-cost rule the sibling part accepts for itself at `(Apple S1 §Boundary cost, s1_p1)`, and the reason the interrupted pass's records S1P1-05 and S1P1-06 are corrected as S1P1-CF-10 rather than quietly re-dated). No line below is a Stage-1 fact unless its document is inside the window.

### E.1 The maker's own description of the first product

The only first-party description of the Apple-I anywhere in the cached corpus is signed by its designer in his own byline, with his employer's address, in BYTE May 1977 (`sources/ia_byte_1977/byte-1977-05.txt`; dossier record **A2-25**, configuration **A2-26**): "The Apple-I, my first video oriented single board computer, was designed late in 1975 and sold by word of mouth throughout California and later nationwide through retail computer stores." And: "The Apple-I video computer board was originally intended as a television terminal product which could also operate in a stand alone mode without much in the way of memory, although it did have a processor, space for 8 K bytes of 4 K dynamic memory chips, and its shared video generation and dynamic memory refresh logic. **Apple-I was sold as a completely assembled and tested processor board with a price under $700 at the retail level.**"

Class: **FOUNDER CLAIM (contemporaneous)** — printed while the company was two months old and the outcome unknown, which is a better class than memoir, and still one voice. Confidence: **High** that the sentences were printed under his name; **Medium** for the design date, which A2-25 records as corroborated by *nothing* in this corpus; **Medium** for "under $700", which is a founder's rounded aggregate characterisation, not a published price schedule — A2-23 establishes that no 1976 print states one.

**The intent clause is the section's first finding, and it is anti-hagiographic on its face.** "Originally intended as a television terminal product which could also operate in a stand alone mode" has the first product conceived as a *peripheral substitute*, not as a computer. That is the opposite of the later story about setting out to build a personal computer, and it is the designer's own contemporaneous statement about his own intent — the only evidence class available on intent at all (`independence_note`: one voice, one document; no corroboration asserted).

### E.3 Reconstructing the delivered contents of the Apple-I by difference

The corpus never itemises what an Apple-I buyer received. It does itemise the successor, and the successor's own price table separates board from box, which permits a bounded reconstruction rather than a guess. Apple's June 1977 "Apple II Price List" (A2-28, `sources/ia_byte_1977/byte-1977-06.txt`) prints 4K system **$1,298.00** against 4K board-only **$598.00**, a difference of **$700** for "the case, keyboard, power supply, two game paddles and a demonstration cassette". Class **ESTIMATE / DERIVED**, arithmetic `1298 − 598 = 700`, confidence **High** that the subtraction is right and **Medium** that the 1977 contents list maps onto the 1976 machine at all — which the corpus does not establish. What follows is therefore negative and firm: on the maker's own later numbers, an integrated case and keyboard were a **$700 add-on**, i.e. roughly the entire retail band Wozniak claims for the Apple-I. **The Apple-I is thus reconstructed as a board, not as an appliance** — consistent with its description as a "processor board" (A2-26), with the user's modified-television output path (A2-22), and with the 1976 price geography (E.5). It is not consistent with "computer in a case", and no 1976–77 document says that.

### E.4 The $666.66 disposition — folklore, and here is the local test

Re-run against `sources/` this pass: the string `666.66` occurs in **no** cached document; every appearance of the figure in the repository is commentary *about its absence* (`research/A2_periodical_archive_mine.md` A2-23, U-A2-4, DG-4; `research/D_adversarial.md` E-10 and L-2). The only dollar-shaped 666 in the whole cached periodical corpus is a competitor's strike-through anchor in BYTE December 1976 (`sources/ia_byte_1976/byte-1976-12.txt`, advertiser block, OCR display line "a $666 value / only $595") — an **IMSAI 8080 bundle discount, not an Apple price**, on a page whose advertiser identity is not legible. Accordingly: the repeating-decimal price is recorded as **FOUNDER CLAIM / folklore at best, UNKNOWN as a figure**, never as FACT, and never as a Stage-1 price. The route that could still settle it is named, not skipped: Kilobaud's 1976 issues carry no text layer in this cache (A2 DG-4, importance HIGH) — see the UNTRIED list, §E-U.

### E.6 The window contains two products, and the record shows the swap

The standard account of Stage 1 ends with one machine. The dated print ends the window with the first machine already being retired from the maker's plans. The ladder is short and every rung is a document: 1976-04-30 the 6502 board is a loanable guest at another club (A2-19) → 1976-09/11 dealers advertise and mail-order it (A2-08, A2-12) → **1976-11-20 two founders bring a "prototype Apple-ll" to a Palo Alto motel room and the editor spends the evening programming on it** (A2-31, CONTEMPORARY OBSERVATION by a named publisher who was there; **High** that it was written within five months, **Medium** on detail — `independence_note`: single witness, and that witness is a friend of the subjects, priced into provenance not into corroboration counts) → 1976-12 "Apple Computers" appears on the committed exhibitor list for the first West Coast Computer Faire (A2-16, A2-41) → April 1977 pre-announcement and the designer's article → June/July 1977 the company advertises **only** the Apple II, and the Apple-I vanishes from the price list, the specification box and the order form (A2-72). A2-72's own formulation is the finding: "the first product's ending is visible only as silence in the maker's own price list." No terminated-product notice, no trade-in, no clearance of Apple-I stock exists in the cached record. Stage 1's product is therefore not "the Apple-I"; it is **a board that was being replaced by a system while Stage 1 was still open** — and the decision to replace it is invisible except as an absence.

## F. CUSTOMER

STATUS: WRITTEN 2026-09-26

### F.1 The first-customer field stays UNKNOWN, and this is the reason

The keystone claim of Apple's founding story — **fifty Apple-1 boards at $500 apiece, ordered by the Byte Shop in July 1976** — is not a Stage-1 fact and cannot be made into one from this corpus. `research/D_adversarial.md` **L-1** chased the lineage to its carriers and found no document: the designer's own contemporaneous sentence names no buyer, no quantity and no price (A2-25/A2-26, read at line level); the Byte Shop's own 1976 advertising never mentions Apple in any of the twelve cached issues, and appears repeatedly selling *other* lines (authorized dealer for Processor Technology at five, then six Bay Area addresses; a ten-outlet chain directory); the commercial custodian of the transaction, an auction house's 2026 essay, states the numbers as "fifty computers at five hundred dollars apiece, to retail at $666.66" while **relying on direct conversation with the merchant, with no invoice, dated correspondence, printed volume or magazine entry anchoring the figures — and it dates the deal "Spring 1976", not July**. A second carrier gives 50 boards at 25,000 USD total, i.e. a different arithmetic frame for the same alleged order.

Class: **FOUNDER CLAIM / third-party-embedded merchant memory (retrospective)**; Confidence: **Low** on every element; the first-customer field: **not establishable in the Stage-1 record** — which is a statement about this archive, not a claim that no order occurred (§2, §12). What may be printed is the *relationship*, which is genuinely well evidenced and is kept separate from the transaction: Jobs and Terrell named as peers by an editor on the WESCON floor in September 1976 (A2-15); "Apple Computers" and the Byte Shop circuit co-listed in the same December 1976 print (A2-16, A2-13); the Byte Shop chain documented as a live, multi-store, trans-Pacific franchise in the month the successor was introduced (A2-35, post-window witness). **No number from the canon may be printed as FACT in this section, in §K, or in the timeline** (L-1's merge decision, adopted here).

Three structural readings follow, none of which requires hindsight. (i) **The earliest documented customer of Apple's output is a retailer's *intention*, printed as an advertisement** — the record contains offers, not orders. A2-62 states the corpus-wide extent: the machine is present in the stock lists of at least four separately-owned concerns while "no document in these files records a purchase, a quantity, an invoice, a shipment, a payment or a credit arrangement between Apple and any of them" (**FACT (documented extent), High**). (ii) The brand's earliest *seller* is on the East Coast, which inverts the geography of every California-first account (A2-08 vs A2-12, and see `research/D_adversarial.md` **L-4**). (iii) **No 1976 document evidences an authorized Apple dealer relationship of any kind**: the New York advertiser itemises the four lines it is an authorized dealer for, and Apple is not among them; the Berkeley advertiser claims no relationship either (L-4, adopted as this section's governing constraint). "First dealer", "first store" and "dealer network" are therefore refused for 1976 in both directions — the Byte Shop-first story *and* its rivals.

### F.3 The buyer as a countable population, in-period

What "the customer" meant as knowable in 1976 is one quantified self-survey, and it is the club's own: at the 1976-06-09 meeting, "That totals 101 systems up and running out the the group. About 250 hobbyists were at this meeting. A similar survey in the October 15, 1975 meeting turned up 38 systems with about 80 hobbyists attending" (A2-61; **CONTEMPORARY OBSERVATION, Medium-High**; **independence_note: one organiser's count, no independent count behind it — the record-selection null applies in full**). DERIVED from those printed rows: systems per meeting `101/38 = 2.66×`, attendance `250/80 = 3.13×` over about eight months. The terms on which such a buyer actually transacted are printed twice, by the firm that advertised the Apple-1 by mail: **10% off the manufacturer's current list, plus 2% shipping and handling, personal cheques held three weeks, minimum order $80.00, California residents add sales tax** (A2-12, A2S-04; and the same formula at A2-39 with the tax quantified at 6%). Two consequences are arithmetic, not testimony. **A single board was orderable**: at the designer's own "under $700" band the $80 minimum is not binding, and A2-40 computes the delivered figure — `700 × 0.90 = 630`, `+ 6% tax 37.80`, `+ 2% carriage 12.60` ≈ **$680.40 delivered** (Class **ESTIMATE / DERIVED**, confidence **Medium**, *because its base is a founder's rounded number*). The discount was largely consumed by tax and carriage: **the terms of trade, not the list price, set the real cost** of a 1976 hobby purchase.

### F.4 The finding: the customer record and the memoir record describe different mechanisms

The founder's contemporaneous sentence describes a **social** channel — "sold by word of mouth throughout California and later nationwide through retail computer stores" (A2-25). The two best surviving **commercial** artifacts of the same period describe an **enumerated** one: BYTE's April 1977 national, state-by-state computer-store directory, roughly sixty named outlets across 22 jurisdictions including Canada and Japan, of which about thirty-five lines are Byte Shop outlets with street address, telephone and a named manager (A2-35, `sources/ia_byte_1977/byte-1977-04.txt`, **FACT (the printed list), High**, corroborated at earlier dates and smaller scale by two independent printings of the same circuit — BYTE September 1976's distributor list and its November 1976 Bay Area list, A2-09/A2-13; **independence_note: three separate documents, but two of the three are the same magazine's own pages, so the directory is counted as one origin with duration, not three**), and Apple's own June 1977 mail-order form, which takes the order directly, pays continental freight, and throws in a carrying case the company values at $50 (A2-29).

The tension is the finding, and it is threefold. **(a) Sequence.** L-10 shows the founder's own ordering — "California **and later** nationwide" — is contradicted by the dated ladder: New York advertising in September 1976 precedes Berkeley in November 1976, and both precede the maker's own advertising by nine months. The class call is recorded exactly as the adversarial pass sets it: the sentence is usable as a **FOUNDER CLAIM (contemporaneous)** about channel *type*, while the *sequence* must be built from the dated advertisements. **(b) Mechanism.** "Word of mouth" implies a maker present at each transaction; a printed directory plus a postage-paid order form imply a maker one step away from the customer, selling into a circuit it did not own and, from June 1977, competing with that circuit on its own resellers' terms (A2-29's own reading). **(c) Scale vocabulary.** The memoir counts in boards; the print counts in outlets, jurisdictions and named managers. Neither is wrong, but they are not the same measurement, and no document in the corpus reconciles them. **Best-supported interpretation:** Apple's first distribution was a **multi-dealer ramp through businesses that were themselves weeks old** — Kentucky Fried Computers is documented six months before its Apple advertisement as a storeless two-man mail-and-telephone operation that "plan[s] to open a store later this year" (A2-39) — and not a single founding order. **Residual uncertainty: high.** An invoice, Terrell's own papers, or the unread 1976-04-01 instrument could still establish a transaction; this corpus neither establishes nor refutes one (U-A2-2's residual, adopted).

**F.5 What this section cannot say about customers at all (§7 knowability).** NOT KNOWABLE from Stage-1 print: total number of purchasers; repeat purchase; returns, cancellations or refunds; the ratio of enthusiast to non-enthusiast buyers; whether any buyer was a business; geographic distribution of end users; and payment terms between Apple and its resellers (only *retail-facing* terms are printed). UNKNOWN with reason: every one of these requires a company sales ledger, and no Apple ledger of any period exists in this corpus or was reachable (see the register rows requested for `data_gaps.csv`).

**Record-selection null (§2).** The surviving customers are those who could buy advertising space or write a letter to a club newsletter. A hobbyist who bought one board at a counter in 1976 leaves no trace in this family, and a dealer who ordered and *failed* to sell would leave none either — the corpus has no mechanism for printing a bad outcome. The absence of complaint, return or failure evidence in §F is therefore a property of the source class, and is not offered as evidence that the product satisfied its buyers.

## G. SUPPLY / HOST SIDE

STATUS: WRITTEN 2026-09-26

**Adaptation note (§7).** The hardware frame for this section is *prototypes, component sourcing, yield*. The Apple Stage-1 record supplies the first and third only as absences, and the second only from the **outside** — prices other people printed for the same components. So the section is reconstructed in two halves: **G.1–G.3 the supply side as the firm's own capacity (almost entirely unrecorded)**, and **G.4–G.6 the host side as the apparatus that supplied Apple with something it could not supply itself: circulation, an audience, and shelf**. The standard frame's implicit assumption — that a manufacturer's procurement paper exists — does not hold here, and saying so is part of the deliverable (§12 `UNKNOWN` as a complete answer).

### G.1 Assembly: the one claim, and everything it does not carry

The maker's only statement about production is six words inside a founder's article: Apple-I "was sold as a completely assembled and tested processor board" (A2-26, BYTE May 1977; **FOUNDER CLAIM (contemporaneous)**, confidence **Medium** as to the words, **High** that they were printed). "Assembled and tested" asserts a manufacturing step and a quality step. No document in the corpus names who performed them, where, at what rate, with what yield, on what labor basis, or with what reject rate. No 1976 document of any origin states Apple's output capacity, and no in-window document counts units; the circulating "about 200 Apple-1s" is wiki-derived, whose only cited source is a 2011 Wikipedia retrieval (`research/D_adversarial.md` **L-9**), and L-9's merge decision binds this file: a unit count may appear only as an ESTIMATE with its derivation and carrier named, or as UNKNOWN, **and may not be used as a denominator in §L** — which is where such a number usually smuggles in, to make an alleged fifty-board order look like a majority of output. The Apple-1 is also *not* documented as a kit: the corpus's kit/assembled pairs belong to other firms (Sol-20 $995 kit / $1,495 assembled, A2-45; Xitan $1,369 / $1,749, A2-47), and Apple's own board-only option appears only with the successor in June 1977 (A2-28).

### G.2 Components: priced by everyone except Apple

The strongest supply evidence in this corpus is that Apple's inputs were **commoditised and publicly priced** in the same pages, which is what makes a one-board design purchasable at all.

A2-28's other derived line cuts the opposite way for the *customer*: undoing the stated "20% savings" implies the undiscounted equivalent of a 16K set is `600 / 0.8 = $750` (**ESTIMATE / DERIVED, Medium** — the ad does not say which column carries the discount, and that ambiguity is recorded rather than resolved).

### G.3 Labour, and the function nobody is credited with

The earliest printed personnel list associated with the company is a four-name acknowledgement box in Apple's own voice, May 1977: Allen Baum "for originating the Apple-// debug software, Doug Kraul for helpful suggestions on the I/O structure, and Randy Wigginton and Chris Espinosa for many long and late hours testing the Apple BASIC" (A2-33; **FOUNDER CLAIM (contemporaneous, first-party)**, **High** as printed). A2-33 draws the boundary this section must hold: **no title, wage, hire date or headcount appears anywhere**, and employee-numbering claims (e.g. "employee #6") exist nowhere in these files, so any ordering claim about these four is unsupported. On the founder side, the one dated observational record of labour division shows the designer *and* programmer working inside the machine — Helmers records that after their evening session "Steve Wozniak recoded the program using the 6502 processor's assembly language facility" (A2-59, A2-31) — and names no function for Jobs. A2-59's negative is worth its weight: "neither Wozniak's May 1977 article nor Apple's June 1977 advertisement attributes any function to Jobs. The famous role division is absent from every 1976–77 text on disk." The 1976 duties that *are* recorded — Wayne "Mechanical Engineering and Documentation", Wozniak "Electrical Engineering", Jobs "Electrical Engineering and Marketing" — come from an auction catalogue's description of an instrument this project has not read (`research/C_corporate_legal_org.md` C-29/C-34), i.e. **Tier-3 cataloguing of a Tier-1 object**, and is carried here as the catalogue's claim, not as a Stage-1 fact.

### G.4 Host side I — the club as infrastructure

Before any commercial apparatus attached to it, the machine's distribution medium was social and physical: a club newsletter carried news of it (reprinted from *another* club's letter, A2-19), and the designer drove it to the meeting — "We are grateful to STEVE WOZNIAK for providing transportation." That single clause is the corpus's only in-window evidence of how a unit physically moved to an audience (**FACT (as printed)**, **High** that it was printed; **Medium** as an account of a distribution mechanism — n=1). The same newsletter network then printed a member's technical letter about the machine (A2-22) and listed "Apple Computers" among roughly twenty concerns in the field (A2-21). The arc is short enough to measure: the club's editing postbox (Mountain View), the first Byte Shop storefront (Mountain View, El Camino Real) and the company's later address of record (Cupertino) sit inside a thirty-mile arc, and A2-19's own gloss is the right frame — "this is a market that fits on one magazine page."

### G.5 Host side II — the magazine that hosted the field

BYTE was not a neutral mirror of Apple's supply position; it was a piece of it. It had run "ON DISPLAY AT / BYTE SHOP" vendor advertisements for three months before the Apple-1's first appearance in its pages (A2-07, **four issues = one campaign, counted as one source with a duration, not four**), its October 1976 editorial records a general-business weekly having profiled the Byte Shop in July 1976 and describes the shop as "one of the largest retail outlets among the more than 250 stores coast to coast which regularly stock BYTE" (A2-10, **CONTEMPORARY OBSERVATION**, High that BYTE reported it, UNKNOWN what that article said and whether it mentioned Apple at all — the dated pointer is recorded in the UNTRIED list, §G-U), and it gave the company's designer **page 3** of its May 1977 issue (A2-48). A2-48's independence warning is the load-bearing caveat for §L as well: the magazine's editor was a participant — he was the third man at the 1976-11-20 keyboard — so "page-3 placement in a magazine whose editor is a participant is a *relationship* datum, not an endorsement datum."

### G.6 Host side III — shelf, and how fast shelf can move

The retail circuit is where supply became visible, and it was neither exclusive nor stable. Computer Mart of New York carried the Apple-1 in a brand block of eleven competing makers from the first month (A2-08, A2-11); by May 1977 the same store's list runs to twenty-five named lines, "of which Apple is the 17th — `1/25 = 4%` of shelf advertising share at a store that had carried the machine since September 1976" (A2-67, **ESTIMATE / DERIVED**, High as a print observation of relative prominence and nothing else). The same page-set proves turnover: the December 1976 brand block **swaps Sphere for Veras**, so "a brand's presence in one month does not prove continuity of supply" (A2-17) — and it proves that a competing new machine was being introduced into the same stores at the same moment (A2-63's Gulliver line, April 1977). By April 1977 the host circuit was itself a growth thesis being argued by other people: Computer Shack's president, "planning to double or quadruple the present number of computer stores in existence through a nationwide franchise program" (A2-66, **CONTEMPORARY OBSERVATION**, High as printed intention), with Radio Shack's Honolulu franchisees travelling to the Faire as prospective stockists (A2-44, one report, single origin).

**G-U (UNTRIED, web budget 0).** (i) Component procurement: no chip-distributor or broker record was sought for Apple; route is a California trade-journal and UCC-1 search family not present in `sources/`. (ii) Assembly contract: no printed evidence of a contract manufacturer was chased; a period trade profile of hobby-board assembly houses would be the route. (iii) The July 12 1976 Business Week item named at A2-10 — dated, named, and unretrieved (A2 §Data gaps DG-11). Each is recorded as a pointer, not as a null.

**Record-selection null (§2).** Every supply fact in this section that is *not* UNKNOWN was printed by somebody else: a magazine, a club, or a retailer's price sheet. The firm's own supply interior — what it bought, at what price, in what quantity, from whom, with what reject rate, on what credit — leaves **zero** trace in the four cached document families, and no surviving record of that class is known to this project. That asymmetry, not the $25-per-1K arithmetic, is the honest headline of Apple's Stage-1 supply side: the reconstruction is possible only on the *host* half, because hosts are the ones who printed.

### H.2 What an in-period observer could actually know

The category itself was unsettled in the window, and the settlement is datable in this corpus. Carl Helmers' April 1977 column defines the "**appliance computer**" in retail terms — "by definition a completed system which is purchased off the retail shelf, taken home, plugged in and used" — and hedges that the machine he was shown in November 1976 "may be the first product to fully qualify" (A2-31, **CONTEMPORARY OBSERVATION by a named publisher who had a personal relationship with the subjects**, priced into provenance rather than into corroboration; High for the words, Medium for the judgment). Two consequences bear on market-knowability. First, a 1976 observer could not have sized a market whose boundary nobody had agreed on: the same machines were being counted as "video terminals", "kit computers", "boards" and "systems", and the corpus's own price rows show that kit-versus-assembled was the operative axis, not personal-versus-business. Second, the genesis narrative was already a commercial product before any company supplied one: the Faire's February 1977 flyer opens "**San Francisco Bay Area — Where It All Started** — Has Its First Home Computing Convention" (A2-42), which A2-42 correctly files as "evidence that a genesis narrative was already being sold before any company had an origin story to sell" — context for this dataset's own hindsight problem, and a warning against treating 1977 regional pride as a market measurement.

**Franchising was the live channel thesis of the period, argued by other people (1977 print).** Computer Shack's president planned to "double or quadruple the present number of computer stores in existence" (A2-66, High as printed intention), and Radio Shack's Honolulu franchise principals "had flown to San Francisco especially for the Faire… anxious to carry computers in their store" (A2-44). The inference that survives the firewall is narrow and arithmetical: a columnist could contemplate *doubling or quadrupling* the store population, therefore the population was small enough to count — which is precisely what A2-35's directory does. **No 1977 print in this cache connects Radio Shack and Apple**, and the possibility is recorded as un-evidenced, not as unlikely.

### H.3 What was NOT knowable in-period, and what is not knowable now

`KNOWABLE` in-window (with the documents above): competitor asking prices; one club's installed base and its processor mix; a magazine's own store circuit; the existence of an organised retail channel with named managers and telephone numbers; dealer terms of trade (10% off list, 2% carriage, three-week cheque clearance, $80 minimum, 6% California tax — A2-12/A2-39/A2-40); the dates and billed scale of one trade show.

`NOT KNOWABLE` from any surviving public record of this period: total national unit sales of hobby computers; total market revenue; Apple's own share of anything (no sales figure exists for FY1976 or FY1977 — see §K); the number of dealers carrying the product (two named in-window, four named across 1976–77, **no list of Apple's own dealers exists**); the durability of the category; and whether the growth visible in one club's two survey points described an industry at all.

`UNKNOWN` with reason: 1976 revenue and unit volume, because no document in any cached family carries them and the printed series begins at FY1978 in an unsigned 1981 column resting on one witness (§K, U-A2-7).

**Record-selection null (§2), applied hardest here.** The counts that survive are counts of what the winners' chroniclers happened to print: one club's meetings, one magazine's pages, one show's flyers. There was no independent market research in this family for 1976 hobby computers, and nothing in the corpus approximates one — no subscription base, no sell-through report, no wholesaler's tally. A Stage-1 reader who treats the 101-system survey as a market proxy is doing the arithmetic the record never licensed; and a reader who treats the ~35-store directory as a distribution achievement is reading a magazine's own advertising inventory as somebody's sales channel. Both errors are available in this corpus precisely because the surviving numbers look like market data.

**Method for this section, stated because the corpus forces it.** No Apple document in any cached family names a competitor, responds to one, or claims superiority over a named firm; and no 1976–77 document records anyone else reacting to Apple. That is an unusually strong base for 1976 and an unusually weak one for intent: it shows what a buyer was shown alongside an Apple, and nothing about how the firms positioned themselves to each other.

### I.2 The comparator that matters for the first product, and it is in-window

The closest printed competitor to the Apple-I is not a later historian's choice but a June 1976 club news item: "**Electronic Tool Co. has recently introduced a complete microcomputer system, based on the MOS Technology 6502 CPU, for $675** … comes with a 40-key keyboard, a programmable 8-digit display, I/O interfaces, power supply and more. **All systems are fully assembled, tested and ready to run.**" (Homebrew 1976-06-09, Hawthorne CA item; A2-68, `sources/ia_homebrew/hcc0206.txt`, A2S-10; **FACT (as printed)**, High, corroboration **1 advertiser**).

Set against the designer's only printed number — Apple-I "sold as a completely assembled and tested processor board with a price under $700 at the retail level" (A2-26) — the in-period competitive position is reconstructible without hindsight, and it is not a vacant position: A2-68's own reading is that "the ETC-1000 at **$675** is the closest printed 6502-based *complete* system" to the Apple-I's claimed band, that "the 1976 assembled-machine band in these documents runs **$675 → $1,495**", and that "Apple's first product was priced inside an existing 6502-system band, and its second product was priced at the top of it." **INFERENCE** from printed rows, confidence **Medium**; and the firewall clause matters: "Nothing in this supports or undercuts the choice." The one asymmetry the record does license is a product-form point from §E — Apple's claimed figure covered a *board*, while the comparator's covered a keyboarded *system*, so the two asking prices were not for the same object even where they were numerically close.

### I.4 Rivalry inside the language, and a cautionary comparator

Two competitive facts in this corpus are about naming rather than machines, and both are load-bearing for method. (i) **"Zapple" was another company's product line**: BYTE May 1977 carries Technical Design Laboratories' "THE ZAPPLE MONITOR", "ZAPPLE BASIC … $50", "SUPER BASIC … $95" (A2-70, High, corroborated by the same family in the June and July 1977 Xitan ads — **`independence_note`: two advertisers, one product family, and TDL also appears in Computer Mart's stocked-brand list, so it is a competitor and a component of the shelf at once**). Practical consequence: **any grep for `apple` in this period returns other firms' trademarked matter**, alongside the "Big Apple"/"apple pie"/"bad apples" tokens that made the 1976 census work necessary (A2-01…A2-05). (ii) The **garage** was a stock figure of the field before it was Apple's motif: BYTE July 1976 describes Sphere — "all the company consisted of was two people in a garage" (`sources/ia_byte_1976/byte-1976-07.txt`, per `research/D_adversarial.md` L-5, under that file's provisional-issue-date caveat, see `(Apple S1 §Provenance risk, s1_p1)`). Sphere is also in Computer Mart's September 1976 authorized-dealer line and out of the December block. The comparator value is documentary, not moral: a two-person garage company was a **recognisable 1976 type** that retailers stocked and dropped within a quarter, and the trade press had the phrase ready before it had the story.

`NOT ESTABLISHED`: any Apple statement about competition (there is none in the window; the successor's June 1977 advertisement makes category superlatives about itself — "the first personal computer with a fast version of BASIC permanently stored in ROM" — which A2-27 correctly prices as "an advertising claim, not a fact of priority"); any competitor's statement about Apple; price-cutting or other strategic response; relative unit volumes; whether any listed brand was carried *because* customers asked for it. `UNKNOWN` with reason: the competitive sets at **Kilobaud** and **Creative Computing**, the two titles most likely to hold both Apple's own advertising and a fuller 1976 competitor field — Kilobaud's 1976 items carry no text layer in this cache and Creative Computing is unmined (A2 §Contradictions U-A2-4, DG-4; `research/D_adversarial.md` E-10). **Record-selection null (§2).** The competitive record here is a *retail* record, so it over-represents firms that bought magazine pages and under-represents firms that sold direct, by catalogue, or through the S-100 wholesale chain. It also carries no failures: no 1976–77 document in the cache reports a competitor exiting, a board unsold, or a store closing — the December brand-block swap is as close as this archive comes to recording a disappearance, and it records only that a name stopped being printed.

### J.2 The successor's specification, as the window's end-state

The May 1977 article and the June 1977 advertisement agree on an unusual machine for the period: "an 8 slot motherboard for IO which has a fully buffered bus, prioritized interrupts, two prioritized direct memory access (DMA) schemes"; a cassette interface whose "data transfer rate averages over 180 bytes per second"; 4K or 16K DRAM intermixing; roughly 200 bytes of the 8K ROM given to monitor control; "a 5×7 dot matrix in upper case only"; an analog game-paddle circuit on 555-type timer chips (A2-34, **FACT (as printed by the company's designer)**, High; A2-27's post-window spec box adds 8K ROM supplied up to 12K, 4K RAM supplied with 48K advertised, five software-selectable display modes — text 40×24 upper case, colour 40h×48v at **15 colours**, hi-res 280h×192v at 4 colours with 12K RAM minimum). The BASIC present at the 1976-11-20 demonstration is dated inside the window and described in detail by the editor who used it: "the Apple-ll BASIC (which is a 5 K interpreter with 16 bit integer arithmetic)", with which three men wrote "the Color Eater game" in "perhaps 30 to 45 minutes" (A2-31, CONTEMPORARY OBSERVATION, High that it was written within five months; `independence_note`: single witness, and that witness was a friend of the subjects — the file prices this into provenance, not into corroboration).

**A unit-of-measure trap that a careless pass would report as a contradiction.** The June 1977 advertisement prints a "1500 bps cassette interface" while the May article prints "over 180 bytes per second". `1500 / 8 = 187.5` — the two are the same measurement in different bases, and A2-12's conflict row resolves it as "no conflict of fact; a unit-of-measure conflict", the live example of §6's rule that numerals carry their basis (**High**). This section records it because §K and §P-style tables draw on both documents.

### J.3 What the technology did *not* include, per the record

For the first product, in-window: no case, no keyboard, no mass storage, no expansion bus and no bundled software are **documented**; the buyer's remaining purchases are evidenced only for the successor, whose own advertisement states that the customer still needed "a television" and "an inexpensive modulator (not supplied)" (A2-27, High as printed) — and §E's differential arithmetic puts the maker's own 1977 price of case-plus-keyboard-plus-supplies at **$700** (`1298 − 598`, DERIVED). On intellectual property, the record is equally clear by its shape: **the earliest US patent application naming Apple as assignee is dated 1977-04-11**, after the window, and C-35 states the point this dataset needs — "the Apple-1 was never patented" (**FACT via a register aggregator**, Tier 2, confidence Medium: `independence_note` — the search was run against a patent aggregator's rendering and "page 2 was not read", so the null is *this project's search*, not the register's). The first named non-founder assigning work **to the corporation** is dated 1978-02-01 (C-36), i.e. after Stage 1; inside the window the only credited labour is the four-name acknowledgement box (§G.3).

### J.4 Dependence structure, as observable in period

Four dependencies are documented, and all four were shared by every firm in the shelf lists at §I.1 — which is the firewall point: these were the period's defaults, not Apple's vulnerabilities or prescience. (i) **A single CPU vendor**: the 6502 family, installed at `18/101 = 17.8%` of one club's systems in June 1976 and second only to the 8080's 52.5% (A2-61/A2-69, DERIVED, Medium); no second source for the part is documented anywhere in the cache. (ii) **A bus standard somebody else controlled**: the successor's eight-slot board is described in S-100-adjacent terms in the same month the Faire's own programme ran "a conference session on the S-100 bus" (A2-41, FACT as printed). (iii) **The domestic television set** as display device, requiring user modification (A2-22) or an add-on modulator (A2-27). (iv) **The cassette recorder** as storage, whose rate is quoted in both bases at J.2. A fifth dependence is *social*: the machine's earliest documented circulation was as a loanable exhibit carried by its designer to another club's meeting (A2-19).

**J.5 Knowability (§7).** `KNOWABLE` in-window: CPU family, board integration, PROM-monitor architecture, the cassette protocol, the existence of a working 5K integer BASIC by 1976-11-20, and one user's display path. `NOT KNOWABLE`: design provenance (whether the earlier club-machine lineage the field associates with the 6502 boards is relevant — no document in the cache states it), rejected designs, yield, reliability, revision counts, the Apple-I's exact memory complement as shipped, whether any Apple-I unit was field-upgraded, and every question about who wrote what (the acknowledgement box names four hands and gives them no titles). `UNKNOWN` with reason: component supply terms and second sourcing, because no procurement document survives (§G.2).

**Record-selection null (§2).** The technology of Stage 1 survives almost entirely as **somebody else's description of a working object** — a club letter, a firmware article, a reader's letter, an editor's evening — plus one designer's article at the window's edge. That selects ruthlessly for what was *observable at a demonstration* and against what made a product manufacturable: no document in the corpus records an engineering decision, a rejected option, a bug list, or a redesign. A reader should treat §J as a description of what the machine presented, and not as evidence of how it came to be that way.

### K.2 Two live conflicts, held as conflicts

**S1P2-CF-01 — Ronald Wayne's stake and the length of his stay.** CLAIM A: **10%** and **eleven days** — the auction catalogue of the dated primary states "Wayne received a 10% stake" and records the allocation as **Jobs 45 / Wozniak 45 / Wayne 10**, and the record supplies an independent dated object for the interval: the one-page amendment letter of **1976-04-12**, eleven days after the 1976-04-01 instrument, signed by all three (C-27, C-28, C-29). CLAIM B: the circulating canon of **12%** and **"12 days"**, carried by retellings including 2024–26 press (AP-20). WHY THEY DIFFER: arithmetic — `45 + 45 + 10 = 100`, so **12% cannot coexist with the equal split of the balance**; and a rounded day-count in oral transmission. EVIDENCE WEIGHT: A rests on a catalogue's reading of a document this project has **not read**, and B on no document at all. BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: *10% is the better-supported of the two figures and remains a Medium-confidence reading of an unread instrument; the true figure is UNKNOWN until the text is transcribed.* RESIDUAL UNCERTAINTY: the instrument's actual clause form. CONFIDENCE: **Medium on A / Low on B**, and **no average of the two is printed anywhere in this dataset** — an average would be the one treatment the evidence forbids, since the two claims are mutually exclusive allocations of the same hundred percent.

**S1P2-CF-02 — Mike Markkula's terms.** CLAIM A: the standard money-man package — roughly **$250,000** in funding, a **$91,000** personal guarantee, and a **26–28%** stake (book and encyclopaedic retellings). CLAIM B: the entire in-record footprint of the man in this corpus is **one sentence in one unsigned column, BYTE February 1981** — "A C Markkula, 32 years old, who took Apple from a garage operation to its current enviable position, also holds 8.3 million shares" — his **only** occurrence in ~13 MB of cached trade print, and **no appearance in any 1976 or 1977 document** (C-11, A2-61a). WHY THEY DIFFER: A is a downstream narrative that has never been anchored to a document this project can reach; B is a holding reported four years after the events, in a column whose own provenance is "a chain of one unnamed journalist back to a document nobody in this pass has read" (C-08). EVIDENCE WEIGHT: neither side evidences the *terms*; B evidences only that a man of that name held a founder-sized block by December 1980. BEST-SUPPORTED INTERPRETATION: *the terms are UNKNOWN and the entry date is UNKNOWN — "his entry is not dated in any retrieved record" (B §B.4). ### K.3 The one arithmetic cross-check the periodicals permit

A2-71 supplies "the corpus's only usable cross-check on the founders' capital-raising memoir, and it is arithmetic rather than testimony." Contemporaneous print prices the calculator Wozniak later said he sold for $500: "the SR-52 retails for $395 while the HP-65 retails for $795 (although it can sometimes be obtained for $695 if one looks hard enough)" (BYTE April 1976, A2S-01), and the club's own used-market rate: an IMSAI system of "Total retail price $3,066.58" offered at "asking $2,000 … a savings of 35%" (Homebrew 1976-06-09). **DERIVED**: `500/795 = 62.9%`, `500/695 = 71.9%`, against the observed used-market `2000/3066.58 = 65.2%` of retail. A2-71 states the limit in its own words and this file adopts it: "That is **not corroboration of the event — it is a plausibility bound on the number**, and it is the most that periodical evidence can do for this anecdote. The event itself remains UNCORROBORATED." `independence_note`: two independent items in two publications price the *bound*; the anecdote has one carrier, dated 2006.

### K.4 Personal finances of the principals, as in-window facts

**Effectively none of it is documented.** No document in any cached family states what either founder earned, was paid, owned, owed, or could raise: `research/B_founder_forensics.md` §header and A2-61a find the founders' interior leaves "no trace in this family at all", and the same census shows **no founder officer title anywhere** — no occurrence of "president", "vice president", "chairman", "treasurer", "secretary", "board of directors" or "officer" in the corpus is joined to an Apple name (C-13). The correct formulation, and the one used throughout this file, is that **an officer title for 1976–77 is not establishable in the Stage-1 record** — not that none existed; the record class here is a trade archive that printed no corporate personnel news of any kind for this firm. The same rule governs ages, which exist only in 1981 print ("Steve Jobs, 25 years old… Steve Wozniak, 30 years old", A2-60), yielding a **DERIVED** band of roughly 21 and 25.6 at the April 1976 partnership, ±1 year because the printed ages are integers (confidence **Low**, retrospective). No payroll, no lease, no bank instrument, no ledger, and no tax or assessment record for 1976 exists in this corpus (AP-35 and the probe's §verdict exclusion: "no founding-era financial document of any kind surfaced").

### K.5 What the registrant's own filing carries — and the lineage rule that limits it

The earliest EDGAR text for this registrant is dated **1994-01-26** (a `424B5` and a `10-Q` for the quarter ended 1993-12-31); the FY1994 10-K, filed 1994-12-13, is the first 10-K on disk, and the entire 1976–1993 SEC record is paper-only (AP-01, AP-02; EDGAR full-text search has no coverage before 2001, AP-07). From that filing Apple's Stage-1 money is invisible and its Stage-1 legal date is the **one** fact the registrant supplies: "Apple Computer, Inc. … was incorporated under the laws of the State of California on January 3, 1977." Per §3's filing-lineage rule, that sentence and the company's exhibit pointers to paper filings "that exist in the company's filing history but are absent from EDGAR's electronic index" (AP-06) are **one instrument, one lineage** — they do not corroborate each other, and they do not corroborate any figure in K.1. The incorporation date is nonetheless the stage's hardest fact, because it survives on **two documents of unrelated lineage**: the registrant filing and BYTE February 1981's independent "Apple, incorporated in 1977, reported profits of $11.7 million on sales of $117 million for the fiscal year ending September 26, 1980" (D's H-1; `independence_note`: different author, publisher and decade; **High** on the year, **Medium** on the day, which rests on the filing alone). Note what the 1981 column's own numbers do: they are **FY1980** figures, four years past the window, and they are the earliest printed revenue of any kind in this corpus.

### K.6 The company's silence about its own first years is itself a finding

Three company-side voices survive from after the window and **none narrates Stage 1**. The corporation's first national advertisement (June 1977) sells the future — "Clear the kitchen table. Bring in the color TV." — and says nothing about the firm's origin, its partners, or the machine it had stopped listing (§E.6, A2-27, A2-72). Its last pre-IPO advertisement (December 1980) issues the closest thing to a history line the corpus holds, and it is an invitation rather than an account: "Don't let history pass you by. Visit your nearest Apple dealer" (A2-53, **FACT (company's published text)**; note the address change, Stevens Creek Bldg. B3-C → 10260 Bandley Drive, which is the corpus's only physical-premises evidence beyond 1977). And the earliest corporate self-narrative on the archived web, **1996-10-22** at apple.com's root, contains **nothing about 1975–1980** (AP-08/AP-09, "Empty pre-1994 at apple.com root… no Stage-1 content"). The company's own later timeline is therefore company self-narrative at one further remove, and it is silent exactly where §K needs numbers. `independence_note`: no corroboration is asserted for any money figure in this section; where two carriers print the same number (e.g. the offering terms in AP-18 and A2-50) they are **the same document**, and C-08 says so.

**Record-selection null (§2), which is largest here.** The winners' archive preserved the magazine, the club and the dealer; it preserved no company ledger. Consequently §K can prove *that* a business existed — dated instruments, a printed price list two years later, a printed revenue series four years later — and cannot prove *how it was financed*. Nothing above should be read as showing that the founders were poorly capitalised, that Markkula over- or under-paid for control, or that the folklore figures are false; the record is silent, and silence in a survivor's archive is a property of the archive (§12 `UNKNOWN`).

### L.1 What the ladder, taken as a whole, validates

Three propositions are supported without importing a single later fact. (i) **Technical validation preceded commercial validation, and both preceded the maker's own voice.** The machine is documented as *working* in April 1976, as *marketable* in September 1976, and as *the maker's own advertised offer* only in June 1977 — and by then the first product had already left the maker's price list (A2-72). (ii) **The strongest in-window commercial signal is indirect and it is a formula, not a number:** a dealer printing "10% discount from manufacturer's current list prices" proves a manufacturer's list circulated, and A2-23 simultaneously proves the figure is unrecoverable from these files. A validation ladder whose pivotal rung is an *absent number* should be reported that way. (iii) **Standing was conferred by a very small set of observers** — one editor, one club's newsletter, two retailers — and A2-48's warning generalises across this whole table: placement in a magazine whose editor was personally in the business being covered is a relationship datum, and this file prices it into provenance rather than counting it as corroboration.

### L.2 Signals the corpus cannot produce, and what their absence is worth

Absent and unreplaceable: any sell-through figure; any reorder signal from a named dealer (A2-62 — the corpus "names dealers who carried the product and never names an order"); any independent count behind the Faire's announced 12,800 (A2-43, a promoter's self-report); any repeat-purchase, return, warranty or cancellation record; and any negative signal about the product's reception, because the source class printed almost none. On the negative side, the two documented silences must be reported as **silences, not as findings of inactivity**: the club newsletters of 1976-12-10 and 1977-01-19 carry zero body occurrences of the name after five issues carried it across 1976 (C-20), and BYTE's 1977 Apple coverage does not resume until April. High-importance gaps created by this section are handed to the merge as `data_gaps.csv` rows (see the register block), with the follow-up tasks the method requires.

**Record-selection null (§2).** Validation in this corpus is *visible* only where a third party had both a press and a reason to print — so this ladder systematically over-represents the Bay Area hobby press and New York/California retailers, and cannot see the customer who bought quietly, the dealer who ordered and could not sell, or the buyer who returned a board. §L's shape (eleven rungs, nine of them other people's paper, zero of them the company's sales data) is that selection, not Apple's performance.
