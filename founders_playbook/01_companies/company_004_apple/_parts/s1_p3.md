**M.0 The asymmetry that governs this section, and why it is stated first.** §L counted eleven rungs of validation and found nine of them to be other people's paper and none of them the company's sales data `(Apple S1 §L, s1_p2)`. The negative side of the same corpus is thinner still, for a reason that is a property of the source class and not of the venture: **the 1976–77 hobby trade press printed almost no failure data about a company it liked.** Across the 16 cached BYTE issues and 13 cached Homebrew newsletters (`sources/ia_byte_1976/`, `ia_byte_1977/`, `ia_byte_1981/`, `sources/ia_homebrew/`; extent statement at `research/A2_periodical_archive_mine.md` A2-62, A2-76), there is no printed complaint, no returns figure, no cancellation, no warranty record, no reorder refusal and no critical review of either Apple machine inside the window. That is not evidence the venture went well. It is evidence that **the negative layer of this record is absent as a class**, and it means §M must be built from four substitute materials, each labelled for what it is:

1. **Documented terminations and exits** — things the record shows stopping, being abandoned, or being superseded.
2. **Counted silences** — negatives with an explicit denominator and an explicit statement that silence is not inactivity.
3. **Failures of this project's own evidence** — the retractions, demotions and mis-datings the dossiers and the two released parts recorded against themselves.
4. **The market's own churn denominator** — the neighbouring machines and dealers that were present in 1976 print and gone within five years, carried so the survivor's path is not read as the obvious one.

**Record-selection null (§2), §M.** What a real failing venture would have generated in 1976 and does not survive anywhere in this corpus: the order book, the invoice run, the yield and rework rate on hand-built boards, the returns and warranty pile, the bank relationship and any overdraft or refusal behind it, the dealer who ordered and could not sell, and any internal argument about the two product lines. None of these was ever printed by anybody, so §M cannot be read as a base rate for failure, and the near-absence of negatives here measures **the press's willingness to print them**, not the venture's freedom from them.

### M.2 Negatives inside the market and the channel

- **Share of shelf, measured from the only printable denominator.** At the New York retailer that had advertised the machine since September 1976, Apple occupies **one line of about twenty-five** by May 1977 (`1/25 = 4%`, A2-67; s1_p2 record S1P2-10). This is a count of printed brand lines on one page, not of revenue, units or preference; it is carried here as the single most negative-looking *quantified* in-family datum the Stage-1 record supports, and it is not evidence of failure either.
- **No reorder signal exists.** The corpus "names dealers who carried the product and never names an order" (A2-62; `Apple S1 §F.1, s1_p2`). Absence of a reorder is not evidence of no reorder; it is the documented null registered at **U.027**.
- **The pivotal validation rung is an absent number.** The strongest price evidence inside the window is a dealer's *formula* — "Starred items available by mail order at 10% discount from manufacturer's current list prices" (`sources/ia_byte_1976/byte-1976-11.txt`, A2-12) — which proves a manufacturer's list circulated and states no figure. §L.1's point is carried into §M: a validation ladder whose central rung is an absent number cannot be scored as a success or a failure on price.
- **Primacy claims in the channel were contested, and Apple was not the claimant.** Three interested parties print three different scopes of "first" (Terrell's franchise claim, Computer Mart's East-Coast claim, the club's own mail-order precedent) — U-A2-5 in `research/conflicts.csv`, minted at **U.015**. The negative content for Apple is that in 1976 print it is **the stocked object in other people's primacy stories**, never a claimant of any.
- **The Faire number is a promoter's self-report.** The announced 12,800 attendance has no independent count behind it (A2-43; `research/quantitative.csv` row for 1977-04) and the event lies after the boundary in any case; it appears here only to record that the venue in which the successor was billed is unmeasured → **U.022**.

### M.3 The market's churn denominator (so the survivor's path is not the obvious one)

A2-75 collects the dead ends that the same pages print alongside Apple, all as printed fact: a "complete microcomputer system … for $675" from an electrical-tools company (ETC-1000, Hawthorne CA, June 1976, A2-68); the GULLIVER 1 promoted by the very Skokie dealer that stocked Apple (April 1977, A2-63); Vector 1, Quantum/Seals, Tarbell, Oliver, Polymorphic Systems, Morrow, Quay Corp, Compucolor, Alpha Micro and Smoke Signal as stocked lines (A2-67); "BABY by STM Systems" named in the same technical sentence as "the new Apple computer" (A2-14); and a MITS-style line printed with the words **"(Discontinued; limited to stock on hand.)"** in September and October 1976. **Interpretive coda, with mechanism named as UNKNOWN:** the corpus's own later issues show a large part of this advertising base absent by December 1980, so the churn is a documented property of the periodical record. ## N. FOUNDER DECISIONS

STATUS: WRITTEN 2026-09-25

**The evidentiary problem this section must state before it can be read.** A decision is an internal act. The Stage-1 record of Apple contains **no internal document of any kind**: no minutes, no memorandum, no board paper, no diary, no letter written inside the venture and preserved, and — because the only 1976 instruments reach this project through an auction catalogue — no clause of the one paper the founders did sign. Everything callable a "decision" below is therefore **an inference from a dated public act to an act of choosing**, and the inference is weakest exactly where the story is strongest. §10 lists "product history is vague" and "funding history is incomplete" as research-debt triggers; both fire here, and the honest output is a table whose `Rationale` column says **UNKNOWN** more often than a founder-biography table would.

**Three rules applied to every row.** (i) **Date the act, not the memory of the act**: a retrospective account is tagged `RETROSPECTIVE SOURCE` (§6) and can only establish that someone later believed a choice was made. (ii) **Alternatives must be ones the period's own print shows to have been available** — a counterfactual is only admissible if some 1976–77 document proves the option existed and was visible (that test is applied again in §O). (iii) **`Actual result` may reference post-stage outcomes only when labelled `RETROSPECTIVE`**, and where it is labelled so it does no argumentative work (§2).

### O.2 Options the record shows were *not* visible, and which are therefore refused here

- **"The obvious retail chain" and "the obvious bank line."** No 1976–77 document in any cached family shows Apple at a bank, on a credit line, or in a lease. - **"Contract manufacturing at scale."** The corpus prints no supplier, no procurement, no payroll, no yield and no production site (S1P2-11; §G). Any sentence beginning "had they industrialised production earlier…" has no antecedent in the evidence and is excluded from this file. **Refused.**
- **"The obvious mass market."** §H's in-period knowability finding is that an observer could count clubs, dealer pages and one magazine's circulation, and could not size a national market; a counterfactual that imports the 1977–82 consumer curve into 1976 is hindsight contamination by definition. **Refused.**

His 2022 written account is a FOUNDER CLAIM (retrospective) and is used in §M and §K only. **Refused as a counterfactual; carried as a null (U.029).**

### O.3 What §O adds to the volume

Two things, and no more. First, a **capacity argument**: the strongest counterfactual the record actually supports is O-b — that the mail-order mechanism existed, was printed, and was used by a third party to sell Apple's machine in November 1976 before the maker had any printed address or telephone. That is an observation about what was *possible* in the window, not about what was *wise*. Second, a **denominator argument**: O-a, O-c and O-e are all options that the corpus's churn inventory (§M.3) proves were being taken by firms that were gone within five years, so the mere availability of an option is weak evidence that declining it was a mistake. **Mechanism** for why these alternatives existed and were visible: a single trade magazine with an advertising-driven business model, a club press with no editorial distance, and a component market whose prices were public. **Alternative explanation, not excluded:** that the founders' real constraint was hours and hands, not options — for which the corpus has exactly one datum, Sphere printed in 1976 as "all the company consisted of was two people in a garage" (about **another** firm, L-5), and none for Apple. **Confidence:** Medium for the admission test's outcomes; **UNKNOWN** for every counterfactual's effect. §O makes no claim that any of these rows would have changed anything, which is the whole point of the firewall.
