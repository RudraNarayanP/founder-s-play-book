**Dataset:** THE FOUNDER'S PLAYBOOK — forensic longitudinal reconstruction, frozen universe `00_universe/`.
**Company:** Apple (internal key **company_004_apple**) — the entity that traded as **Apple Computer Company** (a partnership) and then as **Apple Computer, Inc.**
**Stage:** 1 of 3.
**File:** `company_004_apple/_parts/s1_p1.md` — **Header + window, STAGE BOUNDARY JUSTIFICATION, §A, §B, §C, §D.** Sections E–P and Q–U and the consolidated claim-record appendix are other volumes; section letters, record IDs and conflict numbering continue across parts (§9.3) and are never renumbered to make this part look self-contained. Cross-references take the form `(Apple S1 §D.1, s1_p1)`.
**Local record namespace:** this part mints **S1P1-01 … S1P1-nn** as dossier-local IDs (§13); global uniqueness lives only in `sources.csv` at merge, and no `source_id` is defined here.
**Window:** see §STAGE BOUNDARY JUSTIFICATION. The window is argued, not assumed; every candidate end-date in the record is tested there.
**Stage definition (working, defended below):** from the earliest document attested origin of the venture to the first meaningful real-world test of it — an artifact, a price, or an order that existed outside the founders' own assertion.
**Hindsight firewall (§2).** Nothing below treats Apple's later scale as evidence that a 1976–77 decision was rational, that any party was obviously right or wrong, or that any outcome was foreseeable. No 1980-or-later product, institution, price or reputation is imported into 1976–77. The anti-hagiography test applied to every section: *would this paragraph still read as plausible if the firm had failed in 1981?* Where it would not, the paragraph is rewritten or deleted. **Record-selection null (§2):** stated per section, because this is a survivor's archive — what does not survive is internal deliberation, rejected options, contemporaneous failure nobody printed, and any independent count behind a company self-report.
**Confidence (§3):** **High** = 2+ independent sources or a primary document; **Medium** = one reliable source, or approximate date corroborated later; **Low** = conflicting, vague, or retrospective-only; **UNKNOWN** = no evidence recovered. **UNKNOWN is a complete answer.**
**Independence and filing-lineage (§3).** Repeated reprinting of one origin story is ONE source. Documents inside one lineage — a corporate instrument and its later recital of itself, an advertisement and the company page that reprints it, a magazine's republication of another magazine's copy — are one source however many files they occupy. Corroboration requires an independent origin.
**Time audit (§6).** No statement imports later knowledge into an earlier period; a later source explaining an earlier event is tagged `RETROSPECTIVE SOURCE`. Numerals carry their basis: nominal 1976–77 dollars unless stated; units, boards, systems, dealers, dollars distinguished.
**Quotation discipline.** Quotation marks appear only around strings read at the cited line of a local file. Paraphrase is written unquoted and labelled. Line numbers are locators, not addresses (§14.12); each pointer carries a stable label alongside it.
**Zero web requests this pass.** Every unmet evidentiary need is recorded as UNTRIED with the exact query (§14.1, §14.4).

**Boundary cost and how it is handled.** Because the window closes at 1977-01-03, every artifact that prints a maker's price, an address of record, a telephone, a payment instrument or a dealer list post-dates it: "Inc." first appears in Apple's own printed voice in June 1977 (C-05), the corporation's advertising capacity is visible only from June–September 1977 (A2-30, AP-22's superseding marker), and the price ladder is June 1977 (A2-28). Two claim records minted by the interrupted pass describe the April 1977 directory and the June/July 1977 order form as "inside the window" (S1P1-05, S1P1-06). Under the adopted boundary they are **outside** it. This part does not silently re-date them; the defect is registered as **S1P1-CF-10** and the records are corrected there rather than deleted here.

**Two dated events, never one "founding" (D's L-8 merge decision).** The 1976-04-01 instrument and the 1977-01-03 incorporation are different events of different legal character, nine months apart, and **no document in this corpus makes one a continuation of the other** (C-01). The word "founded" is therefore not used in this file without attaching one of the two dates to it. What the succession mechanics were — conversion, new charter over the old firm, or something else — is UNKNOWN: the California Secretary of State entity file, which the company's own 1994 filing names as the charter registry (C-01's AP-06 route, and the exhibit index line "88-S3 Restated Articles of Incorporation, filed with the Secretary of State of the State of California on January 27, 1988"), was tried and not answered (C-07).

**Place of formation is not Los Altos.** The universe register's cell reads "1976-04-01, Los Altos, California" and traces there to a Wikipedia raw page (A's header). Against it: the lot's own bibliographic line places the signing "[Mountain View, California]" (C-34); 1976 dated print places the firm nowhere at all; the first address of record is Cupertino, 20863 Stevens Creek Blvd Bldg B3-C, and it is printed May–June **1977** (A2-25, C-05); and "garage" attaches to Apple in this corpus **only** in the February 1981 gloss "who took Apple from a garage operation to its current enviable position" (C-11, read at `sources/ia_byte_1981/byte-1981-02.txt` per L-5). Worse for the motif's evidentiary weight, the same magazine printed the garage as a **stock figure of the field** about another firm in July 1976 — "all the company consisted of was two people in a garage" of Sphere (L-5, `sources/ia_byte_1976/byte-1976-07.txt` l.3023–3027, under the issue-date caveat below). Premises: **UNKNOWN** (D's DG-D5).

**Provenance risk carried on the boundary itself (L-6).** The 1976 BYTE issues are dated by Internet Archive metadata plus internal cross-references that **hold for October–December and fail for July**: the `-07` file previews pieces appearing in "BYTE's August and September issues" yet narrates events through "October 30". Nothing in the adopted boundary depends solely on `-07` — the start edge rests on BYTE May 1977 and the end edge on the 1994 filing and BYTE February 1981 — but every Stage-1 statement sourced to a Jan–Sep 1976 BYTE file carries `issue-date: provisional pending page-sequence verification`, and month confidence for those files is **Medium**, not High.

**Record-selection null for this section (§2).** The boundary is drawn where paper survives, not where decisions were taken. There is no 1976 filing of any kind, no charter text, no partnership clause this project has read, and no rejected alternative — no document records anyone *considering* a later date, staying a partnership, or dissolving. A boundary built from the first surviving legal instrument is therefore a boundary set by the **archive's shape**; a firm that had filed papers in 1975 would have a different Stage 1, and nothing in the record shows the chosen edge was the one the participants experienced.

**The finding, stated as the record supports it.** Stage 1's substance is not a company being built; it is a **product entering a market through other people's paper**. The single most load-bearing asymmetry in the Apple corpus is that the venture's first nine printed months contain *no maker-generated commercial document whatsoever* — no price, no offer, no address — while they contain two independent retailers' claims to be selling the thing (AP-22 with its superseding marker; A2-65, which draws exactly this conclusion). What follows without hindsight is narrow but firm: a partnership formed around a machine had, by September 1976, produced something a New York hobby shop thought worth advertising, and by December 1976 an editor who dealt in four other companies' hardware treated one of its principals as a peer. A2-65 names the mechanism question the corpus cannot answer, and this file does not answer it either: **why the maker did not advertise in 1976 is UNKNOWN** — money, capacity, or having no literature to print are the three available explanations and none is evidenced.

**What the window-end legal event does *not* buy.** The 1977-01-03 incorporation is the most certain date in this stage and, by itself, tells nothing about capital, control, officers, or purpose: no charter text is held (C-07), no incorporation-era stock record surfaced, and the month's own print is silent (C-20). The temptation this file must resist is the one the record-selection null exists to name — reading a legal milestone as an organisational one.

**Hindsight-firewall test applied to this section (§2).** Every row above is a dated document's statement. Nothing here treats the choice to incorporate, the choice to sell through dealers, or the choice of the 6502 as vindicated by anything after 1980. A reader who knew only this section would know the firm's revenue, units, prices, first customer, premises, officers and capital as UNKNOWN — which is, on this evidence, the accurate description of Apple at 1977-01-03.

**Record-selection null (§2).** What is unrecoverable about this period is unrecoverable because the surviving archive is not the company's. The record-keeping parties are an editor (BYTE), a club (its newsletter run) and dealers (their advertisements). They preserved products, prices, stock lists and their own gossip about the field. They preserved **no** internal deliberation, **no** rejected option, **no** contemporaneous failure, and **no** independent count behind any company self-report. Concretely: the founders' interior — what they were paid, what they owned, who refused them, what they argued about, what they could raise — leaves no trace in this family at all (`research/B_founder_forensics.md` §header, citing A2-61a: `Wozniak` in four cached files, `Jobs` as a personal name in three, `Markkula` once in ~13 MB, `Wayne` never). Every one of the UNKNOWNs in the table above is a property of that selection, not a finding that the corresponding fact was small.

## C. ORIGINAL PROBLEM

STATUS: WRITTEN 2026-09-25

For a hardware venture of this period the standard §7 frame — a problem statement someone wrote down and a customer someone named — does not apply unmodified. **No document in this corpus contains a problem statement, a mission, a market study, or a named customer need belonging to the partnership.** What the record supplies instead is a three-part assembly: (i) the designer's own contemporaneous account of what the first machine was *intended* to be, (ii) a countable population whose equipment the machine addressed, and (iii) a category the trade press was coining at the same moment, in retail terms. Those three are what "the original problem" is establishable as, and the section is written to that limit.

The firewall discipline here is to refuse both directions: it does not follow that "the personal-computer vision" is false, and it does not follow that the founder's 1977 description of 1975 intent is a reliable record of 1975 intent — a statement made while the company was two months old, about a design made while it did not exist, is contemporaneous **as to its making** and retrospective **as to its subject**. ### D.2 The first-customer question stays UNKNOWN, and here is what it does not stay

**UNKNOWN: the transaction.** The canonical first order — 50 Apple-1 boards at $500 each, July 1976, from Paul Terrell's Byte Shop, for resale at $666.66 — **has no document in this corpus**, and `research/D_adversarial.md` L-1 chased the lineage to its carriers and found: the designer's own 1977 sentence naming no buyer, no order, no unit count and no price; the Byte Shop's own 1976 advertising **never mentioning Apple** (across all twelve 1976 issues the token `Apple` occupies 13 lines, of which zero fall in a Byte Shop advertisement); and commercial carriers that admit resting on direct conversation. The apple-1-replica study gives "50 completed boards … 25,000 USD cash on delivery" citing a stamped manual cover plus a **2021 email reply** from Terrell. A rival price exists and is unadjudicated: a Tier-4 post attributed to Terrell himself, "sold Apple 1s to my Byte Shop owners for **$525** each and made $25 per unit". The 2026 Christie's essay restates the canon without citing a document (C-32). **One lineage, contradicting itself on date and on price.** No number from this cluster may appear as FACT in this file, in §F, §K, or the timeline (L-1's merge decision, adopted).

**Well documented: the relationship.** The separate proposition — that Terrell and the firm knew each other commercially in 1976 — is supported by three dated items, none of them memoir: Terrell and Jobs named as peers in one printed sentence after a stated September 1976 conversation on the WESCON floor; "Apple Computers" and "Byte Shop of Palo Alto" listed together on the same December 1976 exhibitor page; and Terrell's own 1976 Polaroids of the founders showing him the machine, published in 2022 through a collector registry (AP-30) — photographs whose **date attribution is the curator's**, not printed on the images as read here. The distinction is the section's load-bearing one: an early wholesale relationship is **very likely**; the units, price, date and payment terms are **undocumented** (U-C-4, U-AP-2).

**And one rung that the canon does not survive.** L-4 inverts the "first dealer" question entirely: because the New York advertiser enumerates the lines it is an **authorized dealer for** and Apple is not among them, and because the Berkeley advertiser claims no relationship either, **no 1976 document in the corpus evidences an authorized Apple dealer relationship of any kind**. That bars "first store", "first dealer" and "dealer network" as 1976 statements in *either* direction — the Byte-Shop-first story and its rivals — and is consistent with a partnership selling boards over the counter to whoever came.

### D.3 The tension that is this section's finding

Two artifacts in the local corpus describe the commercial machine far better than any in-window document does, and they are the two the brief names. **Both fall after the window closes** (see S1P1-CF-10), and their evidentiary use here is strictly as witnesses to the channel the window's product entered, not as Stage-1 state.

| Artifact | Date | What it prints | Why it is in tension |
|---|---|---|---|
| BYTE's national computer-store directory | 1977-04 | A state-by-state list of roughly **sixty named outlets across 22 jurisdictions including Canada and Japan**, of which about thirty-five are Byte Shop outlets, each with street address, telephone and a **named manager** — "Byte Shops, Inc., 1261 Birchwood Dr., Sunnyvale, CA 94086, (408) 734-9000, Bryan Kerr … Byte Shop of Tokyo, 2-9-9 Sotokanda, Chiyodaku, Tokyo" (A2-35) | The chain that memoir credits as Apple's founding customer is documented, in the month of the Apple II's announced introduction, as a **multi-state and trans-Pacific franchise with a named managing entity**. | Apple's own direct-mail order form | 1977-06, re-run 1977-07 | A nine-row price ladder (4K $1,298 system / $598 board-only up to 48K $2,638 / $1,938), California tax add-ons, aftermarket chip sets at $125 and $600, "we will include free a custom vinyl carrying case (a $50 value). **The finding, stated as a tension rather than resolved into a story.** L-10 adjudicates only the part the print can carry, and this file carries exactly that much: the founder's *sequence* is a compression — "California first, then nationwide, then retail" is contradicted in ordering by a New York advertisement in September 1976 and by the fact that the retail stage was running **before the maker advertised at all** (the maker's first advertisement is nine months later than the first dealer's). The *type* of channel Wozniak names — word of mouth, then generic retail, with **no founding order and no named buyer** — is corroborated by the strongest negative in the corpus: no 1976 document evidences any dealer agreement (L-4), Apple placed no advertisement of its own in 1976 (AP-22, A2-65), and no 1976 print states the price (A2-23). So the two best commercial artifacts do **not** refute the memoir; they **postdate its subject** and describe a different company, three months into corporationhood. What cannot be written either way is the transition itself: **no document in this corpus records how a word-of-mouth board sale became a bank-card mail-order business, and the mechanism is UNKNOWN.** The three candidate mechanisms — capital arriving with an outside investor, the Apple II making direct mailing viable, or a dealer network that formed between December 1976 and April 1977 — are all consistent with the print and none is evidenced by it.
