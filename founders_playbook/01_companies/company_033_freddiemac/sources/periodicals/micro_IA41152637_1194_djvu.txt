OPENING STATEMENT OF SENATOR RIEGLE 


Senator RiEGLE. The committee will come to order. 

The chairman will be arriving late, and so I will initiate this 
hearing until he arrives. 

Our hearing this morning is on S. 2467, which is Senator 
D’Amato’s bill, cosponsored by others of us to remove the current 
restrictions on the ownership and transfer of participating pre- 
ferred stock of FHLMC, the Federal Home Loan Mortgage Corpora- 
tion. The restrictions were imposed in 1984 when the stock was 
issued. Under the restrictions, the stock may be owned only by in- 
stitutions that are members of the Federal Home Loan Bank or by 
brokers-dealers that are making a market in the stock. In addition, 
a single shareholder generally may not own more than 1 percent of 
the outstanding shares. The bill is intended to increase the value of 
the participating preferred stock by greatly expanding the universe 
of potential en ay Any such increased value would benefit 
the thousands of FSLIC-insured thrift institutions that own the 
stock, and so we very much look forward to hearing from our 
witnesses. 

— D’Amato, do you have a comment that you would like to 
make? 


OPENING STATEMENT OF SENATOR D’AMATO 


Senator D’Amarto. Well, yes, I do, Mr. Chairman. First of all, let 
me thank the committee and particularly Senator Proxmire, for 
scheduling this hearing as expeditiously as he had indicated on the 
floor of the Senate. I would hope, Mr. Chairman, given the kind of 
support that the committee has demonstrated for this bill and un- 
derstanding that many Members of the Congress are really kind of 
in the loop—that we would be able to move expeditiously to a 
markup. But let me indicate that not only am I sponsoring the bill 


(1) 


but Senators Shelby, Karnes, Armstrong, Gramm, Hecht, Bond, 
Heinz, Cranston, Dodd, and Dixon, have already cosponsored this 
legislation. 

More than 2,900 of the Nation’s 3,100 thrifts own FHLMC pre- 
ferred stock, the subject of this hearing. Fifteen million shares are 
outstanding, trading on June 1, 1988, at about $60 a share. This 
stock represents nearly $1 billion in capital to the thrift industry. 

Now however, estimates by stock analysts are that the stock ; 
would double and possibly triple in value if ownership and transfer 
restrictions were lifted. At $180 a share, that means an additional 
$2 billion in capital for the thrift industry—convertible to cold, 
hard cash for those who need it. 

Now this legislation seems to inject at no cost $2 billion of new 
capital into the industry simply by removing two regulatory re- 
strictions on the ownership and transferability of nonvoting pre- 
ferred stock of the Federal Home Loan Mortgage Corporation, 
known as FHLMC. 

Currently, there are two restrictions. The first is, it can only be 
held by members of the Federal Home Loan Bank System, the 
other is no one investor can hold more than 1 percent, except those 
acquired through other institutions with stock or those that at the 
initial distribution were entitled to more than 1 percent. We are 
talking about where FSLIC brings about a merger of one bank with : 
another because of the problems they’re having. | : 

I think some interesting statistics will bear out the kind of 
impact this could have. Thirty-nine percent of the total outstanding 
shares of FHLMC stock are held by thrift institutions that are in 
desperate need of capital. Thrifts that have a net worth of 4 per- 
cent or less. Twenty-four percent of the total outstanding shares—I 
think this gives some relevancy to why this is an infusion of capital 
into a badly—desperate industry in certain cases—24 percent of 
the total outstanding shares are held by savings institutions that 
have a negative net worth. 

Now no one suggests that by increasing the value—and we have 
every reason to believe that the value would increase by a mini- 
mum of $1 billion—that that is going to take all of these failing 
thrifts and turn them overnight magically into successful ones. But 
it will provide a tremendous stimulation to their value. 

Let me indicate, of the 2,900 saving institutions that own 
FHLMC preferred stock, 2,881 are insured by FSLIC. Some other 
interesting statistics, the entire thrift industry benefits from this 
legislation. A doubling of the value will add—now that is a conserv- 
ative element, a doubling of the value—would add 11% percent to 
the tangible net worth of the entire system of FSLIC insured insti- 
tutions; 11% percent, not 1 penny of taxpayers’ money. A tripling 
of the value, which means that the stock would go up to $180, and 
if you take profit, PE ratios, if you take dividend ratios, that is not 
an unrealistic figure—would add 21.3 percent to the tangible net 
worth of the system. 

So Mr. Chairman, there are other reasons. But we are here to 
hear Mr. Brendsel and other experts, and again, I thank you for 
your cosponsorship and for the promptness of this hearing.

PORATION, WASHINGTON, DC 


Mr. BRENDSEL. Thank you, Mr. Chairman. 

I appreciate your invitation to appear today to discuss the remov- 
al of restrictions on FHLMC’s preferred stock. I do have a full 
rior Esa ‘which I request your permission to submit for the

Senator RIEGLE. Without objection, so ordered. 

Mr. BRENDSEL. In making the few brief comments here today, I 
want to remind the committee that as President of FHLMC, I am 
not a member of FHLMC’s Board of Directors, and thus do not vote 
on this or any other matter. In addition, I would also say that the 
Board at this time has not yet voted upon any resolution related to 
the removal of the preferred stock restrictions. 


BACKGROUND OF FHLMC 


Now by way of background, FHLMC was created by Congress in 
1970 to increase the availability and affordability of conventional 
mortgage loans for home buyers. To help eee our mission, 
in 1982 Congress authorized FHLMC to issue preferred stock. Then 
in 1984 during a time of increasing concern over the capital ade- 
quacy of the thrift industry our Board of Directors authorized a 
special distribution of preferred stock, 15 million shares to be pre- 
cise, only to the members of the Federal Home Loan Bank System. 
Their objective was not to raise capital for FHLMC but to help the 
thrift industry. The restrictions on the stock have kept the price 
much lower than it otherwise would be. 

Certainly, if there were any doubts in this matter, Senator 
D’Amato’s bill has dispelled them. Before introduction of Senator 
D’Amato’s bill, trading volume in a the stock averaged between 
10,000 and 15,000 shares a day, and the price per share was around 
$60. Since introduction of Senator D’Amato’s bill, the stock trading 
volume has averaged more than 10 times that amount, and it has 
reached an all-time high of $92 per share. 

In anticipation of this hearing, yesterday 500,000 shares of our 
stock changed hands. 

Overall, I think the market volatility in the stock will continue 
until this matter is resolved. My own view is that lifting the re- 
strictions on the stock is a win for FHLMC and for housing. It is a 
win for our thrift stockholders, and it is a win for the FSLIC. 


REMOVING RESTRICTIONS ON THE STOCK 


Due to FHLMC’s growth, its primary capital is modest compared 
to other similar institutions. Overall, FHLMC must maintain a 
sound financial position. Removing the restrictions on the stock 
would be a significant step toward helping the corporation in the 


19 


future to achieve this objective. Also, eliminating the restrictions 
on the stock would dramatically increase the value of the stock 
which would translated into additional capital for thrift sharehold- 
ers. In addition, an indirect—an increase in net worth could permit 
some thrifts to raise still more capital by converting from a mutual 
charter to a stock charter. 

There are also indirect benefits for the FSLIC. An increase in 
thrift net worth under the circumstances I have just described 
mitigates the potential drain institutions could place on the FSLIC. 

Broader stock ownership would in no way alter the corporation’s 
management or control. FHLMC’s charter is quite explicit, that 
FHLMC shall be under the Director of a Board of Directors com- 
posed of the members of the Federal Home Loan Bank Board. 
Unless Congress changes FHLMC’s charter, the three presidential- 
ly appointed members of the Federal Home Loan Bank Board will 
continue to determine corporate policy. Nonetheless, Board mem- 
bers have expressed concern that public ownership of FHLMC’s 
preferred stock would create an additional constituency. A public 
constituency concerned with the Federal Home Loan Bank Board’s 
actions, as they relate to FHLMC, could increase public pressure 
on Board decisionmaking. It might also seek fundamental changes 
such as in the composition of the FHLMC Board. 

I might also add that Board members currently are also grap- 
pling with accounting issues in the hope of avoiding thrifts having 
to sell their FHLMC stock in order to realize the gain associated 
with the market price appreciation of the stock on their books. 

But the Board and I want to express our appreciation to you, 
Senator D’Amato, for introducing this legislation. Your action has 
resulted in a substantially increased focus on this issue and has 
even caused movements in points of view. 

Now I will be happy to answer any questions you may have.

BACKGROUND 


By way of background, Freddie Mac was created by Congress in 1970 to 
increase the availability and affordability of conventional 
residential mortgage funds for homebuyers. Freddie Mac accomplishes 
this by purchasing mortgages from lenders nationwide, thereby 
replenishing their supply of funds to rclerd to homebuyers. We 
package these mortgages into pools, and then sell securities backed 
by these pools. These mortgage-backed securities are then sold to 
institutional investors, such as pension funds and insurance 
companies. In this regard, Freddie Mac is not a retail lender and 
has no direct contact with borrowers, as such. We do not make 
mortgage loans; rather, we purchase loans from lenders. Freddie Mac 
bas purchased one out of every seven conventional mortgages 
outstanding in the United States. 


Freddie Mac was the pathfinder in developing the link between the 
capital markets and loca) convedtional mortgage markets. We were 
also a pioneer with Fannie Mae in developing uniform underwriting 
guidelines, innovative mortgage programs and mortgage-backed 
securities. These developments have become standards for the 
industry. They may now seem simple, but it took many years of 
carefully assessing the needs of the marketplace and the diverse 
interests of lenders, homebuyers and investors to arrive at the 
marketplace we have today. We are proud that we have been able to 
meet our Congressional mandate.

BENEFITS OF S 2467 


S 2467 would simply remove both the 1 percent cap on the amount of shares allowed to be 
owned per thrift institution, and the restriction on trading of the shares by non-thrifts 
(i,e., the general public). MBA strongly supports removal of these restrictions for the 
following reasons. 


We believe these restrictions never were needed. They artificially suppress the liquidity 
and therefore the market value of the preferred stock. Prior to recent discussions in the 
press and otherwise regarding the lifting of these restrictions, trading in the stock was at 
times so limited that some market makers of FHLMC's preferred stock lamented that 
"trading by appointment" was how shares changed hands. Cognizant of this, many thrift 
institutions, including members of MBA, support removal of the restrictions because they 
believe the liquidity and market value of their investments would increase as a result. 


Allowing these shares to be freely tradeable, without caps or restrictions, would allow the 
trading price to reflect more fully FHLMC's earnings and business prospects. The Federal 
National Mortgage Association (FNMA), which is FHLMC's sister corporation in the 
secondary mortgage marketplace, is a publicly held corporation whose common stock is 
freely traded. Comparing FNMA's and FHLMC's 1987 operating results, FNMA's earnings 
were $376 million, and its price/earnings ratio was 6.6x. Its stock closed at $30.50 per 
share on December 31, 1987. FHLMC, on the other hand, earned $301 million in 1987. 
However, its price/earnings ratio was 2.7x. Its stock closed at $48.50 per share on 
December 31, 1987. As can be seen from these price/earnings ratios, FNMA's earnings 


are more accurately reflected in the trading price of its shares than are FHLMC's 
earnings in its shares. This is attributable to the high degree of liquidity for PFNMA's 
shares, and the relatively illiquid market for FHLMC's shares.

stock. In fact, the mere proposa! of lifting the restrictions has had an impact. Before 
Senator D'Amato introduced S 2467 on Monday, June 6, FHLMC's stock was trading on the

market makers. The restrictions, which went into effect June 15, now allow each market

125 percent of the number of shares the market maker owned or had a beneficial interest

BACKGROUND 


In December of 1984, the Federal Home Loan Bank Board created 15,000,000 
shares of nonvoting participating preferred stock of Freddie Mac that was 
distributed as a dividend to members of the Federal Home Loan Bank System. The 
stock was disbursed to the industry to bolster the capital of the industry. 


The participating preferred stock is nonvoting and subordinated to the 
common stock held by the 12 Federal Home Loan Banks. This common stock was 
acquired with the initial $100,000,000 put up by the banks in 1970 as the 
initial capitalization of Preddie Mac. 


The stock was distributed to holders of shares in the Federal Home Loan 
Banks. The shares were distributed to all banks based on their original 
capitalization of Freddie Mac, and then within those districts to member banks 
based on the amount of stock held by those banks. This includes FDIC insured 
member banks. 


There are two ownership restrictions on the stock. The first is that it can 
only be held by members of the Federal Home Loan Bank system. The other is that 
no one investor can hold more than 1% of the stock (except those that acquired 
other institutions with stock, or those that, at the initial distribution were 
entitled to more than 1%). Market makers may hold stock on an interim basis in 
their efforts to support this market. 


Due to these two significant restrictions on the stock’s ownership, the 
market price of the stock has been depressed and has been tremendously 
undervalued according to stock analysts that have followed it. These 
restrictions, which have been imposed through regulatory action, have diminished 
the positive effects to savings institutions’ balance sheets originally intended 
when the preferred stock was issued.

Dear Representative St Germain: 


During the last several weeks, there have been a number of inquiries from 
Congress and various housing industry groups regarding reports that dis- 
cussions were underway which would somehow change Freddie Mac's character. 
Such reports are incorrect. 


We are writing you to outline the nature of such discussions, which hav* been 
and are taking place under our auspices as Freddie Mac's Board of Directors, 
and to assure you that any change contemplated would not alter the fundamental 


character of Freddie Mac in any way or limit or affect the access of any cus- 
tomer to Freddie Mac's services. 


The discussions are aimed at allowing Freddie Mac's owners to recognize the 
appreciated value of their holdings by distributing to them a non-voting 
security. Freddie Mac is owned by America's savings institutions through the 
common stock held in trust for them by the twelve Federal Home Loan Banks 
across the nation. 


The contemplated change would allow individual savings institution members of 
the Federal Home Loan Bank System to recognize on their balance sheets a sub- 
stantial portion of the value of the Freddie Mac stock they beneficially own 
and to receive dividends directly from Freddie Mac. 


Both results would buttress the net worth of these savings institutions during 
a period of continued concern over the capital adequacy of the thrift indus- 
try. Because these institutions cannot now reflect on their financial state- 
ments the appreciated value of the investment they made in all of Freddie 
Mac's stock 14 years ago, the benefit they enjoy from their holdings of 
Freddie Mac stock is limited. The investment they made in Freddie Mac created 
and capitalized the Corporation when it was founded in 1970 and this is an 
opportunity to recognize that investment at a time when it is most needed. 


An additional benefit of the contemplated recognition of the beneficial 
ownership of Freddie Mac would be a streamlined process for distributing

Home Loan Banks who pass the funds on to member savings institutions.

from them. This paper transfer would not change -- in any way -- the direc-

Since preferred shareholders receive 90% of all distributions above their 
preferential $10-million distribution, dividends paid to preferred share- 


holders should expand in line with earnings advances. Further, since the 
common is callable, its value is a static $100 million plus 10% of retained 
earnings Participating preferred shareholders thus receive 90% of the

ingly, we believe it is appropriate to analyze the preferred stock as a 
common stock equivalent. The existence of the common stock can be thought 
of as an additional 10% equivalent shares.

Freddie Mac's operations are a cross between that of a mortgage banker, an 
S&L, did an insurance company. The mortgage banker portion of its opera- 
tions buys and sells loans in order to generate “spread” income; the S&L 
holds loans in portfolio and funds those loans at a positive spread; and 
the insurance company portion of its operations guarantees mortgages and 
absorbs credit risk. 


Assuming that credit standards are stringent and losses are contained, 
Freddie Mac's operations are, in part, dependent on the volume of loans 
purchased and sold in the secondary markets. The level of prepayments is 
also critical to the “base” of mortgages sold, which, in turn, determines 
the amount of revenue generated by its sold portfolio Table 6 depicts 
the dramatic growth in its outstanding pools of loans sold in contrast to 
the relatively constant “management” spread. The pools of loans sold to 
investors is a net number of loans outstanding, which grows as its cumula- 
tive amount of loans sold to investors less repayments. Its spread income 
has thus advanced in line with growth in its outstanding loan pools; accord- 
ingly, this stream of income is highly predictable to investors. 


The loan pools sold to investors expand as industry volume grows, which, in 
turn, is highly dependent on the level of interest rates and mortgage 
activity. We anticipate that loans sold to investors will expand to $200 
billion by next year. Its actual management spread could actually expand 
somewhat, reflecting overall market conditions

attractive relative to other securities. We are 
anticipating a robust 21% earnings increase next

expected $14.25. We anticipate a similar, though 
possibly slightly slower, growth in dividends next 
year to a $4.50-$4.60 indicated rate, which would 
make the stock yield an attractive 7.0%-7.5%. 
Accordingly, should the shares simply trade to 
yield 6.0%, a still-generous yield considering the 
growth characteristics of the company, we believe 
the shares could trade into the $80s on a near-term

While we cannot predict with any degree of cer- 
tainty the impact of privatization, we note that 
Freddie Mac's role as a mortgage conduit has never 
been stronger and more vital, and that recently- 
released studies of Freddie Mac's future role by a 
special task committee would indicate no funda- 
mental change in Freddie Mac's basic role as a 
secondary mortgage-market conduit. While Freddie 
Mac's earnings growth in the future will be largely 
a function of its secondary market activity and the 

wth in loans sold to investors at a spread, 
Freddie Mac's operations are unique among financial 
service companies because a slowdown in activity 
or even a cessation in its current activity should 
not erode shareholder value. For instance, because 
so much of future earnings and cash flow arises 
from assets originated and sold in the past, 
Freddie Mac will generate significant amounts of 
cash flow no matter what happens. For illustra- 
tive purposes only, our analysis suggests that 
Freddie Mac's value, assuming it never bought or 
sold another loan after the end of next year, would 
easily exceed $100 per preferred share. This 
analysis is presented Ister and is based on a 
discounted cash flow of assets already originated 
and sold, for which future cash flow arises from 
payments on mortgages sold to investors at a

CONCLUSION 


LAST JANUARY’S COMMENTS BY FREDDIE MAC PRESIDENT LELAND 
BRENDSEL CONCERNING THE LIFTING OF OWNERSHIP RESTRICTIONS 
ON THE PREFERRED STOCK, ALONG WITH CLARIFICATION OF THE 
STATUS OF THE DIVIDENDS-RECEIVED DEDUCTION, BOOSTED THE 
PRICE OF FREDDIE MAC PREFERRED FROM AROUND $45 TO $64 
BEFORE A RETREAT TO THE HIGH 50S ON PROFIT-TAKING. THE 
RECENT ANNOUNCEMENT OF A PREDICTABLE DIVIDEND POLICY 
($1.25 PER QUARTER) IS ALSO GOOD NEWS AND THE RESULTING 
YIELD MAKES FREDDIE MAC PREFERRED ATTRACTIVE ON THIS 
BASIS. FOR THE FUTURE, FURTHER APPRECIATION IS MOST 
DEPENDENT ON WHETHER THE OWNERSHIP RESTRICTIONS ARE 
LIFTED. WE BELIEVE THIS IS AN ISSUE THAT WILL PROBABLY BE 
ADDRESSED LATER THIS YEAR. GIVEN THIS ATTRACTIVE YIELD AND 
FUTURE POTENTIAL FOR LIFTING OF RESTRICTIONS, FREDDIE MAC 
PREFERRED IS A GOOD INVESTMENT FOR THOSE WITH A MEDIUM- 
TO LONG-TERM TIME HORIZON. 


EARNINGS PERFORMANCE 


Freddie Mac had record earnings of $301 million in 1987 versus $247 million in 
1986. This strong showing continued the trend of recent years reflecting Freddie 
Mac's strong growth. We believe that earnings for 1988, barring a significant rise in 
rates or problems in the housing sector, will be respectable and growth of 10% to 
20% achievable based on prior performance. 


OWNERSHIP RESTRICTIONS 


In mid-January Leland Brendsel, president of Freddie Mac, stated that the issue of 
lifting ownership restrictions on the preferred stock should be raised and that such 
an action could lead to substantial apprecistion in the stock, which would benefit 
the savings institutions that hold the securitics. While Brendsel did not give a

constituency to support the elimination or moderation of restrictions on ownership. 
To a large extent, the ball is ir the court of the savings industry to press the Home

DIVIDEND POLICY 


Freddie Mac has announced that, tentatively, the dividend for each quarter of 1988 
will be $1.25. The announcement removes a great deal of uncertainty from what

TMIC EXPOSURE 


The recent action of the California insurance commissioner, bringing into question 
the adequacy of Ticor Mortgage Insurance Corporation’s (TMIC) resources, along 
with the possibility of his liquidating the troubled mortgage insurer, has raised 
questions over possit . exposure for holders of TMiC-insured paper. Freddie Mac 
is currently evaluating «ts total exposure in this area. Management has indicated 
that it does not expect any “material” effects from this question. In the absence of 
information that quantifies any potential problem, we believe there may be short- 
term effects on the stock, depending on the extent of the exposure. Despite this, 
there continues to be fundamental value in the stock for those with a longer-term 
view. We believe that any price deterioration on TMIC news could be viewed as a 
buying opporiunity, given the continuing possibility of ownership restrictions being 
lifted and the probability of future strong operating performance.

IMPACT OF S. 2467 


Our association is a $128 million association. We have a net 
worth problem resulting from restructuring due to the interest rate 
volatility of the early 1980’s. The restructuring included the sale of 
a portfolio of low interest rate loans which produced a sizeable ioss 
carry-forward. However, our operations are strong. For instance, 
we made 461 real estate loans amounting to $23,517,000 during 
1987 which is an all-time high for us. From January 1, 1988 
through May 31, 1988, we made $6,025,000 in real estate mortgages. 
Not a record, but still a very creditable performance for us again. 
Our deposit base has been and remains stable without paying ex- 
cessive rates of interest. Yet we desperately need capital. 

Charter Federal wants to convert to a stock association. It would 
be difficult for us to do so without some internally generated cap- 
ital. Removal of ownership restrictions as proposed by Senator 
D’Amato’s bill would result in a dramatic increase in the value of 
FHLMC stock. The gain on our 150,000 shares of FHLMC partici- 
pating preferred stock would encourage Charter Federal’s manage- 
ment and customers to invest additional capital into the associa- 
tion’s stock conversion. If this scenario would be replayed across 
the country many times, the FSLIC would receive a double boost in 
its ability to perform. There would be increased industry capital 
from the appreciation in the price of FHLMC stock and from the 
infusion of new capital by local investors through mutual to stock 
conversions. 

How would this upward movement in price impact Charter Fed- 
eral? At a price of $80 per share, we have a GAAP net worth equal 
to about 2.3 percent. At about a price of $120 a share, we would 
have a GAAP net worth of about 5.5 percent. The price of FHLMC 
stocks bears directly on our net worth position and our financial 
well-being. 

Our officers have studied FHLMC stock very carefully and on a 
regular basis, since the original allocation of the nonvoting pre- 
ferred shares were issued in December 1984. We initiated a pur- 
chase program in early 1985 to increase our ownership position to 
150,000 shares. We felt that the stock was a very sound investment, 
that it provided excellent yield, capital appreciation, that it was a 
very liquid investment, and one with which we could help our asso-

Basic Idea 


Holders of Freddie Mac's participating preferred shares would be 
given an opportunity to exchange those shares ("old preferred 
shares") for shares of a new class of participating preferred 
stock ("new preferred shares") upon paying an exchange fee of $25 
per share. Each old preferred share would be exchanged for one 
new preferred share. 


Unlike old preferred shares, new preferred shares would be fully 
transferable and could be owned by anyone, in any amount. [In all 
other respects, new preferred shares would have the same rights 
and be subject to the same restrictions as old preferred shares. 
All exchange fees would be remitted to FSLIC. 


Financing Exchange Fees 


Some thrift institutions would have difficulty raising money to 
pay an exchange fee. To help such institutions exchange their 
shares, the Federal Home Loan banks would be authorized to 
finance the exchange fees of FSLIC-insured member institutions. 
(This authority would be in addition to the Home Loan banks' 
current lending authority.) Such loans would be secured by the 
borrowers’ interest in the Freddie Mac stock being exchanged. 


Rationale 


S. 2467 would create a $1 to $2 billion windfall for the current 
holders of Freddie Mac's participating preferred stock. 


The Bank Board gave that stock to the thrift industry in 1984, 
just before Christmas. (The gift was timed so as to head off any 
contrary action by Congress or OMB.) The industry did not pay a 
cent for the stock. 


The giveaway was rationalized as an indirect return on the 
Federal Home Loan banks' original $100 million investment in 
Freddie Mac. Yet Freddie Mac is federally sponsored and owes 
much of its success to that sponsorship. In any event, the Bank 
Board had no obligation to give the thrift industry anything. 


Although the 1984 giveaway was massive, it was not complete. 
Among the rights withheld were those of free transferability and 
unrestricted ownership. Instead of just giving away those 
rights, Congress should recoup part of the increased value and 
channel it directly into FSLIC. A flat $25 exchange fee would 
increase FSLIC's reserves by some $375 million.

SECTION 1. SHORT TITLE. 


This Act may be cited as the Federal Home Loan Mortgage 


Corporation Preferred Stock Act of 1988, 


SEC. 2. DEFINITIONS. 

For purposes of this Act -- 

(a) Board. -- The term "Board" means the Board of Directors 
of the Corporation. 

(b) Corporation. -- The term "Corporation" means the Federal 
Home Loan Mortgage Corporation. 

(c) Exchange Period. -- The term “exchange period" means a 
period (not exceeding 60 calendar days) prescribed by the Board 
during which holders of old preferred shares may tender those 
shares pursuant to section 3. The exchange period shall commence 
not later than 90 days after the date of enactment of this Act. 

(d) New Preferred Share. -- The term “new preferred share" 
means a share of participating preferred stock that shall have 
the same rights and be subject to the same restrictions as an old 
preferred share, except that -- 

(1) a new preferred share may be transferred to any 
person, and 


(2) any person may own any number of new preferred 


shares.

SEC. 3. EXCHANGING OLD PREFERRED SHARES FOR NEW PREFERRED
