ABSTRACT 

This report presents information on checking 
accounts, describes how to read a monthly statement from the bank, 
and provides guidelines for balancing a checkbook. Topics covered 
include: how banks record transactions; the importance of keeping 
personal records and of balancing the checkbook promptly; what 
happens when there are insufficient funds to cover a check; 
responsibilities of the individual and the bank; and the value of 
checking accounts for money management. A step-by-step explanation is 
given of the different categories included in a Bank of America 
"Timesaver" statement. Cuidelines for balancing a checkbook register 
using the monthly statement are offered along with a checklist of 
errors to look for when the checkbook does not balance. Suggestions 
for balancing a checkbook that has not been balanced for several 
months are also included. Information in this report is current at 
the time of printing, May 1982, but is subject to change. (DC)

Your checking account is in balance when your checkbook and your 
bank statement reflect the same additions and subtractions. At first 
glarice, they probably won't. This report explains now to adjust the two 


records to make them agree. 


THE BALANCING PROCEDURE The 
checkbook balancing procedure described 
inside heips you keep an accurate record of 
the money in your checking account. Each 
month you receive a statement from the 
bank to compare with your own check regis- 
ter or check stubs. Even though you may 
have made deposits or written checks since 
the date your statement was prepared— 
and show a different balance in your 
checkbook than the one that appears on 
your statement—this procedure can help 
you reconcile the differences 


Once you've balanced your checkbook, you 
can keep it up to date as you make deposits 
or withdraw money from your account. Your 
accurate balance becomes a reference 
point that helps you quickly figure out how 
much money you have in your account from 
day to day 


THE BANK'S RECORD Your bank 
statement is the bank's record of your 
checking account. It lists all activity in your 
account including deposits, checks paid, 
and service charges. It also includes a 
record of your automated teller machine 
(ATM) transactions and any automatic deb- 
its (subtractions) and credits (additions) 
that you've authorized the bank to mare on 
a regular basis 


When you make a deposit, the bank posts 
(adds) it to your account at the end of the 
business day. When the checks you write 
reach the bank, they are paid (subtracted 
from your account), canceled, and filed 
(Checks may not reach the bank in the 
order you wrote them.) The bank also keeps 
a record of any other activity, such as 
service charges. At adout the same time 
each month, the bank prepares a siatement 
and mails it to you. In most cases, the bank 
will also return your canceled checks 
Otherwise, your checks are stored on film 
by the bank and photocopies are available 
at your request 


YOUR RECORD it's important that you 
also keep an accurate record of the activity 
in your checking account. Since you may 
not have your canceled checks, your regis- 
ter may be the only record you have to 
compare with your statement

Your record keeping need not be compli- 
cated, but it should be consistent: (1) record 
each check as you write it and each deposit 
as yOu make it (record any ATM transac- 
tions as you would a check or deposit); (2) 
promptly subtract the amount of each check 
or other deduction (such as an automatic 
money transfer or monthly service charge) 
from your balance and add each deposit. 
Be sure to add and subtract exact amounts. 
Don't round off. 


REVIEW AND CORRECT By balancing 
your checkbook as soon as you receive 
your statement, you have the chance to re- 
view your register to verify amounts of 
checks, deposits, ATM transactions (be 
sure to keep any duplicate deposit slips and 
your ATM transaction records), and other 
subtractions or additions. You'll also be able 
to correct any errors in your checkbook 
promptly or ask the bank to correct any 
mistakes it may have made. 


DON’T GET CAUGHT SHORT If you 
don't balance your checkbook regularly, 
you can't be sure how much money you 
have in your account. As a result, you may 
accidentally write a check that you don't 


have enough money to cover. In that case, 
the bank may “reject” the check and return 
it, unpaid, to the party you issued it to. Or, it 
may create an overdraft by paying the 
check. The decision on which way to handle 
this situation depends on the amount of the 
check and your vast checking activity. 
Either way, you'll probably pay a charge for 
the check. Merchants and businesses that 
allow you check-cashing privileges may 
also charge you for unpaid checks the bank 
returns to them. 


With computers, banks process checks 
faster than ever. A check you cash today 
could easily be subtracted from your ac- 
count tonight. You may risk an overdraft ora 
returned check if you plan on “float time” to 
cover your checks with a depcsit. (Float is 
the time it takes the check to travel to the 
institution on which it's drawn and where it 
will be paid.) 


RESPONSIBILITY Your checking ac- 
count is a contractual relationship with your 
bank. Both you and the bank have certain 
responsibilities. 


You are responsible, of course, for not 
writing checks for more money than you 
have in your account. For your own protec- 
tion, you should also promptly examine your 
statement and items enclosed with the 
statement; report errors, suspected for- 
geries, or alterations on any check or item; 
and report lost or stolen checks. 


The bank, in turn, is responsible for main- 
taining sound operating standards, re- 
sponding promptly to inquiries or problems 
whenever they occur, and complying with 
state and federal laws that govern banking 
procedures. 


SOUND MONEY MANAGEMENT A 
properly maintained checking account can 
become a good credit reference: it can indi- 
cate the way you handle your finances. 


Your checking account also can help you 
keep a record of income and expenses. 
When you balance your checkbook, you 
can take the opportunity to review your 
financial situation—to see where your 
money is going and whether you need to 
adjust your spending.

Tracking Down Errors 


If you've gone through the balancing proce- 
dure described inside and your checxbook 
still won't balance, this checklist may help 
you locate the error. 


|) Transactions the Bank Makes Make 
sure that you've added to cr subtracted 
from your record any debits or credits the 
bank makes for you that appear on your 
statement. These may include service 
charges or other automatic money 
transfers. 


(|) ATM Transactions it's important to 
record all deposits and withdrawals made 
through an automated teller machine at the 
time you make them. Keep your transaction 
records for reference. 


() Arithmetic Check addition and sub- 
traction in your checkbook and in the 
balancing procedure. (If the difference be- 
tween your balance and the bank's can be 
evenly divided by 2, the error could have

est statement.
